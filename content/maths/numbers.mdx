## Integers

Integers are the numbers we think of without decimals. The $$1,2,3,4$$ numbers. $$0$$ and $$-1$$ are
also integers. There are different kinds of integers, though you might think of them as one type:

- **Positive integers**: these are the counting numbers $$1, 2, 3, 4, \dots$$
- **Natural numbers**: the positive integers together with $$0$$. $$0$$ is neither negative nor
  positive.
- **Negative integers**: the opposite of positive integers, these are written with a minus sign
  $$-$$, such as $$-1, -2, -3, \dots$$.

When we say **Integers** we generally refer both positive and negative integers, including $$0$$.

### The number line

A good way to picture the integers is as evenly spaced marks along a straight line that goes on
forever in both directions. We pick one mark to be $$0$$, called the **origin**, and a fixed step
size. Every step to the right is one more, and every step to the left is one less. Positive integers
sit to the right of $$0$$ and negative integers to the left.

<NumberLine from={-6} to={6} points={[0]} />

The further right a number sits, the bigger it is, so $$-1$$ is bigger than $$-4$$ even though $$4$$
is bigger than $$1$$.

Integers show up whenever something can go above or below a starting point: floors above and below
the ground floor of a building, a bank balance that can go into overdraft, or height above and below
sea level.

### Addition as movement

Adding can be read as a walk along the number line.

- Adding a **positive** number moves you **right** by that many steps. Starting at $$-2$$ and adding
  $$6$$ lands on $$4$$:

<NumberLine from={-4} to={6} points={[-2, 4]} jumps={[{ from: -2, to: 4 }]} />

- Adding a **negative** number moves you **left** by that many steps. Starting at $$4$$ and adding
  $$-7$$ lands on $$-3$$:

<NumberLine from={-5} to={6} points={[4, -3]} jumps={[{ from: 4, to: -3 }]} />

- **Subtracting** a number does the same as adding its opposite, so $$4 - 7$$ and $$4 + (-7)$$ are
  the same walk, and both equal $$-3$$.

### Zero and opposites

Adding $$0$$ means taking no steps at all, so the number stays where it is. For any integer $$a$$:

```math
a + 0 = 0 + a = a
```

Every integer $$a$$ has an **opposite**, written $$-a$$. It sits the same distance from $$0$$, but
on the other side. Adding a number to its opposite always walks you back to the origin:

```math
a + (-a) = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad -a + a = 0
```

<NumberLine from={-5} to={5} points={[-4, 4]} jumps={[{ from: 4, to: 0, label: "+(−4)" }]} />

Because adding it "undoes" $$a$$, the opposite $$-a$$ is also called the **additive inverse** of
$$a$$.

Taking the opposite twice flips you to the other side and back again, so you end up where you
started:

```math
-(-a) = a, \qquad \text{for example} \quad -(-7) = 7
```

### The minus sign does not mean "negative"

It is tempting to read $$-a$$ as "a negative number", but that is only true when $$a$$ itself is
positive. The minus sign means "the opposite of". If $$a = -2$$, then $$-a = -(-2) = 2$$, which is
positive. Reading $$-a$$ out loud as "minus $$a$$" or "the opposite of $$a$$" avoids the confusion.

## Rules for addition

Addition of integers follows a handful of rules. The first two, along with one basic fact about
positive numbers further down, are taken as given; everything else in this section can be worked out
from them together with the facts about $$0$$ and opposites above.

### Order does not matter

Swapping the two numbers in a sum gives the same answer. This is called **commutativity**: for any
integers $$a$$ and $$b$$,

```math
a + b = b + a
```

For example, $$6 + (-9) = -3$$ and $$(-9) + 6 = -3$$. On the number line the two walks take
different routes but finish in the same place:

### Grouping does not matter

When adding three numbers, it does not matter which pair you add first. This is called
**associativity**: for any integers $$a$$, $$b$$ and $$c$$,

```math
(a + b) + c = a + (b + c)
```

For example, with $$4$$, $$-6$$ and $$10$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
(4 + (-6)) + 10 &= -2 + 10 = 8 \\
4 + ((-6) + 10) &= 4 + 4 = 8
\end{aligned}
```

Since the grouping never changes the result, the brackets are usually left out and we simply write
$$4 + (-6) + 10$$. Using both rules over and over, you can add any list of integers **in any order
and in any grouping**. This is handy for spotting easy pairs:

```math
17 + (-8) + 3 + 8 = (17 + 3) + ((-8) + 8) = 20 + 0 = 20
```

### Writing subtraction

Rather than writing $$a + (-b)$$ every time, we write it more briefly as $$a - b$$. They mean
exactly the same thing, so subtraction is just adding an opposite. That means the two rules above
apply to subtraction too, as long as each minus sign stays attached to the number after it:

```math
a - b + c = a + c - b
```

For example, $$9 - 12 + 5 = 9 + 5 - 12 = 14 - 12 = 2$$.

### An opposite is the only way back to zero

If two integers add up to $$0$$, each one must be the opposite of the other:

```math
\text{if } a + b = 0, \text{ then } b = -a \text{ and } a = -b
```

To see why, add $$-a$$ to both sides of $$a + b = 0$$. The left side becomes
$$-a + a + b = 0 + b = b$$, and the right side becomes $$-a + 0 = -a$$, so $$b = -a$$. In other
words, $$a$$ has only one opposite: nothing else added to $$a$$ gets you back to $$0$$.

This also explains $$-(-a) = a$$ from earlier: since $$-a + a = 0$$, the rule says $$a$$ is the
opposite of $$-a$$. Stacking up more minus signs just keeps flipping between $$a$$ and $$-a$$. An
even number of minus signs gives back $$a$$, and an odd number gives $$-a$$:

```math
-(-(-(-6))) = 6, \qquad -(-(-6)) = -6
```

### The opposite of a sum

To take the opposite of a sum, take the opposite of each part:

```math
-(a + b) = -a - b
```

This works because $$(a + b) + (-a - b)$$ can be regrouped as $$(a - a) + (b - b) = 0 + 0 = 0$$, and
by the rule above, anything that adds to $$a + b$$ to give $$0$$ must be its opposite.

Take care when the parts are already negative, because the minus signs combine:

```math
\begin{aligned}
-(2 + 9) &= -2 - 9 = -11 \\
-(-6 + 1) &= -(-6) - 1 = 6 - 1 = 5 \\
-(8 - 13) &= -8 - (-13) = -8 + 13 = 5
\end{aligned}
```

### Signs of sums

- Adding two **positive** integers always gives a **positive** integer. This is taken as a basic
  fact, for example $$28 + 14 = 42$$.
- Adding two **negative** integers always gives a **negative** integer.

The second fact follows from the first. A negative integer is the opposite of a positive one, so we
can write $$a = -m$$ and $$b = -n$$ with $$m$$ and $$n$$ positive. Then
$$a + b = -m - n = -(m + n)$$, and since $$m + n$$ is positive, $$a + b$$ is negative. For example,
$$-5 + (-11) = -(5 + 11) = -16$$.

When the signs are mixed, the result can go either way: $$-5 + 11 = 6$$ but $$5 + (-11) = -6$$.

### Rearranging an equation

These rules let us move numbers from one side of an equation to the other. If

```math
a + b = c
```

then adding $$-b$$ to both sides gives $$a + b - b = c - b$$, which simplifies to $$a = c - b$$. In
the same way, $$b = c - a$$. In short, a number added on one side can be **subtracted from the other
side**.

This is how simple equations with an unknown are solved:

- $$x + 8 = 3$$: subtract $$8$$ from both sides, so $$x = 3 - 8 = -5$$.
- $$6 - y = 10$$: add $$y$$ to both sides to get $$6 = 10 + y$$, then subtract $$10$$ from both
  sides, so $$y = -4$$.
- $$-3 - z = -1$$: add $$z$$ to both sides to get $$-3 = -1 + z$$, then add $$1$$ to both sides, so
  $$z = -2$$.

Checking is easy: put the answer back in. For the last one, $$-3 - (-2) = -3 + 2 = -1$$.

## Rules for multiplication

Multiplying two integers always gives another integer. A product can be written with a dot,
$$4 \cdot 9 = 36$$, or, when letters are involved, just by writing them side by side: $$ab$$ means
$$a \cdot b$$. Like addition, multiplication follows a few basic rules, and these work the same way
whether the numbers are positive, negative or zero.

### Order and grouping

Just as with addition, the order of the factors and the way they are grouped make no difference:

```math
ab = ba \qquad \text{and} \qquad (ab)c = a(bc)
```

For example, both groupings of $$2 \cdot 5 \cdot (-3)$$ give the same answer:

```math
\begin{aligned}
(2 \cdot 5) \cdot (-3) &= 10 \cdot (-3) = -30 \\
2 \cdot (5 \cdot (-3)) &= 2 \cdot (-15) = -30
\end{aligned}
```

This means a long product can be rearranged freely. A useful habit is to gather all the plain
numbers at the front and all the letters at the back:

```math
(4x)(-3y) = 4 \cdot (-3) \cdot xy = -12xy
```

```math
(2a)(5b)(7c) = 2 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 \cdot abc = 70abc
```

### Multiplying by one and by zero

Multiplying by $$1$$ leaves a number unchanged, and multiplying by $$0$$ always gives $$0$$:

```math
1 \cdot a = a \qquad \text{and} \qquad 0 \cdot a = 0
```

### Distributivity

The rule that links multiplication and addition is **distributivity**. Multiplying a sum is the same
as multiplying each part and then adding:

```math
a(b + c) = ab + ac \qquad \text{and} \qquad (b + c)a = ba + ca
```

A good way to picture this is as the area of a rectangle. A rectangle of height $$a$$ and width
$$b + c$$ can be cut into two smaller rectangles, of areas $$ab$$ and $$ac$$:

<AreaModel rows={["a"]} cols={["b", "c"]} cells={[["ab", "ac"]]} colSizes={[3, 2]} />

This is what makes mental arithmetic like $$6 \cdot 13$$ easy: split $$13$$ into $$10 + 3$$, so
$$6 \cdot 13 = 6 \cdot 10 + 6 \cdot 3 = 60 + 18 = 78$$. It is also how brackets are expanded:

```math
\begin{aligned}
5(2x - 3y) &= 10x - 15y \\
-2(4a - b) &= -8a + 2b
\end{aligned}
```

Only one of the two forms needs to be assumed. Because the order of factors does not matter, the
second follows from the first: $$(b + c)a = a(b + c) = ab + ac = ba + ca$$.

Similarly, the rule $$0 \cdot a = 0$$ does not need to be assumed either; it can be worked out.
Since $$0 + 1 = 1$$, distributivity gives

```math
0 \cdot a + a = 0 \cdot a + 1 \cdot a = (0 + 1)a = 1 \cdot a = a
```

So $$0 \cdot a + a = a$$. Subtracting $$a$$ from both sides leaves $$0 \cdot a = 0$$.

### Minus signs in products

Multiplying by $$-1$$ gives the opposite:

```math
(-1)a = -a
```

This is because $$(-1)a + a = (-1)a + 1 \cdot a = (-1 + 1)a = 0 \cdot a = 0$$, and the only number
that adds to $$a$$ to give $$0$$ is $$-a$$.

In the same way, a minus sign can be moved onto either factor of a product:

```math
-(ab) = (-a)b = a(-b)
```

To check the middle one, $$ab + (-a)b = (a + (-a))b = 0 \cdot b = 0$$, so $$(-a)b$$ is the opposite
of $$ab$$. For example, $$-(4x) = (-4)x = 4(-x)$$.

Moving the minus signs out one at a time shows what happens when **both** factors have one:

```math
(-a)(-b) = -(a(-b)) = -(-(ab)) = ab
```

So the two minus signs cancel. In particular $$(-1)(-1) = 1$$, and $$(-6)(-7) = 42$$. This gives the
familiar sign rules:

| Signs of the factors | Sign of the product | Example                |
| -------------------- | ------------------- | ---------------------- |
| positive × positive  | positive            | $$3 \cdot 8 = 24$$     |
| negative × negative  | positive            | $$(-3)(-8) = 24$$      |
| positive × negative  | negative            | $$3 \cdot (-8) = -24$$ |
| negative × positive  | negative            | $$(-3) \cdot 8 = -24$$ |

With more factors, count the minus signs: an even number of them gives a positive product, and an
odd number gives a negative one. For example, $$(-2) \cdot 3 \cdot (-5) \cdot (-1)$$ has three minus
signs, so it is $$-(2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 1) = -30$$.

Minus signs are one of the most common sources of mistakes, so it pays to go slowly:

```math
-3(5a - 7b) = -15a + 21b
```

Here $$-3$$ times $$-7b$$ is $$+21b$$, not $$-21b$$.

### Powers

Multiplying a number by itself several times is written with a small raised number called the
**exponent**:

```math
a^2 = aa, \qquad a^3 = aaa, \qquad a^n = \underbrace{a \cdot a \cdots a}_{n \text{ times}}
```

$$a^n$$ is read "$$a$$ to the power $$n$$" or "the $$n$$-th power of $$a$$", and $$a$$ is called the
**base**. For example, $$2^5 = 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 = 32$$.

Three rules follow straight from counting how many times $$a$$ is multiplied (here $$m$$ and $$n$$
are positive integers):

- **Multiplying powers of the same base adds the exponents**, because $$m$$ copies of $$a$$ followed
  by $$n$$ more copies is $$m + n$$ copies:

  ```math
  a^m a^n = a^{m+n} \qquad \text{for example} \quad x^2 x^4 = (xx)(xxxx) = x^6
  ```

- **A power of a power multiplies the exponents**, because $$n$$ groups of $$m$$ copies is $$mn$$
  copies:

  ```math
  (a^m)^n = a^{mn} \qquad \text{for example} \quad (x^2)^3 = x^2 x^2 x^2 = x^6
  ```

- **A power of a product is the product of the powers**, because the factors can be rearranged:

  ```math
  (ab)^n = a^n b^n \qquad \text{for example} \quad (3y)^2 = 3y \cdot 3y = 9y^2
  ```

These combine, for example $$(3x^2)^3 = 3^3 (x^2)^3 = 27x^6$$.

Because of the sign rules, a negative base gives a positive result for an even power and a negative
result for an odd power: $$(-2)^4 = 16$$ but $$(-2)^3 = -8$$.

Powers appear naturally in repeated growth. Suppose a culture of $$500$$ bacteria triples every
hour. After one hour there are $$3 \cdot 500$$, after two hours $$3^2 \cdot 500$$, and after four
hours

```math
3^4 \cdot 500 = 81 \cdot 500 = 40{,}500 \text{ bacteria}
```

### Three formulas worth memorising

These three expansions come up constantly:

```math
\begin{aligned}
(a + b)^2 &= a^2 + 2ab + b^2 &&\qquad \text{square of a sum} \\
(a - b)^2 &= a^2 - 2ab + b^2 &&\qquad \text{square of a difference} \\
(a + b)(a - b) &= a^2 - b^2 &&\qquad \text{difference of squares}
\end{aligned}
```

Each one comes from distributivity, multiplying every part of the first bracket by every part of the
second:

```math
\begin{aligned}
(a + b)^2 &= (a + b)(a + b) = a(a + b) + b(a + b) = a^2 + ab + ba + b^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2 \\
(a - b)^2 &= (a - b)(a - b) = a(a - b) - b(a - b) = a^2 - ab - ba + b^2 = a^2 - 2ab + b^2 \\
(a + b)(a - b) &= a(a - b) + b(a - b) = a^2 - ab + ba - b^2 = a^2 - b^2
\end{aligned}
```

The first one can be seen as a square of side $$a + b$$ cut into four pieces: one $$a^2$$, one
$$b^2$$, and **two** rectangles of area $$ab$$. That is where the $$2ab$$ comes from, and why
$$(a + b)^2$$ is not just $$a^2 + b^2$$.

Some examples:

```math
\begin{aligned}
(x + 4)^2 &= x^2 + 2 \cdot 4x + 4^2 = x^2 + 8x + 16 \\
(5 - 2y)^2 &= 5^2 - 2 \cdot 5 \cdot 2y + (2y)^2 = 25 - 20y + 4y^2 \\
(3a + 1)(3a - 1) &= (3a)^2 - 1^2 = 9a^2 - 1
\end{aligned}
```

They are also handy for mental arithmetic: $$21^2 = (20 + 1)^2 = 400 + 40 + 1 = 441$$, and
$$19 \cdot 21 = (20 - 1)(20 + 1) = 400 - 1 = 399$$.

### Expanding longer products

A product of three or more brackets is expanded two at a time. For example, to expand
$$(x + 3)(x - 1)(2x + 1)$$, first multiply out the first two brackets:

```math
(x + 3)(x - 1) = x^2 - x + 3x - 3 = x^2 + 2x - 3
```

Then multiply that result by the last bracket, and collect terms with the same power of $$x$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
(x^2 + 2x - 3)(2x + 1) &= (x^2 + 2x - 3) \cdot 2x + (x^2 + 2x - 3) \cdot 1 \\
&= 2x^3 + 4x^2 - 6x + x^2 + 2x - 3 \\
&= 2x^3 + 5x^2 - 4x - 3
\end{aligned}
```

A quick way to check an expansion is to try a value. With $$x = 2$$, the original is
$$5 \cdot 1 \cdot 5 = 25$$, and the expansion gives $$16 + 20 - 8 - 3 = 25$$.
