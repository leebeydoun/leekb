Imagine a friend waving a sparkler in the dark while you take a photo with the shutter held open.
The photo shows a glowing figure-eight, but nothing in it says where the sparkler started, which way
it went or how long it took. A GPS logger taped to the sparkler would record more: a place for each
second. The middle of the eight would then carry two times, because the sparkler passed through it
twice.

<SparklerTrail caption="The photo keeps only the shape. The log keeps a place for each second, and the middle of the eight gets two times, 0 s and 4 s." />

The log is a mapping (:ref{#mapping}) from times to points of the plane, and the photo is its image
(:ref{#image-of-set}). A mapping like this is called a **parametrised curve**. In this guide we'll
trace lines, circles and other curves this way, turn a moving point back into an equation, and
follow a mapping that bends straight lines into circles.

## A point that moves

Picture a point moving across the plane. At each moment $$t$$ it has a place, with two coordinates,
and as $$t$$ runs on, the point leaves a trail. The trail is a set of points, while the mapping also
says when the point was at each one.

:::definition[Parametrised curve]{#parametrised-curve}

Let $$J$$ be an interval of numbers (:ref{#real-numbers/interval}), possibly all of
$$(-\infty, \infty)$$. A **parametrised curve** is a mapping $$C: J \to \mathbb{R}^2$$, written

```math
C(t) = \big(x(t),\ y(t)\big)
```

The number $$t$$ is the **parameter**, often thought of as time, and $$x(t)$$ and $$y(t)$$ are the
coordinates of the point at time $$t$$. The image $$C(J)$$ is the **curve traced** by $$C$$.

:::

You've met mappings like this already. The line through a point $$P$$ with direction $$A$$ is the
set of points $$P + tA$$ (:ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/line-formula}), so $$t \mapsto P + tA$$ is a
mapping from all numbers into the plane that traces that line. With $$P = (-1, 3)$$ and
$$A = (2, -1)$$ it is $$C(t) = (-1 + 2t,\ 3 - t)$$:

| $$t$$    | $$-1$$      | $$0$$       | $$1$$      | $$2$$      | $$3$$      |
| -------- | ----------- | ----------- | ---------- | ---------- | ---------- |
| $$C(t)$$ | $$(-3, 4)$$ | $$(-1, 3)$$ | $$(1, 2)$$ | $$(3, 1)$$ | $$(5, 0)$$ |

```plane
point −1 -3 4 ne
point 0 -1 3 ne
point 1 1 2 ne
point 2 3 1 ne
point 3 5 0 ne
line −1 3 color=blue extend=0.25
caption "The points C(t) = (−1 + 2t, 3 − t), each labelled with its time t. Every step of 1 in time moves the point 2 across and 1 down."
```

Each step of $$1$$ in time adds the direction $$A = (2, -1)$$, so the dots are equally spaced along
the line. The point moves at a steady speed, in one direction, forever.

### Circles and ellipses

The unit circle is traced by $$t \mapsto (\cos t, \sin t)$$ for $$0 \le t < 2\pi$$, since every
point of the circle is $$(\cos t, \sin t)$$ for exactly one such $$t$$
(:ref{#trigonometry/unit-circle-point}). At $$t = 0$$, $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$, $$\pi$$ and
$$\frac{3\pi}{2}$$ the point is at $$(1, 0)$$, $$(0, 1)$$, $$(-1, 0)$$ and $$(0, -1)$$. So it goes
round anticlockwise, a quarter of the way in each $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$ of time.

Scaling by $$3$$ and sliding by $$(2, 1)$$ gives the mapping

```math
C(t) = (2 + 3\cos t,\ 1 + 3\sin t) \qquad 0 \le t < 2\pi
```

Its points satisfy $$(x - 2)^2 + (y - 1)^2 = 9\cos^2 t + 9\sin^2 t = 9$$, by
:ref{#trigonometry/pythagorean-identity}. So they lie on the circle with centre $$(2, 1)$$ and
radius $$3$$ (:ref{#coordinates/circle-equation}). Every point of that circle is reached too: if
$$(x - 2)^2 + (y - 1)^2 = 9$$, then $$\big(\frac{x - 2}{3}, \frac{y - 1}{3}\big)$$ is on the unit
circle, so it is $$(\cos t, \sin t)$$ for some $$t$$, and then $$(x, y) = C(t)$$.

Stretching across and up by different amounts gives an ellipse. The mapping
$$t \mapsto (5\cos t, 2\sin t)$$, for $$t$$ from $$0$$ to $$2\pi$$, traces the graph of
$$\frac{x^2}{25} + \frac{y^2}{4} = 1$$, by
:ref{#parabolas-ellipses-and-hyperbolas/ellipses-parametrisation}. At $$t = \frac{\pi}{4}$$ the
point is $$\big(\frac{5\sqrt2}{2}, \sqrt2\big) \approx (3.54, 1.41)$$, and as a check,
$$\frac{12.5}{25} + \frac{2}{4} = 1$$.

### The curve and the mapping are different things

A curve can be traced by many different mappings, so it's worth keeping the two apart. The mapping
$$t \mapsto (\cos 2t, \sin 2t)$$, for $$t$$ from $$0$$ to $$2\pi$$, also traces the unit circle, but
it goes round twice, twice as fast. The mapping $$t \mapsto (\cos t, -\sin t)$$ goes round once,
clockwise.

All three mappings have the same image, the unit circle. They are still three different mappings
(:ref{#mappings-equal}), because they give different points at the same time: at
$$t = \frac{\pi}{2}$$ the first gives $$(0, 1)$$, the second $$(-1, 0)$$ and the third $$(0, -1)$$.
Like the sparkler photo, the image forgets the times.

### A curve with a sharp point

The path of a moving point need not be smooth. Take $$C(t) = (t^2, t^3)$$ for every number $$t$$:

| $$t$$    | $$-1.5$$           | $$-1$$      | $$-0.5$$           | $$0$$      | $$0.5$$           | $$1$$      | $$1.5$$           |
| -------- | ------------------ | ----------- | ------------------ | ---------- | ----------------- | ---------- | ----------------- |
| $$C(t)$$ | $$(2.25, -3.375)$$ | $$(1, -1)$$ | $$(0.25, -0.125)$$ | $$(0, 0)$$ | $$(0.25, 0.125)$$ | $$(1, 1)$$ | $$(2.25, 3.375)$$ |

Since $$x = t^2$$ is never negative, the curve stays on the right of the $$y$$-axis. Putting $$-t$$
for $$t$$ keeps $$x$$ and changes the sign of $$y$$, so the curve for negative times is the mirror
image, in the $$x$$-axis, of the curve for positive times. Every point satisfies $$y^2 = x^3$$,
since $$(t^3)^2 = t^6 = (t^2)^3$$.

<CuspCurve caption="The curve traced by t ↦ (t², t³). The point comes in below the x-axis, stops at O when t = 0, and goes back out above it, so the curve has a sharp point at O." />

The table shows the point slowing down near $$t = 0$$. From $$t = -1$$ to $$t = -0.5$$ it moves from
$$(1, -1)$$ to $$(0.25, -0.125)$$, about $$1.15$$ units, but in the next half second it moves from
there to $$O$$, about $$0.28$$ units. At $$t = 0$$ it comes to a stop at $$O$$ and heads back out to
the right, now above the $$x$$-axis. That leaves a sharp point in the trail, called a **cusp**.

Try the mappings of this section in the tracer below. The small dots on the trail mark equal steps
of time, so they are far apart where the point moves fast and crowded where it moves slowly. The
last choice, $$t \mapsto (\sin 2t, \sin 3t)$$, traces a looping figure called a Lissajous curve, and
it is there for fun.

## Removing the parameter

Throwing away the times in the log and keeping only the places is called **removing the parameter**.
When the first coordinate can be solved for $$t$$, there is a recipe: solve $$x$$ for $$t$$ and put
the result into $$y$$. What's left is an equation in $$x$$ and $$y$$ alone, with no $$t$$ in it.

Take $$C(t) = (2t + 1,\ t^2 - 2t)$$. From $$x = 2t + 1$$ we get $$t = \frac{x - 1}{2}$$, and so

```math
\begin{aligned}
y &= \left(\frac{x - 1}{2}\right)^2 - 2 \cdot \frac{x - 1}{2} && \text{put } t = \tfrac{x - 1}{2} \text{ into } y = t^2 - 2t \\
&= \frac{x^2 - 2x + 1}{4} - (x - 1) && \text{square, and cancel the 2} \\
&= \frac{x^2 - 6x + 5}{4} && \text{over a common denominator}
\end{aligned}
```

Check with $$t = 3$$: $$C(3) = (7, 3)$$, and $$\frac{49 - 42 + 5}{4} = 3$$. So the curve is the
graph (:ref{#functions/function-graph}) of $$y = \frac{1}{4}x^2 - \frac{3}{2}x + \frac{5}{4}$$, a
parabola with vertex $$(3, -1)$$ by :ref{#parabolas-ellipses-and-hyperbolas/quadratic-parabola}. The
vertex is $$C(1)$$, the point at time $$1$$.

:::proposition[Removing the parameter]{#curves-eliminate}

Let $$a$$, $$b$$, $$c$$, $$d$$ and $$e$$ be numbers with $$a \ne 0$$, and let

```math
C(t) = (at + b,\ ct^2 + dt + e)
```

for every number $$t$$. Then the image of $$C$$ is the graph of the function on all numbers

```math
q(x) = c\left(\frac{x - b}{a}\right)^2 + d \cdot \frac{x - b}{a} + e
```

When $$c \ne 0$$, this graph is a parabola.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parametric_equation#Implicitization"}

Write $$s(x) = \frac{x - b}{a}$$, which makes sense because $$a \ne 0$$. Two sets are equal when
each lies inside the other (:ref{#basic-proofs/set-equality}), so there are two things to show.

**The image lies on the graph.** Take a point $$(x, y) = C(t)$$ of the image. Then $$x = at + b$$,
so $$s(x) = \frac{at + b - b}{a} = t$$. Putting $$s(x)$$ in place of $$t$$,
$$y = ct^2 + dt + e = q(x)$$. So $$(x, y)$$ is on the graph of $$q$$.

**The graph lies in the image.** Take a point $$(x, q(x))$$ of the graph, and let $$t = s(x)$$. Then
$$at + b = (x - b) + b = x$$, and $$ct^2 + dt + e = q(x)$$ by the formula for $$q$$. So
$$(x, q(x)) = C(t)$$, a point of the image.

**The parabola.** Multiplying out,

```math
q(x) = \frac{c}{a^2}\,x^2 + \left(\frac{d}{a} - \frac{2bc}{a^2}\right)x + \left(\frac{cb^2}{a^2} - \frac{db}{a} + e\right)
```

When $$c \ne 0$$, the number $$\frac{c}{a^2}$$ in front of $$x^2$$ is not $$0$$, so the graph is a
parabola by :ref{#parabolas-ellipses-and-hyperbolas/quadratic-parabola}.

:::

The recipe needs the first coordinate to give back $$t$$. For the unit circle it doesn't:
$$x = \cos t$$ is the same at $$t$$ and $$-t$$, so $$x$$ alone can't say which time it was. There
the identity $$\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t = 1$$ does the job instead, and removes $$t$$ to leave
$$x^2 + y^2 = 1$$.

## A ball thrown from a cliff

Stand at the top of a cliff $$20$$ m high and throw a ball out level at $$6$$ m/s. Across, nothing
pushes the ball, so it keeps moving $$6$$ m every second. Down, gravity pulls it, and in the first
$$t$$ seconds it falls $$4.9t^2$$ metres, half of $$9.8$$ (the pull of gravity in m/s²) times
$$t^2$$. We ignore the air, which would slow the ball a little.

Put the origin at the foot of the cliff, with $$x$$ across and $$y$$ up. Then $$t$$ seconds after
the throw the ball is at

```math
B(t) = (6t,\ 20 - 4.9t^2)
```

from $$t = 0$$ until it lands. The section
[The path of a thrown ball](../parabolas-ellipses-and-hyperbolas/parabolas.mdx#the-path-of-a-thrown-ball)
rounded $$9.8$$ to $$10$$; here we keep $$9.8$$.

- **When it lands.** The ball lands when its height is $$0$$, so $$4.9t^2 = 20$$, that is,
  $$t^2 = \frac{20}{4.9} \approx 4.08$$. So $$t = \sqrt{20/4.9} \approx 2.02$$ seconds. Check:
  $$4.9 \cdot 2.02^2 \approx 19.99$$.
- **Where it lands.** At that time $$x = 6 \cdot 2.02 \approx 12.1$$, so it lands about $$12.1$$ m
  from the foot of the cliff.
- **The path.** By :ref{#curves-eliminate} with $$a = 6$$, $$b = 0$$, $$c = -4.9$$, $$d = 0$$ and
  $$e = 20$$, putting $$t = \frac{x}{6}$$ into the height gives
  $$y = 20 - \frac{4.9}{36}x^2 = 20 - \frac{49}{360}x^2 \approx 20 - 0.136x^2$$. The ball traces the
  part of this parabola from $$x = 0$$ to $$x \approx 12.1$$, because the times stop at $$2.02$$.
  The same two steps as in the proof show it, with $$t$$ kept between $$0$$ and $$2.02$$.

The top of the parabola is $$(0, 20)$$, where the ball leaves your hand: a level throw starts at the
highest point of its path.

<CliffBall caption="A ball thrown level at 6 m/s from a 20 m cliff, with a dot every half second. The dots spread out downwards as the ball speeds up, and the ball reaches half height at 1.43 s and the ground at 2.02 s." />

The ball is at half height, $$10$$ m, when $$4.9t^2 = 10$$, that is, at
$$t = \sqrt{10/4.9} \approx 1.43$$ seconds, about $$8.57$$ m out. So it takes $$1.43$$ s to fall the
first $$10$$ m and only about $$0.59$$ s for the second $$10$$ m, because it falls faster and
faster. You can watch it happen with the ball in the tracer under
[A curve with a sharp point](#a-curve-with-a-sharp-point).

## The polar map

Polar coordinates describe a point by its distance $$r$$ from $$O$$ and an angle $$\theta$$
(:ref{#trigonometry/polar-coordinates}). Turned into a mapping, the rule takes in a pair of numbers
and gives out a point. Picture the pairs $$(r, \theta)$$ as points of a second plane, with $$r$$
across and $$\theta$$ up: the mapping bends that plane's straight lines into circles and into lines
through $$O$$.

:::definition[The polar map]{#polar-map}

The **polar map** is the mapping $$P: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$$ given by

```math
P(r, \theta) = (r\cos\theta,\ r\sin\theta)
```

for all numbers $$r$$ and $$\theta$$.

:::

For $$r \ge 0$$, the point $$P(r, \theta)$$ is the point with polar coordinates $$r$$ and
$$\theta$$. The polar map also takes negative $$r$$: $$P(-2, \frac{\pi}{4}) = (-\sqrt2, -\sqrt2)$$,
the point $$2$$ from $$O$$ in the direction opposite to the angle $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$. Many pairs go
to the same point. For example $$P(2, 0) = P(2, 2\pi) = (2, 0)$$, and $$P(0, \theta) = O$$ for every
$$\theta$$.

:::proposition[Images under the polar map]{#polar-map-images}

1. **Vertical lines go to circles**: for a number $$c > 0$$, the image of the vertical line
   $$r = c$$, the set of pairs $$(c, \theta)$$ for every $$\theta$$, is the circle with centre $$O$$
   and radius $$c$$.
2. **Horizontal lines go to lines through O**: for any number $$c$$, the image of the horizontal
   line $$\theta = c$$, the set of pairs $$(r, c)$$ for every $$r$$, is the line through $$O$$ with
   direction $$(\cos c, \sin c)$$.
3. **Rectangles go to pieces of rings**: let $$0 \le r_1 \le r_2$$ and $$\theta_1 \le \theta_2$$.
   The image of the rectangle of pairs with $$r_1 \le r \le r_2$$ and
   $$\theta_1 \le \theta \le \theta_2$$ is the set of points $$X = |X|(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$$
   with $$r_1 \le |X| \le r_2$$ and $$\theta_1 \le \theta \le \theta_2$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_coordinate_system#Polar_equation_of_a_curve"}

Each part says two sets are equal, so each needs both directions (:ref{#basic-proofs/set-equality}).

**Part 1.** Every point $$P(c, \theta) = (c\cos\theta, c\sin\theta)$$ is at distance
$$\sqrt{c^2\cos^2\theta + c^2\sin^2\theta} = \sqrt{c^2} = c$$ from $$O$$, by
:ref{#coordinates/distance-formula} and :ref{#trigonometry/pythagorean-identity}, since $$c > 0$$.
So it is on the circle. Now take a point $$X = (x, y)$$ of the circle. The point
$$U = (\frac{x}{c}, \frac{y}{c})$$ is at distance $$\frac{c}{c} = 1$$ from $$O$$, so it is on the
unit circle, and $$U = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$$ for some $$\theta$$
(:ref{#trigonometry/unit-circle-point}). Then $$X = (c\cos\theta, c\sin\theta) = P(c, \theta)$$.

**Part 2.** Write $$A = (\cos c, \sin c)$$, which is on the unit circle, so $$A \ne O$$. Then

```math
P(r, c) = (r\cos c,\ r\sin c) = O + rA
```

As $$r$$ runs through all numbers, the points $$O + rA$$ are exactly the points of the line through
$$O$$ with direction $$A$$ (:ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/line-formula}).

**Part 3.** Take $$r$$ and $$\theta$$ in the ranges, and let $$X = P(r, \theta)$$. Then
$$X = r(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$$, and its length is $$|X| = r \cdot 1 = r$$, because $$r \ge 0$$
and $$(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$$ has length $$1$$ (:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/norm-scale}). So
$$X = |X|(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$$ with $$r_1 \le |X| \le r_2$$. The other way round, a point
$$X = |X|(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$$ with $$|X|$$ and $$\theta$$ in the ranges is $$P(|X|, \theta)$$,
the image of a pair in the rectangle.

:::

In words, part 3 says the rectangle goes to the points between the circles of radius $$r_1$$ and
$$r_2$$ that lie on the rays from $$O$$ at angles from $$\theta_1$$ to $$\theta_2$$. That is a piece
of a ring, called a **polar rectangle**. For example, the rectangle $$1 \le r \le 2$$,
$$\frac{\pi}{2} \le \theta \le \pi$$ goes to a quarter of the ring between radius $$1$$ and radius
$$2$$, in the top left quarter of the plane. Its corners go to $$P(1, \frac{\pi}{2}) = (0, 1)$$,
$$P(2, \frac{\pi}{2}) = (0, 2)$$, $$P(2, \pi) = (-2, 0)$$ and $$P(1, \pi) = (-1, 0)$$
(:ref{#trigonometry/quarter-values}).

<PolarMapPicture caption="The polar map P sends the vertical line r = 2.5 onto the circle of radius 2.5, the horizontal line θ = π/4 onto the line y = x, and the rectangle 1 ≤ r ≤ 2, π/2 ≤ θ ≤ π onto a quarter of a ring." />

The sides of the rectangle show where the arcs and the straight edges come from. The sides $$r = 1$$
and $$r = 2$$ go to arcs of the two circles, by part 1, and the sides $$\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}$$ and
$$\theta = \pi$$ go to pieces of the axes, by part 2. In the picture, the line $$r = 2.5$$ is drawn
from $$\theta = -\pi$$ to $$\theta = \pi$$, which is enough for the whole circle. Carrying on up the
line goes round the circle again and again, since sine and cosine repeat every $$2\pi$$
(:ref{#trigonometry/periodic}).

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on curves traced by mappings, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-curves-table}

Let $$C(t) = (3 - t,\ 2t + 1)$$ for every number $$t$$.

1. Find $$C(-1)$$, $$C(0)$$ and $$C(2)$$.
2. Find the time $$t$$ at which the point is at $$(0, 7)$$.
3. Decide whether the point $$(1, 4)$$ is on the curve traced by $$C$$.

:::solution

1. $$C(-1) = (4, -1)$$, $$C(0) = (3, 1)$$ and $$C(2) = (1, 5)$$.
2. The first coordinate is $$0$$ when $$3 - t = 0$$, so $$t = 3$$. Then $$2 \cdot 3 + 1 = 7$$, so
   $$C(3) = (0, 7)$$.
3. It is not. The first coordinate is $$1$$ only at $$t = 2$$, since $$3 - t = 1$$ gives $$t = 2$$.
   At that time the point is $$(1, 5)$$, not $$(1, 4)$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-curves-circle}

Write down a mapping that traces the circle with centre $$(-1, 2)$$ and radius $$4$$ once,
anticlockwise, starting from its point furthest to the right. Find the point at
$$t = \frac{\pi}{2}$$ and at $$t = \pi$$.

:::solution

Take $$C(t) = (-1 + 4\cos t,\ 2 + 4\sin t)$$ for $$0 \le t < 2\pi$$. It traces the circle, as for
the circle with centre $$(2, 1)$$ under [Circles and ellipses](#circles-and-ellipses), and it starts
at $$C(0) = (3, 2)$$, the point $$4$$ to the right of the centre.

By :ref{#trigonometry/quarter-values}, $$C(\frac{\pi}{2}) = (-1 + 0,\ 2 + 4) = (-1, 6)$$ and
$$C(\pi) = (-1 - 4,\ 2 + 0) = (-5, 2)$$. Check: $$(-5, 2)$$ is $$4$$ to the left of the centre, so
it is on the circle.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-curves-remove}

Remove the parameter from $$C(t) = (t - 2,\ t^2 + 1)$$: find the equation of the curve it traces,
and find its vertex and the time at which the point is there.

:::solution

From $$x = t - 2$$ we get $$t = x + 2$$. Putting that into the second coordinate gives

```math
y = (x + 2)^2 + 1 = x^2 + 4x + 5
```

This is :ref{#curves-eliminate} with $$a = 1$$, $$b = -2$$, $$c = 1$$, $$d = 0$$ and $$e = 1$$, so
the curve is the whole graph of $$y = x^2 + 4x + 5$$. In the form $$y = (x + 2)^2 + 1$$ it is
$$y = x^2$$ slid by $$(-2, 1)$$ (:ref{#parabolas-ellipses-and-hyperbolas/quadratic-parabola}), so
the vertex is $$(-2, 1)$$. That is $$C(0)$$, the point at time $$0$$.

Check with $$t = 1$$: $$C(1) = (-1, 2)$$, and $$1 - 4 + 5 = 2$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-curves-same-image}

Let $$C(t) = (t, t^2)$$ and $$D(t) = (-t, t^2)$$ for every number $$t$$. Show that $$C$$ and $$D$$
trace the same curve, and that they are different mappings.

:::solution

**The images are equal.** The image of $$C$$ is the graph of $$y = x^2$$. Each point
$$D(t) = (-t, t^2) = (-t, (-t)^2)$$ is on that graph. And each point $$(x, x^2)$$ of the graph is
$$D(-x)$$, since $$D(-x) = (x, (-x)^2) = (x, x^2)$$. So the image of $$D$$ is the graph of
$$y = x^2$$ too (:ref{#basic-proofs/set-equality}).

**The mappings are different.** At $$t = 1$$, $$C(1) = (1, 1)$$ and $$D(1) = (-1, 1)$$. One time
with different points is enough (:ref{#mappings-equal}). The point of $$C$$ runs along the parabola
from left to right, and the point of $$D$$ runs from right to left.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-curves-wall}

A ball rolls off the top of a wall $$4.9$$ m high at $$5$$ m/s, so that after $$t$$ seconds it is at
$$B(t) = (5t,\ 4.9 - 4.9t^2)$$, in metres from the foot of the wall.

1. Find when and where it lands.
2. Find the equation of its path.
3. Find where it is after half a second, and how much of the height it has fallen by then.

:::solution

1. It lands when $$4.9 - 4.9t^2 = 0$$, that is, $$t^2 = 1$$, so at $$t = 1$$ second. Then $$x = 5$$:
   it lands $$5$$ m from the wall.
2. By :ref{#curves-eliminate}, putting $$t = \frac{x}{5}$$ into the height gives
   $$y = 4.9 - \frac{4.9}{25}x^2 = 4.9 - 0.196x^2$$, for $$x$$ from $$0$$ to $$5$$.
3. $$B(0.5) = (2.5,\ 4.9 - 4.9 \cdot 0.25) = (2.5, 3.675)$$. It has fallen $$4.9 - 3.675 = 1.225$$
   m, which is a quarter of the height, in half the time. Check with the path:
   $$4.9 - 0.196 \cdot 2.5^2 = 4.9 - 1.225 = 3.675$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-curves-polar}

1. Find $$P(2, \frac{\pi}{3})$$, $$P(-2, \frac{\pi}{3})$$ and $$P(3, \frac{3\pi}{2})$$, where $$P$$
   is the polar map.
2. Find the image of the horizontal line $$\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}$$.
3. Find the image of the rectangle $$0 \le r \le 1$$, $$0 \le \theta \le 2\pi$$.

:::solution

1. By :ref{#trigonometry/sin-cos-30-60}, $$\cos\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{1}{2}$$ and
   $$\sin\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$, so $$P(2, \frac{\pi}{3}) = (1, \sqrt3)$$ and
   $$P(-2, \frac{\pi}{3}) = (-1, -\sqrt3)$$. By :ref{#trigonometry/quarter-values},
   $$\cos\frac{3\pi}{2} = 0$$ and $$\sin\frac{3\pi}{2} = -1$$, so
   $$P(3, \frac{3\pi}{2}) = (0, -3)$$.
2. By part 2 of :ref{#polar-map-images}, it is the line through $$O$$ with direction
   $$(\cos\frac{\pi}{2}, \sin\frac{\pi}{2}) = (0, 1)$$. That is the $$y$$-axis.
3. By part 3 of :ref{#polar-map-images}, it is the set of points $$X = |X|(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$$
   with $$|X| \le 1$$ and $$0 \le \theta \le 2\pi$$. Every point $$X$$ can be written this way with
   some $$\theta$$ from $$0$$ to $$2\pi$$ (:ref{#trigonometry/polar-exist}), so the image is every
   point at distance at most $$1$$ from $$O$$: the disc with centre $$O$$ and radius $$1$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-curves-three-times}

Let $$C(t) = (\cos 3t, \sin 3t)$$ for $$0 \le t < 2\pi$$. Show that the curve traced by $$C$$ is the
unit circle, and find every time at which the point is at $$(1, 0)$$.

:::solution

**The image is the unit circle.** Each $$C(t)$$ is on the unit circle, by part 1 of
:ref{#trigonometry/unit-circle-point} with $$3t$$ in place of $$x$$. The other way round, every
point $$U$$ of the unit circle is $$(\cos x, \sin x)$$ for some $$x$$ with $$0 \le x < 2\pi$$, by
part 2. Then $$t = \frac{x}{3}$$ is between $$0$$ and $$2\pi$$, and $$C(t) = U$$.

**The times at (1, 0).** As $$t$$ runs from $$0$$ to $$2\pi$$, the angle $$3t$$ runs from $$0$$ up
to $$6\pi$$. On each of the stretches $$[0, 2\pi)$$, $$[2\pi, 4\pi)$$ and $$[4\pi, 6\pi)$$, sine and
cosine repeat the values of $$[0, 2\pi)$$ (:ref{#trigonometry/periodic}), where only the angle $$0$$
gives $$(1, 0)$$ (:ref{#trigonometry/unit-circle-point}). So $$3t = 0$$, $$2\pi$$ or $$4\pi$$, and
the point is at $$(1, 0)$$ at the three times $$t = 0$$, $$\frac{2\pi}{3}$$ and $$\frac{4\pi}{3}$$.
The point goes round the circle three times.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-curves-cusp}

Show that every point $$(x, y)$$ with $$y^2 = x^3$$ is $$(t^2, t^3)$$ for exactly one number $$t$$.
So the curve traced by $$t \mapsto (t^2, t^3)$$ is the graph of $$y^2 = x^3$$, and each of its
points is passed at one time only.

:::solution

**When x = 0.** Then $$y^2 = 0$$, so $$y = 0$$. A time $$t$$ with $$t^2 = 0$$ must be $$t = 0$$, and
$$(0^2, 0^3) = (0, 0)$$. So exactly one time works.

**When x ≠ 0.** If $$(t^2, t^3) = (x, y)$$, then $$t^2 = x \ne 0$$, so
$$t = \frac{t^3}{t^2} = \frac{y}{x}$$. So at most one time can work, namely $$t = \frac{y}{x}$$. It
does work:

```math
t^2 = \frac{y^2}{x^2} = \frac{x^3}{x^2} = x \qquad t^3 = t \cdot t^2 = \frac{y}{x} \cdot x = y
```

For example, $$(2.25, 3.375)$$ has $$3.375^2 = 2.25^3$$, and $$t = \frac{3.375}{2.25} = 1.5$$.

:::
