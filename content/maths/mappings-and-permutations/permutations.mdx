title: Permutations
description:
  Rearrangements of 1 to n as one-to-one mappings, written in two rows, combined and undone, and
  counted by n factorial.
---

Imagine a magician who lays four cards face up in a row and then rearranges them by a rule she has
practised: the first card goes to the third place, the second to the fourth, the third to the
second, and the fourth to the first. Give her any four cards and she moves them the same way,
because the rule is about places, not about the cards. You'd also notice that no card is lost and no
place is left empty: each place sends its card to one new place, and each new place gets one card.

A rule like hers, which rearranges the places $$1$$ to $$n$$, is called a **permutation**. In this
guide we'll write permutations down, do one after another, undo them, and count how many there are.

## Shuffles as mappings

Number the places $$1$$, $$2$$, $$3$$, $$4$$ from the left. The magician's rule sends each place to
the place its card moves to, so it is a mapping $$\sigma$$ from $$\{1, 2, 3, 4\}$$ to itself
(:ref{#mapping}), with $$\sigma(1) = 3$$, $$\sigma(2) = 4$$, $$\sigma(3) = 2$$ and
$$\sigma(4) = 1$$. The letter $$\sigma$$ is the Greek "sigma". Each place has one arrow out, as for
every mapping, and here each place also has exactly one arrow in.

<CardMoves caption="The magician's shuffle σ on the cards A, B, C and D. The card in place 1 goes to place 3, place 2 to place 4, place 3 to place 2 and place 4 to place 1, so the row becomes D, C, A, B." />

Exactly one arrow in at each place means two things. No place gets two cards, so $$\sigma$$ is one
to one, and no place is left empty, so it is onto. For a mapping of a finite set to itself, the
first already gives the second.

:::definition[Permutation]{#permutation}

Let $$n \ge 1$$. A **permutation** of $$\{1, \ldots, n\}$$ is a one-to-one mapping
$$\sigma: \{1, \ldots, n\} \to \{1, \ldots, n\}$$ (:ref{#injective}).

:::

Because $$\{1, \ldots, n\}$$ is finite, a permutation is also onto, by
:ref{#finite-injective-surjective}. So it is one to one and onto, and it has an inverse, by
:ref{#inverse-exists-bijective}. Every permutation can be undone.

A permutation is written in two rows: the numbers $$1$$ to $$n$$ on top, and under each one the
number it goes to. The magician's shuffle is

```math
\sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 \\ 3 & 4 & 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
```

Read each column downwards: "$$1$$ goes to $$3$$", "$$2$$ goes to $$4$$", and so on. Since
$$\sigma$$ is one to one, the bottom row lists each of $$1$$ to $$n$$ exactly once.

The bottom row can trip you up, so here is the slip to watch for. After $$\sigma$$, the cards
$$A, B, C, D$$ lie in the order $$D, C, A, B$$, while the bottom row reads $$3, 4, 2, 1$$. The
bottom row says where each card **goes**: the card in place $$1$$, which is $$A$$, goes to place
$$3$$. It does not say which card lands in place $$1$$.

Every rearrangement of a row comes from a permutation in this way. Given a new order for the same
$$n$$ cards, send each place $$i$$ to the place where its card ends up. Reversing $$A, B, C, D$$ to
$$D, C, B, A$$, for example, comes from the permutation with bottom row $$4, 3, 2, 1$$. Different
rearrangements come from different permutations, so counting permutations counts rearrangements,
which [Counting permutations](#counting-permutations) does.

A row with a repeat is not a permutation. The rule with bottom row $$2, 4, 2, 3$$ sends both $$1$$
and $$3$$ to $$2$$, so two cards land in place $$2$$ and place $$1$$ is left empty. It is a mapping,
but not one to one.

## One shuffle after another

Suppose the magician has a second shuffle,

```math
\tau = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 \\ 1 & 3 & 4 & 2 \end{pmatrix}
```

read "tau", which leaves the first card alone and moves the other three. Doing $$\tau$$ first and
then $$\sigma$$ is the composite $$\sigma \circ \tau$$ (:ref{#mapping-composite}). For permutations
the circle is usually left out, and $$\sigma\tau$$ means $$\sigma \circ \tau$$.

So in $$\sigma\tau$$ the right-hand permutation, $$\tau$$, acts first, as in $$\sigma(\tau(i))$$. To
work it out, follow each number through $$\tau$$ and then through $$\sigma$$:

- $$1$$ goes to $$\tau(1) = 1$$, and then to $$\sigma(1) = 3$$
- $$2$$ goes to $$\tau(2) = 3$$, and then to $$\sigma(3) = 2$$
- $$3$$ goes to $$\tau(3) = 4$$, and then to $$\sigma(4) = 1$$
- $$4$$ goes to $$\tau(4) = 2$$, and then to $$\sigma(2) = 4$$

<ComposeColumns caption="Working out στ: arrows for τ from the first column to the second, then arrows for σ from the second to the third. The path from 1, in orange, goes to 1 and then to 3." />

That gives the first line below. The other order, $$\tau\sigma$$, does $$\sigma$$ first: $$1$$ goes
to $$\sigma(1) = 3$$ and then to $$\tau(3) = 4$$, and the other columns go the same way.

```math
\sigma\tau = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 \\ 3 & 2 & 1 & 4 \end{pmatrix} \qquad \tau\sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 \\ 4 & 2 & 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
```

The two are different: $$\sigma\tau$$ sends $$1$$ to $$3$$, and $$\tau\sigma$$ sends it to $$4$$. So
the order of two shuffles matters, as the order of two moves of the plane did
(:ref{#isometries/order-matters}). In each order, though, the result is a permutation again.

:::proposition[A composite of permutations is a permutation]{#permutations-composite}

If $$\sigma$$ and $$\tau$$ are permutations of $$\{1, \ldots, n\}$$, then so is $$\sigma\tau$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Composite_of_Permutations_is_Permutation"}

The composite $$\sigma\tau$$ is a mapping from $$\{1, \ldots, n\}$$ to itself. Suppose
$$\sigma(\tau(i)) = \sigma(\tau(j))$$. Since $$\sigma$$ is one to one, $$\tau(i) = \tau(j)$$. Since
$$\tau$$ is one to one, $$i = j$$. So $$\sigma\tau$$ is one to one (:ref{#injective}).

:::

cards. The two rows under the cards keep track of the whole shuffle done so far, from the starting
row. The second view shows $$\sigma\tau$$ and $$\tau\sigma$$ side by side.

## Undoing a shuffle

Doing nothing is a permutation too: the **identity** $$I$$, which sends every number to itself. Its
two rows are the same, $$1, 2, \ldots, n$$ on top and below. Undoing a shuffle means putting every
card back, and the permutation that does it is the inverse.

:::definition[Identity and inverse permutations]{#permutation-inverse}

The **identity permutation** $$I$$ of $$\{1, \ldots, n\}$$ sends each number to itself. The
**inverse** of a permutation $$\sigma$$ is its inverse mapping $$\sigma^{-1}$$
(:ref{#inverse-mapping}): for each $$j$$, $$\sigma^{-1}(j)$$ is the one number $$i$$ with
$$\sigma(i) = j$$. So

```math
\sigma\sigma^{-1} = I = \sigma^{-1}\sigma
```

:::

The inverse is a permutation too, because it has an inverse of its own, $$\sigma$$, and so it is one
to one by :ref{#inverse-exists-bijective}. To find it from the two rows:

1. Swap the two rows, so that each column is turned upside down.
2. Sort the columns so that the new top row reads $$1$$ to $$n$$.

This works because each column of $$\sigma$$ says "$$i$$ goes to $$\sigma(i)$$", and the inverse has
to send $$\sigma(i)$$ back to $$i$$, which is the same column upside down. Sorting the columns
doesn't change which number goes where. For the magician's shuffle,

```math
\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 4 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix} \quad \text{sorted is} \quad \sigma^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 \\ 4 & 3 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}
```

Check $$\sigma\sigma^{-1} = I$$ one number at a time: $$1$$ goes to $$\sigma^{-1}(1) = 4$$ and then
to $$\sigma(4) = 1$$, $$2$$ goes to $$3$$ and back to $$2$$, $$3$$ goes to $$1$$ and back to $$3$$,
and $$4$$ goes to $$2$$ and back to $$4$$. The inverse also explains the row of cards
$$D, C, A, B$$. Place $$j$$ holds the card that started in place $$\sigma^{-1}(j)$$, and the places
of $$D, C, A, B$$ at the start were $$4, 3, 1, 2$$, the bottom row of $$\sigma^{-1}$$.

Undoing two shuffles reverses their order, as taking off shoes and socks does:
$$(\sigma\tau)^{-1} = \tau^{-1}\sigma^{-1}$$, by :ref{#composite-inverse}. Here
$$\tau^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 \\ 1 & 4 & 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$$, and following each
number through $$\sigma^{-1}$$ and then $$\tau^{-1}$$ gives

```math
\tau^{-1}\sigma^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 \\ 3 & 2 & 1 & 4 \end{pmatrix}
```

That is $$\sigma\tau$$ itself, and it should be: $$\sigma\tau$$ swaps $$1$$ and $$3$$, and swapping
them again undoes it. The other order, $$\sigma^{-1}\tau^{-1}$$, sends $$1$$ to $$4$$ instead, so it
is not the inverse.

## Counting permutations

Take the numbers $$1$$, $$2$$, $$3$$. A permutation can send $$3$$ to any of the three, and then
$$1$$ and $$2$$ have to go, one each, to the two numbers left, in one of two ways. So there are
$$3 \cdot 2 = 6$$ permutations, and the tree below lists them by where $$3$$ goes. The same choice
of where $$n$$ goes, followed by a count of the rest, works for every $$n$$.

<CountingTree caption="The six permutations of 1, 2, 3, written by their bottom rows and sorted by where 3 goes. Each choice of σ(3) leaves two ways to send 1 and 2 to the two numbers left." />

:::theorem[Counting permutations]{#permutation-count}

For every $$n \ge 1$$ there are exactly $$n!$$ permutations of $$\{1, \ldots, n\}$$
(:ref{#numbers/factorial}).

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Cardinality_of_Set_of_Bijections"}

We count by matching. Two finite sets whose elements can be paired off, each element of one with
exactly one element of the other, have the same number of elements. That is what counting means:
pairing the things counted with $$1, 2, \ldots$$. The proof is by induction on $$n$$
(:ref{#basic-proofs/induction}).

**Base case.** For $$n = 1$$, the only mapping of $$\{1\}$$ to itself sends $$1$$ to $$1$$, and it
is a permutation. That is $$1 = 1!$$ permutation.

**Inductive step.** Let $$n \ge 2$$, and suppose there are $$(n - 1)!$$ permutations of
$$\{1, \ldots, n - 1\}$$. Sort the permutations $$\sigma$$ of $$\{1, \ldots, n\}$$ by the number
$$j = \sigma(n)$$, which can be any of $$1$$ to $$n$$. We show that for each $$j$$ there are exactly
$$(n - 1)!$$ permutations with $$\sigma(n) = j$$, in two matchings.

_First matching._ Let $$s$$ be the permutation that swaps $$j$$ and $$n$$ and leaves every other
number where it is (if $$j = n$$, then $$s = I$$). Swapping twice changes nothing, so $$ss = I$$.

- If $$\sigma(n) = j$$, then $$s\sigma$$ sends $$n$$ to $$s(j) = n$$.
- If $$\rho(n) = n$$, then $$s\rho$$ sends $$n$$ to $$s(n) = j$$.
- Doing both returns where you started: $$s(s\sigma) = (ss)\sigma = \sigma$$, and in the same way
  $$s(s\rho) = \rho$$.

Each composite is a permutation by :ref{#permutations-composite}. So $$\sigma \mapsto s\sigma$$
pairs off the permutations with $$\sigma(n) = j$$ with the permutations $$\rho$$ with
$$\rho(n) = n$$, each with exactly one.

_Second matching._ Let $$\rho$$ be a permutation of $$\{1, \ldots, n\}$$ with $$\rho(n) = n$$. For
$$i \le n - 1$$, $$\rho(i) \ne \rho(n) = n$$, because $$\rho$$ is one to one. So $$\rho$$ sends
$$\{1, \ldots, n - 1\}$$ into itself, still one to one, and leaving out $$n$$ gives a permutation of
$$\{1, \ldots, n - 1\}$$.

In the other direction, any permutation of $$\{1, \ldots, n - 1\}$$, together with $$n \mapsto n$$,
is a permutation of $$\{1, \ldots, n\}$$ that fixes $$n$$, that is, sends $$n$$ to itself. The two
steps undo each other, so this pairs off the permutations fixing $$n$$ with the permutations of
$$\{1, \ldots, n - 1\}$$.

_Counting._ By the two matchings and the assumption, for each of the $$n$$ values of $$j$$ there are
$$(n - 1)!$$ permutations with $$\sigma(n) = j$$. Every permutation has exactly one value of
$$\sigma(n)$$, so writing the $$n$$ lists one after another lists every permutation exactly once.
That list has $$n \cdot (n - 1)! = n!$$ entries.

:::

The guide [Factorials](../numbers/factorials.mdx) counted rearrangements by choosing what goes
first, then second, and so on, and :ref{#permutation-count} turns that count into a proof. For three
runners in a race, a finishing order sends each runner to a place, so there are $$3! = 6$$ possible
finishes. For four friends taking four seats round a table each week, there are $$4! = 24$$
seatings, enough for almost half a year without a repeat.

Factorials grow fast. A deck of $$52$$ cards has $$52!$$ orders, a $$68$$-digit number:

```math
52! \approx 8.07 \times 10^{67}
```

Suppose every one of the $$8$$ billion people on Earth had shuffled a deck once a second for the
whole age of the universe, about $$13.8$$ billion years or $$4.4 \times 10^{17}$$ seconds. That is
about $$3.5 \times 10^{27}$$ shuffles, fewer than one in $$10^{40}$$ of all the orders. So when a
deck is well shuffled, with every order equally likely, its order has almost certainly never
occurred before.

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on permutations, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-permutations-read}

Let

```math
\sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 \\ 3 & 5 & 2 & 1 & 4 \end{pmatrix}
```

1. Find $$\sigma(2)$$, $$\sigma(\sigma(1))$$, and the number that $$\sigma$$ sends to $$1$$.
2. Cards $$A, B, C, D, E$$ lie in places $$1$$ to $$5$$. Write the row after $$\sigma$$.

:::solution

1. $$\sigma(2) = 5$$, read from the column under $$2$$. $$\sigma(1) = 3$$, so
   $$\sigma(\sigma(1)) = \sigma(3) = 2$$. The $$1$$ in the bottom row is under $$4$$, so
   $$\sigma(4) = 1$$: the number sent to $$1$$ is $$4$$.
2. $$A$$ goes to place $$3$$, $$B$$ to place $$5$$, $$C$$ to place $$2$$, $$D$$ to place $$1$$ and
   $$E$$ to place $$4$$. Reading places $$1$$ to $$5$$, the row is $$D, C, A, E, B$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-permutations-which}

Each line gives the bottom row of a mapping from $$\{1, 2, 3, 4\}$$ to a set of numbers, with top
row $$1, 2, 3, 4$$. Say which are permutations of $$\{1, 2, 3, 4\}$$, with a reason.

1. $$2, 3, 3, 1$$
2. $$4, 1, 2, 3$$
3. $$1, 2, 3, 5$$
4. $$2, 1, 4, 3$$

:::solution

1. Not a permutation: $$2$$ and $$3$$ both go to $$3$$, so it is not one to one.
2. A permutation: the bottom row lists $$1$$, $$2$$, $$3$$, $$4$$ once each, so no two numbers go to
   the same place.
3. Not a permutation: $$4$$ goes to $$5$$, which is not in $$\{1, 2, 3, 4\}$$, so this is not a
   mapping from the set to itself.
4. A permutation: it swaps $$1$$ with $$2$$ and $$3$$ with $$4$$, and the bottom row lists each
   number once.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-permutations-compose}

Let

```math
\alpha = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 \\ 2 & 1 & 4 & 5 & 3 \end{pmatrix} \qquad \beta = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 \\ 1 & 3 & 5 & 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}
```

Find $$\alpha\beta$$ and $$\beta\alpha$$.

:::solution

For $$\alpha\beta$$, $$\beta$$ acts first. Following each number: $$1 \to 1 \to 2$$,
$$2 \to 3 \to 4$$, $$3 \to 5 \to 3$$, $$4 \to 2 \to 1$$, $$5 \to 4 \to 5$$. For $$\beta\alpha$$,
$$\alpha$$ acts first: $$1 \to 2 \to 3$$, $$2 \to 1 \to 1$$, $$3 \to 4 \to 2$$, $$4 \to 5 \to 4$$,
$$5 \to 3 \to 5$$. So

```math
\alpha\beta = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 \\ 2 & 4 & 3 & 1 & 5 \end{pmatrix} \qquad \beta\alpha = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 \\ 3 & 1 & 2 & 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}
```

They differ, for example at $$1$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-permutations-inverse}

Find the inverse of $$\beta$$ from the last exercise, and check that $$\beta\beta^{-1} = I$$.

:::solution

Swap the rows of $$\beta$$ and sort the columns:

```math
\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 & 5 & 2 & 4 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix} \quad \text{sorted is} \quad \beta^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 \\ 1 & 4 & 2 & 5 & 3 \end{pmatrix}
```

Check, with $$\beta^{-1}$$ first: $$1 \to 1 \to 1$$, $$2 \to 4 \to 2$$, $$3 \to 2 \to 3$$,
$$4 \to 5 \to 4$$ and $$5 \to 3 \to 5$$. Every number comes back to itself, so
$$\beta\beta^{-1} = I$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-permutations-count}

1. Count the permutations of $$\{1, \ldots, 5\}$$ that send $$1$$ to $$1$$, and those that send
   $$1$$ to $$2$$.
2. Count the permutations of $$\{1, \ldots, 5\}$$ that send $$1$$ to $$1$$ and $$2$$ to $$2$$.

:::solution

1. A permutation that sends $$1$$ to $$1$$ sends $$2, 3, 4, 5$$ one to one onto $$2, 3, 4, 5$$, a
   rearrangement of four numbers, so there are $$4! = 24$$ of them, by :ref{#permutation-count}.
   Sending $$1$$ to $$2$$ instead, the swap of $$1$$ and $$2$$ pairs these off with the ones that
   fix $$1$$, as in the proof of :ref{#permutation-count}, so there are also $$24$$. Check: the five
   choices of where $$1$$ goes give $$5 \cdot 24 = 120 = 5!$$ in all.
2. Now $$3, 4, 5$$ are rearranged among themselves, in $$3! = 6$$ ways.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-permutations-deck}

A small deck has $$10$$ cards. Find the number of orders it can be in, and how long it would take to
lay out every order once, at one order a second.

:::solution

There are $$10! = 3628800$$ orders, by :ref{#permutation-count}. A day has
$$24 \cdot 60 \cdot 60 = 86400$$ seconds, and $$3628800 \div 86400 = 42$$. So it would take exactly
$$42$$ days, six weeks without a break.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-permutations-cut}

Cutting a deck of $$6$$ cards moves the top card to the bottom: the card in place $$1$$ goes to
place $$6$$, and every other card moves up one place.

1. Write the cut $$c$$ in two rows, and find $$c^2$$.
2. Show that $$c^6 = I$$ but $$c^k \ne I$$ for $$1 \le k \le 5$$, and find $$c^{-1}$$.

:::solution

1. The card in place $$i$$ goes to place $$i - 1$$ for $$i \ge 2$$, and place $$1$$ goes to $$6$$:

   ```math
   c = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 6 & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix} \qquad c^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 5 & 6 & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}
   ```

   For $$c^2$$, follow each number through $$c$$ twice: $$1 \to 6 \to 5$$, $$2 \to 1 \to 6$$,
   $$3 \to 2 \to 1$$, and so on.

2. Follow the top card: $$c$$ sends place $$1$$ to $$6$$, then $$5$$, $$4$$, $$3$$, $$2$$, and the
   sixth cut brings it back to $$1$$. So $$c^k(1) \ne 1$$ for $$1 \le k \le 5$$, and $$c^k \ne I$$.
   Every card makes the same round of six places, so $$c^6 = I$$. By :ref{#repeating-inverse},
   $$c^{-1} = c^5$$, which moves the bottom card to the top:

   ```math
   c^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 & 6 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
   ```

:::

::::exercise{#ex-permutations-right-multiply}

Fix a permutation $$\tau$$ of $$\{1, \ldots, n\}$$, and let $$R$$ be the mapping that sends each
permutation $$\sigma$$ to $$\sigma\tau$$.

1. Prove that $$R$$ is one to one.
2. Deduce that for every permutation $$\rho$$ there is exactly one $$\sigma$$ with
   $$\sigma\tau = \rho$$, and find it.

:::solution

1. By :ref{#permutations-composite}, $$R$$ sends permutations to permutations. Suppose
   $$\sigma\tau = \sigma'\tau$$. Composing both sides with $$\tau^{-1}$$ on the right gives
   $$(\sigma\tau)\tau^{-1} = (\sigma'\tau)\tau^{-1}$$, and regrouping (:ref{#composite-laws}) gives
   $$\sigma(\tau\tau^{-1}) = \sigma'(\tau\tau^{-1})$$, that is $$\sigma I = \sigma' I$$, so
   $$\sigma = \sigma'$$. This is cancelling, as in :ref{#mapping-cancel}.
2. There are $$n!$$ permutations (:ref{#permutation-count}), a finite set, and $$R$$ maps it to
   itself one to one. So $$R$$ is onto, by :ref{#finite-injective-surjective}: some $$\sigma$$ has
   $$\sigma\tau = \rho$$, and part 1 says there is only one. It is $$\sigma = \rho\tau^{-1}$$, since
   $$(\rho\tau^{-1})\tau = \rho(\tau^{-1}\tau) = \rho I = \rho$$.

:::
