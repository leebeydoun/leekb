Imagine wrapping a present. You put the mug in its box, and then you wrap the box in paper. Whoever
opens it has to work backwards: the paper comes off first, and only then can the box be opened.
Taking the mug out first can't be done, because the paper is in the way.

<GiftWrap caption="Boxing, then wrapping. Undoing starts with the step done last: first unwrap, then take the mug out of the box." />

Doing one mapping and then another is called **composing** them, and undoing a mapping means finding
its **inverse**. In this guide we'll compose mappings of every kind, find out exactly which mappings
can be undone, and see why a chain of them comes undone in the reverse order.

## One mapping after another

A school cloakroom has four numbered pegs, and each child hangs their coat on their own peg. Pegs
$$1$$ and $$2$$ are painted red, and pegs $$3$$ and $$4$$ blue. To find the colour of Ana's peg you
follow two arrows: from Ana to her peg, and then from the peg to its colour.

Write $$f$$ for the mapping from the children to their pegs, with $$f(\text{Ana}) = 2$$,
$$f(\text{Ben}) = 4$$ and $$f(\text{Cal}) = 1$$, and $$g$$ for the mapping from pegs to colours.
Following both arrows sends Ana to $$2$$ and then to red, Ben to $$4$$ and then to blue, and Cal to
$$1$$ and then to red. That is a new mapping, straight from children to colours.

<PegChain caption="First, f sends each child to a peg, and g sends each peg to its colour. Then, following both arrows gives one mapping, g ∘ f, from children straight to colours." />

:::definition[Composite]{#mapping-composite}

Let $$f: S \to T$$ and $$g: T \to U$$ be mappings (:ref{#mapping}). The **composite** $$g \circ f$$,
read "$$g$$ after $$f$$", is the mapping from $$S$$ to $$U$$ with

```math
(g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x))
```

for every $$x$$ in $$S$$. The same formula defines $$g \circ f$$ when $$g$$ is a mapping from any
set that contains the image $$f(S)$$ (:ref{#image-of-set}).

:::

This is the composite of functions (:ref{#functions/function-composite}) and of moves of the plane
(:ref{#isometries/composite}), now for any sets. The slip to watch for is the same too: in
$$g(f(x))$$ the mapping next to $$x$$ is $$f$$, so $$f$$ acts first, even though it is written
second.

The two mappings in a composite can be of different kinds. Let $$C(t) = (\cos t, \sin t)$$, the
curve that traces the unit circle (:ref{#parametrised-curve}). Let $$d$$ be the mapping from the
plane to the numbers that gives each point its distance from $$(1, 0)$$, so
$$d(x, y) = \sqrt{(x - 1)^2 + y^2}$$ by :ref{#coordinates/distance-formula}. Then $$d \circ C$$
gives the distance of the moving point from $$(1, 0)$$ at time $$t$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
(d \circ C)(t) &= \sqrt{(\cos t - 1)^2 + \sin^2 t} && \text{put } (\cos t, \sin t) \text{ into } d \\
&= \sqrt{\cos^2 t - 2\cos t + 1 + \sin^2 t} && \text{multiply out the square} \\
&= \sqrt{2 - 2\cos t} && \text{since } \cos^2 t + \sin^2 t = 1
\end{aligned}
```

The last step is :ref{#trigonometry/pythagorean-identity}. So the composite is a function from
numbers to numbers, made from a curve and a function on the plane. At $$t = \frac{\pi}{3}$$ it gives
$$\sqrt{2 - 1} = 1$$, and at $$t = \pi$$, when the point is at $$(-1, 0)$$, it gives
$$\sqrt{4} = 2$$.

The order matters (:ref{#isometries/order-matters}). Take $$f(x) = x + 3$$ and $$g(x) = 2x$$ on all
numbers. Then $$(g \circ f)(x) = 2(x + 3) = 2x + 6$$, while $$(f \circ g)(x) = 2x + 3$$, so at
$$x = 1$$ the first gives $$8$$ and the second $$5$$. For the cloakroom, the other order doesn't
even make sense: $$g$$ gives colours, and $$f$$ only accepts children.

## Doing nothing, and grouping

A step that leaves everything where it is changes nothing when you put it into a chain, before or
after the other steps. And in a chain of three steps, it doesn't matter which two you think of as
one step first: the arrows are followed in the same order either way.

:::definition[Identity mapping]{#identity-mapping}

For a set $$S$$, the **identity mapping** of $$S$$ is the mapping $$I_S: S \to S$$ with
$$I_S(x) = x$$ for every $$x$$ in $$S$$. When the set is clear, we write $$I$$.

:::

For the set $$\{\text{Ana}, \text{Ben}, \text{Cal}\}$$, the identity draws an arrow from each child
back to the same child. On the numbers, it is the function $$x \mapsto x$$.

:::proposition[Composing with the identity, and grouping]{#composite-laws}

Let $$f: S \to T$$, $$g: T \to U$$ and $$h: U \to V$$ be mappings. Then

1. $$I_T \circ f = f$$ and $$f \circ I_S = f$$
2. $$(h \circ g) \circ f = h \circ (g \circ f)$$

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Function_composition#Properties"}

Two mappings are equal when they have the same domain, the same target and the same value at every
element (:ref{#mappings-equal}). In each part both sides go from $$S$$, to $$T$$ in part 1 and to
$$V$$ in part 2, so it is enough to compare values at each $$x$$ in $$S$$.

**Part 1.** By :ref{#mapping-composite} and :ref{#identity-mapping},
$$(I_T \circ f)(x) = I_T(f(x)) = f(x)$$ and $$(f \circ I_S)(x) = f(I_S(x)) = f(x)$$.

**Part 2.** Using :ref{#mapping-composite} twice on each side,

```math
\begin{aligned}
\big((h \circ g) \circ f\big)(x) &= (h \circ g)(f(x)) = h\big(g(f(x))\big) \\
\big(h \circ (g \circ f)\big)(x) &= h\big((g \circ f)(x)\big) = h\big(g(f(x))\big)
\end{aligned}
```

Both sides give $$h(g(f(x)))$$.

:::

So we can write $$h \circ g \circ f$$ without brackets: first $$f$$, then $$g$$, then $$h$$. This is
:ref{#isometries/composite-associative} again, with any sets in place of the plane.

## Undoing a mapping

At a theatre cloakroom you hand over a coat, a hat and a scarf, and get tickets $$1$$, $$2$$ and
$$3$$ back. At the end of the evening you hand the tickets in, and each one leads to exactly one of
your things. Write $$f$$ for the mapping from things to tickets:

```math
f(\text{coat}) = 2 \qquad f(\text{hat}) = 3 \qquad f(\text{scarf}) = 1
```

The attendant uses the mapping $$g$$ from tickets to things with $$g(1) = \text{scarf}$$,
$$g(2) = \text{coat}$$ and $$g(3) = \text{hat}$$. Then $$g$$ undoes $$f$$:
$$g(f(\text{coat})) = g(2) = \text{coat}$$, and the same for the hat and the scarf. And $$f$$ undoes
$$g$$: $$f(g(1)) = f(\text{scarf}) = 1$$, and the same for tickets $$2$$ and $$3$$.

<TicketArrows caption="The cloakroom mapping f from things to tickets, then the same arrows turned round: the attendant's mapping, labelled f⁻¹, which takes each ticket back to its thing." />

:::definition[Inverse mapping]{#inverse-mapping}

Let $$f: S \to T$$ be a mapping. A mapping $$g: T \to S$$ is an **inverse** of $$f$$ when

```math
g \circ f = I_S \qquad \text{and} \qquad f \circ g = I_T
```

That is, $$g(f(x)) = x$$ for every $$x$$ in $$S$$, and $$f(g(z)) = z$$ for every $$z$$ in $$T$$.

:::

A mapping can't have two different inverses, and the argument is the one used for moves of the plane
in :ref{#isometries/inverse-map-unique}.

:::proposition[Only one inverse]{#inverse-mapping-unique}

Let $$f: S \to T$$ be a mapping. If $$g$$ and $$h$$ are both inverses of $$f$$, then $$g = h$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Inverse_Mapping_is_Unique"}

The proof of :ref{#isometries/inverse-map-unique} used only the rules for composites, and those hold
for every mapping (:ref{#composite-laws}). Here there are two identities, $$I_S$$ and $$I_T$$, in
place of the one $$I$$. Since $$f \circ h = I_T$$ and $$g \circ f = I_S$$,

```math
g = g \circ I_T = g \circ (f \circ h) = (g \circ f) \circ h = I_S \circ h = h
```

The first and last steps are part 1 of :ref{#composite-laws}, and the middle step regroups by
part 2.

:::

So we can speak of **the** inverse of $$f$$, written $$f^{-1}$$ and read "$$f$$ inverse". For the
cloakroom, $$f^{-1}$$ is the attendant's mapping $$g$$. Here are three inverses you've met before:

- **Powers and logarithms**: for $$a > 0$$ with $$a \ne 1$$, the mapping $$x \mapsto a^x$$ from all
  numbers to the positive numbers has inverse $$\log_a$$, from the positive numbers to all numbers.
  The two equations of :ref{#functions/log-inverse} are exactly the two in :ref{#inverse-mapping}.
- **Squares and square roots**: squaring, from the numbers $$x \ge 0$$ to the numbers $$y \ge 0$$,
  has inverse $$y \mapsto \sqrt{y}$$, since $$\sqrt{x^2} = x$$ and $$(\sqrt{y})^2 = y$$ for such
  numbers (:ref{#real-numbers/square-root}).
- **Turns**: the turn about $$O$$ by an angle $$\theta$$ has inverse the turn back by $$-\theta$$
  (:ref{#isometries/inverse-map}).

The target matters in the first example. As a mapping from all numbers to all numbers,
$$x \mapsto a^x$$ has no inverse. An inverse $$g$$ would need $$a^{g(-1)} = -1$$, but every power
$$a^x$$ is positive (:ref{#functions/exponential-positive}).

A word about the notation. The preimage $$f^{-1}(B)$$ (:ref{#preimage}) makes sense for every
mapping $$f$$, even one with no inverse. When $$f$$ does have an inverse, the two readings agree: an
element $$x$$ has $$f(x)$$ in $$B$$ exactly when $$x = f^{-1}(z)$$ for some $$z$$ in $$B$$, namely
$$z = f(x)$$.

## Which mappings can be undone

The cloakroom shows the two ways undoing can fail. If two things got the same ticket, that ticket
couldn't say which one to hand back. If some ticket belonged to nothing, handing it in would get you
nothing back. A mapping can be undone exactly when neither happens: when it is one to one
(:ref{#injective}) and onto (:ref{#surjective}).

:::theorem[Inverses exist exactly for one-to-one onto mappings]{#inverse-exists-bijective}

A mapping $$f: S \to T$$ has an inverse exactly when it is one to one and onto (:ref{#bijective}).

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Bijection_iff_Left_and_Right_Inverse"}

**An inverse makes f one to one.** Suppose $$g$$ is an inverse of $$f$$, and let $$x$$ and $$x'$$ be
in $$S$$ with $$f(x) = f(x')$$. Applying $$g$$ to both sides gives $$g(f(x)) = g(f(x'))$$. Since
$$g \circ f = I_S$$, the left side is $$x$$ and the right side is $$x'$$, so $$x = x'$$.

**An inverse makes f onto.** Take $$z$$ in $$T$$. Since $$f \circ g = I_T$$, $$z = f(g(z))$$, so
$$z$$ is the value of $$f$$ at the element $$g(z)$$ of $$S$$.

**One to one and onto give an inverse.** Now suppose $$f$$ is one to one and onto, and take $$z$$ in
$$T$$. Since $$f$$ is onto, at least one $$x$$ in $$S$$ has $$f(x) = z$$. Since $$f$$ is one to one,
at most one does. So exactly one $$x$$ in $$S$$ has $$f(x) = z$$, and we let $$g(z)$$ be that $$x$$.
This defines a mapping $$g: T \to S$$, and we check the two equations.

- For $$z$$ in $$T$$, $$g(z)$$ is an element that $$f$$ sends to $$z$$, so $$f(g(z)) = z$$. That is
  $$f \circ g = I_T$$.
- For $$x$$ in $$S$$, let $$z = f(x)$$. Then $$g(z)$$ is the one element that $$f$$ sends to $$z$$,
  and $$x$$ is such an element, so $$g(f(x)) = x$$. That is $$g \circ f = I_S$$.

:::

The inverse built in the proof turns every arrow of $$f$$ round, as in the cloakroom picture. For a
function that is one to one, :ref{#functions/inverse-function} did the same with the range as the
target, and that is onto by the definition of the range. The theorem also says when there is no
inverse. Sending each child in a class of $$30$$ to their birth month can't be undone, since some
month has at least two children (:ref{#pigeonhole}), and $$x \mapsto x^2$$ from all numbers to all
numbers can't be undone either: it sends $$3$$ and $$-3$$ both to $$9$$.

## Undoing in reverse order

The present came off in the reverse order of going on, and so did the socks and shoes of
:ref{#isometries/inverse-composite}. The same is true of any chain of mappings that can each be
undone: the last one done is the first one undone.

:::proposition[Undoing in reverse order]{#composite-inverse}

1. Let $$f: S \to T$$ and $$g: T \to U$$ be mappings with inverses. Then $$g \circ f$$ has an
   inverse, and $$(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$$.
2. Let $$f_1, f_2, \ldots, f_m$$ be mappings with inverses, where each $$f_k$$ lands in the domain
   of $$f_{k-1}$$, so that the composite $$f_1 \circ f_2 \circ \cdots \circ f_m$$ makes sense. Then

   ```math
   (f_1 \circ f_2 \circ \cdots \circ f_m)^{-1} = f_m^{-1} \circ \cdots \circ f_2^{-1} \circ f_1^{-1}
   ```

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Inverse_of_Composite_Bijection"}

**Part 1.** This is :ref{#isometries/inverse-composite} with any sets in place of the plane. Its
proof used only grouping and the identity, which work for all mappings (:ref{#composite-laws}). By
:ref{#inverse-mapping}, two composites must be identities. Dropping the brackets and working from
the middle out, with $$g^{-1} \circ g = I_T$$ and $$f \circ f^{-1} = I_T$$,

```math
\begin{aligned}
(f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) \circ (g \circ f) &= f^{-1} \circ (g^{-1} \circ g) \circ f = f^{-1} \circ I_T \circ f = f^{-1} \circ f = I_S \\
(g \circ f) \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) &= g \circ (f \circ f^{-1}) \circ g^{-1} = g \circ I_T \circ g^{-1} = g \circ g^{-1} = I_U
\end{aligned}
```

So $$f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$$ is an inverse of $$g \circ f$$, and by :ref{#inverse-mapping-unique} it
is the inverse.

**Part 2**, by induction on $$m$$ (:ref{#basic-proofs/induction}). For $$m = 1$$ both sides are
$$f_1^{-1}$$. Suppose the formula holds for some $$m$$, and take $$m + 1$$ mappings. Group the first
$$m$$ of them as one mapping $$F = f_1 \circ \cdots \circ f_m$$, which has an inverse by the
assumption. Then by part 1, with $$f_{m+1}$$ first and $$F$$ second,

```math
(F \circ f_{m+1})^{-1} = f_{m+1}^{-1} \circ F^{-1} = f_{m+1}^{-1} \circ f_m^{-1} \circ \cdots \circ f_1^{-1}
```

which is the formula for $$m + 1$$.

:::

Here it is with numbers. With $$f(x) = x + 3$$ and $$g(x) = 2x$$, the composite
$$(g \circ f)(x) = 2x + 6$$ adds $$3$$ and then doubles. The inverses are $$f^{-1}(y) = y - 3$$ and
$$g^{-1}(y) = \frac{y}{2}$$, so the proposition says $$(g \circ f)^{-1}(y) = \frac{y}{2} - 3$$:
halve first, then take away $$3$$. Check: $$x = 1$$ goes to $$8$$, and $$\frac{8}{2} - 3 = 1$$.

Undoing in the same order as the steps were done gives $$g^{-1}(f^{-1}(y)) = \frac{y - 3}{2}$$
instead, which sends $$8$$ to $$\frac{5}{2}$$, not back to $$1$$. Try both orders with three moves
of the plane below: a slide $$S$$ by $$(2, 1)$$, the quarter turn $$G$$ anticlockwise about $$O$$,
and the stretch $$D$$ across by $$2$$, $$D(x, y) = (2x, y)$$.

## Cancelling

With numbers, $$3a = 3b$$ gives $$a = b$$, because multiplying by $$\frac{1}{3}$$ undoes multiplying
by $$3$$. A mapping with an inverse can be cancelled from a composite in the same way, from either
side.

:::proposition[Cancelling]{#mapping-cancel}

Let $$h$$ be a mapping with an inverse.

1. If $$h: T \to U$$ and $$f, g: S \to T$$ are mappings with $$h \circ f = h \circ g$$, then
   $$f = g$$.
2. If $$h: S \to T$$ and $$f, g: T \to U$$ are mappings with $$f \circ h = g \circ h$$, then
   $$f = g$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Bijection_iff_Left_and_Right_Cancellable"}

The proof of :ref{#isometries/cancel-map} works here, with :ref{#composite-laws} for each step.

**Part 1.** Do $$h^{-1}$$ after both sides. Since $$h^{-1} \circ h = I_T$$,

```math
f = I_T \circ f = (h^{-1} \circ h) \circ f = h^{-1} \circ (h \circ f) = h^{-1} \circ (h \circ g) = (h^{-1} \circ h) \circ g = I_T \circ g = g
```

**Part 2.** This time do $$h^{-1}$$ before both sides. Since $$h \circ h^{-1} = I_T$$,

```math
f = f \circ I_T = f \circ (h \circ h^{-1}) = (f \circ h) \circ h^{-1} = (g \circ h) \circ h^{-1} = g \circ (h \circ h^{-1}) = g \circ I_T = g
```

:::

The inverse is needed. Let $$h(x) = x^2$$, $$f(x) = x$$ and $$g(x) = -x$$ on all numbers. Then
$$h(f(x)) = x^2$$ and $$h(g(x)) = (-x)^2 = x^2$$, so $$h \circ f = h \circ g$$, but $$f(1) = 1$$ and
$$g(1) = -1$$.

## Undoing a mapping of the plane

Some mappings of the plane come as formulas, and undoing one means solving equations. Take

```math
f(x, y) = (x + 2y,\ 3x + 4y)
```

To undo $$f$$, start from a point $$(u, v)$$ and look for the point $$(x, y)$$ that $$f$$ sends
there. That means solving $$x + 2y = u$$ and $$3x + 4y = v$$ for $$x$$ and $$y$$, which we do by
substitution (:ref{#linear-equations/substitution}):

```math
\begin{aligned}
x &= u - 2y && \text{rearrange the first equation} \\
3(u - 2y) + 4y &= v && \text{substitute into the second} \\
3u - 2y &= v && \text{multiply out and collect } y \\
y &= \frac{3u - v}{2} && \text{solve for } y \\
x &= u - (3u - v) = v - 2u && \text{back-substitute}
\end{aligned}
```

So for every $$(u, v)$$, exactly one point goes to it, and the guess for the inverse is

```math
g(u, v) = \left(v - 2u,\ \frac{3u - v}{2}\right)
```

Both equations of :ref{#inverse-mapping} hold. Putting $$g(u, v)$$ into $$f$$ gives
$$(v - 2u) + (3u - v) = u$$ and $$3(v - 2u) + 2(3u - v) = v$$, so $$f \circ g = I$$. Putting
$$f(x, y)$$ into $$g$$ gives $$(3x + 4y) - 2(x + 2y) = x$$ and
$$\frac{3(x + 2y) - (3x + 4y)}{2} = \frac{2y}{2} = y$$, so $$g \circ f = I$$. So $$g = f^{-1}$$.
Check one point: $$f(1, 1) = (3, 7)$$, and $$f^{-1}(3, 7) = (7 - 6, \frac{9 - 7}{2}) = (1, 1)$$.

What made this work is the number $$1 \cdot 4 - 2 \cdot 3 = -2$$, the determinant of the two
equations (:ref{#linear-equations/determinant}). When it isn't $$0$$, the equations have exactly one
solution for every $$(u, v)$$ (:ref{#linear-equations/determinant-test}), and the mapping has an
inverse. Change $$3x + 4y$$ to $$2x + 4y$$ and the determinant becomes $$0$$. Then $$f(2, 0)$$ and
$$f(0, 1)$$ are both $$(2, 4)$$, so the new mapping is not one to one and has no inverse.

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on combining and undoing mappings, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-combining-and-undoing-fruit}

Dev, Eli and Fay each pick a piece of fruit, given by the mapping $$f$$ with
$$f(\text{Dev}) = \text{apple}$$, $$f(\text{Eli}) = \text{pear}$$ and
$$f(\text{Fay}) = \text{apple}$$. The mapping $$g$$ gives the price of each fruit on the stall:
apple $$30$$p, pear $$45$$p and plum $$50$$p.

1. Find $$g \circ f$$, and say in words what it does.
2. Explain why $$f \circ g$$ makes no sense.

:::solution

1. By :ref{#mapping-composite}, $$(g \circ f)(\text{Dev}) = g(\text{apple}) = 30$$p,
   $$(g \circ f)(\text{Eli}) = g(\text{pear}) = 45$$p and
   $$(g \circ f)(\text{Fay}) = g(\text{apple}) = 30$$p. It sends each person to the price of the
   fruit they picked.
2. $$f \circ g$$ would mean "first $$g$$, then $$f$$". But $$g$$ gives prices, and $$f$$ only
   accepts the three people, so there is nothing for $$f$$ to do with $$30$$p.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-combining-and-undoing-numbers}

Let $$f(x) = 3x - 1$$ and $$g(x) = x^2$$ on all numbers. Find $$(g \circ f)(2)$$ and
$$(f \circ g)(2)$$, and a formula for each composite.

:::solution

$$(g \circ f)(2) = g(5) = 25$$ and $$(f \circ g)(2) = f(4) = 11$$. In general

```math
(g \circ f)(x) = (3x - 1)^2 = 9x^2 - 6x + 1 \qquad (f \circ g)(x) = 3x^2 - 1
```

Check at $$x = 2$$: $$36 - 12 + 1 = 25$$ and $$12 - 1 = 11$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-combining-and-undoing-finite}

1. Let $$f$$ be the mapping from $$\{1, 2, 3, 4\}$$ to $$\{A, B, C, D\}$$ with $$f(1) = C$$,
   $$f(2) = A$$, $$f(3) = D$$ and $$f(4) = B$$. Find $$f^{-1}$$, and check that
   $$f^{-1} \circ f = I$$.
2. Let $$h$$ be the mapping between the same sets with $$h(1) = A$$, $$h(2) = A$$, $$h(3) = B$$ and
   $$h(4) = C$$. Give two reasons why $$h$$ has no inverse.

:::solution

1. Turning the arrows round gives $$f^{-1}(A) = 2$$, $$f^{-1}(B) = 4$$, $$f^{-1}(C) = 1$$ and
   $$f^{-1}(D) = 3$$. Check: $$f^{-1}(f(1)) = f^{-1}(C) = 1$$, $$f^{-1}(f(2)) = f^{-1}(A) = 2$$,
   $$f^{-1}(f(3)) = f^{-1}(D) = 3$$ and $$f^{-1}(f(4)) = f^{-1}(B) = 4$$.
2. $$h$$ is not one to one, since $$h(1) = h(2) = A$$. And $$h$$ is not onto, since no arrow arrives
   at $$D$$. Either one is enough, by :ref{#inverse-exists-bijective}.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-combining-and-undoing-plane}

Let $$f(x, y) = (2x + y,\ x + y)$$. Find $$f^{-1}$$ by solving equations, check both equations of
:ref{#inverse-mapping}, and check your answer with the point $$(1, 2)$$.

:::solution

Solve $$2x + y = u$$ and $$x + y = v$$. Taking the second from the first gives $$x = u - v$$. Then
the second gives $$y = v - x = v - (u - v) = 2v - u$$. So the guess is
$$g(u, v) = (u - v,\ 2v - u)$$.

- $$f(g(u, v))$$: $$2(u - v) + (2v - u) = u$$ and $$(u - v) + (2v - u) = v$$, so $$f \circ g = I$$.
- $$g(f(x, y))$$: $$(2x + y) - (x + y) = x$$ and $$2(x + y) - (2x + y) = y$$, so $$g \circ f = I$$.

So $$f^{-1}(u, v) = (u - v,\ 2v - u)$$. Check: $$f(1, 2) = (4, 3)$$, and $$f^{-1}(4, 3) = (1, 2)$$.
The determinant is $$2 \cdot 1 - 1 \cdot 1 = 1$$, which is not $$0$$, as
:ref{#linear-equations/determinant-test} needs.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-combining-and-undoing-three}

Let $$f(x) = x + 4$$, $$g(x) = 3x$$ and $$h(x) = x - 1$$ on all numbers. Find a formula for
$$h \circ g \circ f$$, and find its inverse in two ways: by solving, and by
:ref{#composite-inverse}.

:::solution

$$(h \circ g \circ f)(x) = h(3(x + 4)) = 3x + 12 - 1 = 3x + 11$$.

**By solving.** $$y = 3x + 11$$ gives $$x = \frac{y - 11}{3}$$.

**In reverse order.** The inverses are $$f^{-1}(y) = y - 4$$, $$g^{-1}(y) = \frac{y}{3}$$ and
$$h^{-1}(y) = y + 1$$. By part 2 of :ref{#composite-inverse}, the inverse is
$$f^{-1} \circ g^{-1} \circ h^{-1}$$: add $$1$$, then divide by $$3$$, then take away $$4$$. That
gives $$\frac{y + 1}{3} - 4 = \frac{y + 1 - 12}{3} = \frac{y - 11}{3}$$, the same.

Check: $$x = 2$$ goes to $$17$$, and $$\frac{17 - 11}{3} = 2$$.

::::exercise{#ex-combining-and-undoing-bijective}

Let $$f: S \to T$$ and $$g: T \to U$$ both be one to one and onto. Prove that $$g \circ f$$ is one
to one and onto.

:::solution

By :ref{#inverse-exists-bijective}, $$f$$ and $$g$$ both have inverses. By part 1 of
:ref{#composite-inverse}, $$g \circ f$$ then has an inverse, namely $$f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$$. Using
:ref{#inverse-exists-bijective} again, in the other direction, a mapping with an inverse is one to
one and onto. So $$g \circ f$$ is one to one and onto.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-combining-and-undoing-one-side}

Let $$f: S \to T$$ and $$g: T \to S$$ be mappings with $$g \circ f = I_S$$.

1. Prove that $$f$$ is one to one and that $$g$$ is onto.
2. Let $$S = \{1\}$$ and $$T = \{1, 2\}$$, with $$f(1) = 1$$ and $$g(1) = g(2) = 1$$. Check that
   $$g \circ f = I_S$$, and show that $$g$$ is not an inverse of $$f$$.

:::solution

1. **f is one to one.** If $$f(x) = f(x')$$, then $$x = g(f(x)) = g(f(x')) = x'$$. **g is onto.**
   Each $$x$$ in $$S$$ is $$g(f(x))$$, the value of $$g$$ at the element $$f(x)$$ of $$T$$.
2. $$g(f(1)) = g(1) = 1$$, so $$g \circ f = I_S$$. But $$f(g(2)) = f(1) = 1$$, not $$2$$, so
   $$f \circ g \ne I_T$$, and :ref{#inverse-mapping} needs both equations. One equation on its own
   is not enough to make an inverse.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-combining-and-undoing-cancel}

Let $$h(x) = x^2$$, $$f(x) = x$$ and $$g(x) = |x|$$ on all numbers. Show that
$$f \circ h = g \circ h$$ but $$f \ne g$$, and explain why this doesn't contradict
:ref{#mapping-cancel}.

:::solution

For every $$x$$, $$f(h(x)) = x^2$$, and $$g(h(x)) = |x^2| = x^2$$ because $$x^2 \ge 0$$. So
$$f \circ h = g \circ h$$. But $$f(-1) = -1$$ and $$g(-1) = 1$$, so $$f \ne g$$
(:ref{#mappings-equal}).

Part 2 of :ref{#mapping-cancel} needs $$h$$ to have an inverse. As a mapping from all numbers to all
numbers, $$h$$ is not one to one, since $$h(1) = h(-1) = 1$$, so it has no inverse
(:ref{#inverse-exists-bijective}).
