Imagine a playlist of five songs set to repeat, and you're listening to song 2. Press "next" and
song 3 starts; press it again and again and you hear songs 4, 5 and 1. The fifth press brings song 2
back, and five more presses bring it back again, however many rounds you make.

<PlaylistLoop caption="The next button on a playlist of five songs on repeat. Every song has one arrow out, and following five arrows from any song brings you back to it." />

A calculator's "×2" key behaves differently. Start from $$3$$ and press it over and over: $$6$$,
$$12$$, $$24$$, $$48$$, and the numbers never come back. Both buttons do one mapping again and
again, and doing a mapping $$k$$ times in a row is called its $$k$$th **power**. In this guide we'll
define powers of a mapping, prove that they follow the rules you know for powers of numbers, and see
which mappings come back to where they started.

## Doing a mapping again

To press a button twice, whatever comes out of the first press has to be something the button
accepts. The next button takes a song and gives a song, so you can press it as often as you like. So
every mapping in this guide goes from a set to itself, $$f: S \to S$$, and the composite
$$f \circ f$$ (:ref{#mapping-composite}) always makes sense.

Write $$N$$ for the next button, a mapping from the songs $$\{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$$ to themselves:
$$N(1) = 2$$, $$N(2) = 3$$, $$N(3) = 4$$, $$N(4) = 5$$ and $$N(5) = 1$$. Pressing twice from song
$$2$$ gives $$N(N(2)) = N(3) = 4$$. Pressing three times gives $$N(N(N(2))) = N(4) = 5$$, and the
brackets pile up, so powers get a shorter name.

:::definition[Powers of a mapping]{#mapping-powers}

Let $$f: S \to S$$ be a mapping from a set $$S$$ to itself. The **powers** of $$f$$ are the mappings
$$f^k: S \to S$$, one for each whole number $$k \ge 0$$, given by

```math
f^0 = I_S \qquad \text{and} \qquad f^{k + 1} = f \circ f^k
```

where $$I_S$$ is the identity mapping of $$S$$ (:ref{#identity-mapping}). Read $$f^k$$ as "$$f$$ to
the $$k$$".

:::

Each power is built from the one before it: to do $$f$$ a total of $$k + 1$$ times, do it $$k$$
times and then once more. A definition made a step at a time like this is called a definition by
**recursion**. The first few steps give $$f^1 = f \circ I_S = f$$, by :ref{#composite-laws}, then
$$f^2 = f \circ f$$ and $$f^3 = f \circ f \circ f$$. This is how :ref{#isometries/map-powers}
counted repeated moves of the plane, now for a mapping of any set.

The two buttons from the start fit the definition. For the playlist, $$N^3(2) = 5$$ and
$$N^5(2) = 2$$. For the doubling key $$d(x) = 2x$$ on the numbers, $$d^4(3) = 48$$.

<DoublingJumps caption="The doubling key pressed four times from 3: the jumps land on 6, 12, 24 and 48, each twice as long as the one before." />

One slip is worth naming. Here $$f^2(x)$$ means $$f(f(x))$$, which is not the square $$(f(x))^2$$.
With $$d(x) = 2x$$, $$d^2(3) = d(6) = 12$$, while $$(d(3))^2 = 36$$. The one place where the habit
differs is trigonometry, where $$\sin^2 x$$ means $$(\sin x)^2$$.

## Powers you can work out

Each of the mappings below has a formula for $$f^k$$. You find it by doing $$f$$ a few times and
spotting the pattern, and then induction (:ref{#basic-proofs/induction}) proves the pattern holds
for every $$k$$.

:::example[Adding 5 over and over]{#repeating-a-mapping-add-five}

Let $$a(x) = x + 5$$ on the numbers. From $$2$$ the powers give $$2$$, $$7$$, $$12$$, $$17$$ and
$$22$$: each press adds $$5$$ more. So we expect $$a^k(x) = x + 5k$$ for every whole number
$$k \ge 0$$.

**Base case.** For $$k = 0$$, $$a^0(x) = I(x) = x = x + 5 \cdot 0$$.

**Inductive step.** Suppose $$a^k(x) = x + 5k$$ for every $$x$$. Then

```math
a^{k + 1}(x) = a\big(a^k(x)\big) = (x + 5k) + 5 = x + 5(k + 1)
```

by the definition of powers and then the assumption.

The principle of induction starts at $$1$$, and this starts at $$0$$. That changes nothing in the
argument: apply the principle to the statement for $$k - 1$$ in place of $$k$$.

:::

The same pattern-then-induction works for other mappings, with the step changed:

- **Doubling**: $$d^k(x) = 2^k x$$, since $$d^{k + 1}(x) = 2 \cdot 2^k x = 2^{k + 1} x$$. Check with
  the key: $$2^4 \cdot 3 = 48$$.
- **Squaring**: for $$s(x) = x^2$$, $$s^k(x) = x^{2^k}$$, since
  $$s^{k + 1}(x) = (x^{2^k})^2 = x^{2^{k + 1}}$$. From $$2$$ the powers give $$2$$, $$4$$, $$16$$,
  $$256$$ and $$65536 = 2^{16}$$. The exponent doubles each time, so $$s^3(x) = x^8$$, not $$x^6$$.
- **A slide**: for the slide $$T_A(X) = X + A$$ by a point $$A$$
  (:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/slide-formula}), $$T_A^k(X) = X + kA$$, since
  $$(X + kA) + A = X + (k + 1)A$$ by :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/sum-rules} and
  :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/scale-sum-rules}. With $$A = (2, 1)$$ and $$X = (-3, 1)$$ the
  trail is $$(-1, 2)$$, $$(1, 3)$$, $$(3, 4)$$.

A turn behaves like the playlist. Let $$G$$ be the turn by $$\frac{2\pi}{5}$$ about the origin
$$O$$, written $$G_{2\pi/5}$$ in :ref{#trigonometry/radian-turns}. Turns add, so $$G^k$$ is the turn
by $$\frac{2\pi k}{5}$$, and $$G^5$$ is the turn by $$2\pi$$, which is the identity. Any point $$X$$
other than $$O$$ visits the five corners of a regular pentagon and comes home.

<TurnPowers caption="The powers of the turn G by 2π/5 about O, applied to X = (3, 0). The five points are a fifth of a turn apart on the circle of radius 3, and the fifth turn brings X home." />

## Adding and multiplying exponents

Pressing next three times and then twice more is pressing it five times. In symbols that should be
$$N^2 \circ N^3 = N^5$$, with $$N^3$$ first since it is on the right. The rule
$$x^m x^n = x^{m + n}$$ for numbers becomes a rule for mappings, and induction proves it.

:::proposition[Adding exponents]{#powers-add}

Let $$f: S \to S$$ be a mapping. For all whole numbers $$m, n \ge 0$$,

```math
f^m \circ f^n = f^{m + n}
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Index_Laws_for_Monoids/Sum_of_Indices"}

Fix $$n$$, and use induction on $$m$$, starting at $$m = 0$$ as in
:ref{#repeating-a-mapping-add-five}.

**Base case.** For $$m = 0$$, $$f^0 \circ f^n = I_S \circ f^n = f^n$$, by :ref{#composite-laws}.

**Inductive step.** Suppose $$f^m \circ f^n = f^{m + n}$$. Then

```math
\begin{aligned}
f^{m + 1} \circ f^n &= (f \circ f^m) \circ f^n && \text{the definition of powers} \\
&= f \circ (f^m \circ f^n) && \text{grouping doesn't matter} \\
&= f \circ f^{m + n} && \text{the assumption} \\
&= f^{m + n + 1} && \text{the definition of powers}
\end{aligned}
```

The second step is the grouping rule in :ref{#composite-laws}.

:::

So $$f^m \circ f^n = f^{m + n} = f^n \circ f^m$$: two powers of one mapping give the same result in
either order. That is worth noticing, since two different mappings can give different results in the
two orders (:ref{#isometries/order-matters}). Taking $$n = 1$$ also gives
$$f^{m + 1} = f^m \circ f$$, so the extra $$f$$ can be done first or last. Check with
$$a(x) = x + 5$$: $$a^3(a^2(x)) = (x + 10) + 15 = x + 25 = a^5(x)$$.

Pressing next twice, three times over, is pressing it six times. That is the second rule for powers
of numbers, $$(x^m)^n = x^{mn}$$.

:::proposition[Multiplying exponents]{#powers-multiply}

Let $$f: S \to S$$ be a mapping. For all whole numbers $$m, n \ge 0$$,

```math
(f^m)^n = f^{mn}
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Index_Laws_for_Monoids/Product_of_Indices"}

Fix $$m$$, and use induction on $$n$$, starting at $$n = 0$$.

**Base case.** For $$n = 0$$, both sides are $$I_S$$: $$(f^m)^0 = I_S = f^0 = f^{m \cdot 0}$$.

**Inductive step.** Suppose $$(f^m)^n = f^{mn}$$. Then

```math
\begin{aligned}
(f^m)^{n + 1} &= f^m \circ (f^m)^n && \text{the definition of powers, for the mapping } f^m \\
&= f^m \circ f^{mn} && \text{the assumption} \\
&= f^{m + mn} && \text{adding exponents} \\
&= f^{m(n + 1)} && \text{since } m + mn = m(n + 1)
\end{aligned}
```

The third step is :ref{#powers-add}.

:::

For squaring, $$s^2(x) = x^4$$, and doing $$s^2$$ three times gives $$((x^4)^4)^4 = x^{64}$$. The
rule agrees: $$(s^2)^3 = s^6$$, and $$s^6(x) = x^{2^6} = x^{64}$$.

## Going backwards

A playlist usually has a "previous" button too, and it undoes "next". Pressing previous three times
is pressing the inverse of $$N$$ three times, and it is natural to write it $$N^{-3}$$. Negative
powers make sense only for a mapping that can be undone, which by :ref{#inverse-exists-bijective}
means one that is one to one and onto.

:::definition[Negative powers]{#mapping-negative-powers}

Let $$f: S \to S$$ be a mapping with an inverse $$f^{-1}$$ (:ref{#inverse-mapping}). For each whole
number $$k \ge 1$$,

```math
f^{-k} = (f^{-1})^k
```

the inverse done $$k$$ times.

:::

For $$k = 1$$ this gives $$f^{-1} = (f^{-1})^1$$, so the new meaning of $$f^{-1}$$ agrees with the
old one. With both definitions together, $$f^k$$ now has a meaning for every integer $$k$$.

Here is what negative powers look like for the examples so far:

- **Adding 5**: $$a^{-1}(x) = x - 5$$, so $$a^{-k}(x) = x - 5k$$. The formula $$a^k(x) = x + 5k$$
  now holds for every integer $$k$$.
- **Doubling**: $$d^{-1}(x) = \frac{x}{2}$$, so $$d^{-k}(x) = \frac{x}{2^k} = 2^{-k}x$$, and again
  $$d^k(x) = 2^k x$$ for every integer $$k$$.
- **Squaring on all numbers**: no negative powers at all, since $$(-3)^2 = 3^2$$ means squaring is
  not one to one, so it has no inverse.
- **The turn** $$G$$: $$G^{-1}$$ is the turn back by $$\frac{2\pi}{5}$$, and $$G^{-k}$$ is the turn
  by $$-\frac{2\pi k}{5}$$.

Pressing next three times and then previous three times should leave you where you were. The next
proposition says so for every mapping with an inverse.

:::proposition[Negative powers undo]{#powers-undo}

Let $$f: S \to S$$ be a mapping with an inverse. For every whole number $$k \ge 0$$,

```math
f^k \circ f^{-k} = I_S = f^{-k} \circ f^k
```

where $$f^{-0}$$ means $$f^0 = I_S$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Powers_of_Group_Elements/Negative_Index"}

Write $$g = f^{-1}$$, so that $$f^{-k} = g^k$$, and use induction on $$k$$, starting at $$k = 0$$.

**Base case.** For $$k = 0$$, both composites are $$I_S \circ I_S = I_S$$.

**Inductive step.** Suppose $$f^k \circ g^k = I_S$$ and $$g^k \circ f^k = I_S$$. By the definition
of powers $$f^{k + 1} = f \circ f^k$$, and by :ref{#powers-add} for the mapping $$g$$,
$$g^{k + 1} = g^k \circ g$$. So

```math
\begin{aligned}
f^{k + 1} \circ g^{k + 1} &= (f \circ f^k) \circ (g^k \circ g) && \text{the two facts just given} \\
&= f \circ (f^k \circ g^k) \circ g && \text{grouping doesn't matter} \\
&= f \circ I_S \circ g && \text{the assumption} \\
&= f \circ g = I_S && \text{the identity, then } g \text{ is the inverse of } f
\end{aligned}
```

using :ref{#composite-laws} for the second and fourth steps and :ref{#inverse-mapping} for the last.
In the other order, $$g^{k + 1} = g \circ g^k$$ and $$f^{k + 1} = f^k \circ f$$ give
$$g^{k + 1} \circ f^{k + 1} = g \circ (g^k \circ f^k) \circ f = g \circ f = I_S$$ in the same way.

:::

So $$f^{-k}$$ undoes $$f^k$$ from both sides, which makes it an inverse of $$f^k$$. A mapping has
only one inverse (:ref{#inverse-mapping-unique}), so $$(f^k)^{-1} = f^{-k}$$. For the slide by
$$A = (2, 1)$$, that says the slide by $$3A = (6, 3)$$ is undone by the slide by $$(-6, -3)$$.

Now mix the signs. Two presses forward and then five back should be three back, that is
$$f^{-5} \circ f^2 = f^{-3}$$. Negative powers were defined separately from positive ones, so this
needs a proof of its own.

:::theorem[Adding exponents for all integers]{#powers-add-integers}

Let $$f: S \to S$$ be a mapping with an inverse. For all integers $$m$$ and $$n$$,

```math
f^m \circ f^n = f^{m + n}
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Powers_of_Group_Elements/Sum_of_Indices"}

Write $$g = f^{-1}$$. The plan is to prove the rule for one press forward or back first, and then to
build up any $$m$$ from those single presses.

**Step 1: one press forward.** For every integer $$n$$, $$f \circ f^n = f^{n + 1}$$. If $$n \ge 0$$,
this is the definition of $$f^{n + 1}$$. If $$n < 0$$, write $$n = -k$$ with $$k \ge 1$$. Then
$$f^n = g^k = g \circ g^{k - 1}$$, so

```math
f \circ f^n = (f \circ g) \circ g^{k - 1} = I_S \circ g^{k - 1} = g^{k - 1}
```

by :ref{#composite-laws} and :ref{#inverse-mapping}. And $$g^{k - 1} = f^{-(k - 1)} = f^{n + 1}$$:
for $$k \ge 2$$ by :ref{#mapping-negative-powers}, and for $$k = 1$$ because both sides are $$I_S$$.

**Step 2: one press back.** For every integer $$n$$, $$g \circ f^n = f^{n - 1}$$. If $$n \le 0$$,
write $$n = -k$$ with $$k \ge 0$$. Then $$g \circ f^n = g \circ g^k = g^{k + 1}$$, which is
$$f^{-(k + 1)} = f^{n - 1}$$ by :ref{#mapping-negative-powers}. If $$n \ge 1$$, then
$$f^n = f \circ f^{n - 1}$$, so

```math
g \circ f^n = (g \circ f) \circ f^{n - 1} = I_S \circ f^{n - 1} = f^{n - 1}
```

**Step 3: m from 0 upwards.** Fix $$n$$. For $$m = 0$$, $$f^0 \circ f^n = I_S \circ f^n = f^n$$.
Suppose $$f^m \circ f^n = f^{m + n}$$ for some $$m \ge 0$$. Then

```math
\begin{aligned}
f^{m + 1} \circ f^n &= f \circ (f^m \circ f^n) && \text{the definition of powers, and grouping} \\
&= f \circ f^{m + n} && \text{the assumption} \\
&= f^{m + n + 1} && \text{step 1}
\end{aligned}
```

So by induction the rule holds for every $$m \ge 0$$.

**Step 4: m from 0 downwards.** Step 2 with $$m$$ in place of $$n$$ says $$f^{m - 1} = g \circ f^m$$
for every integer $$m$$. Suppose $$f^m \circ f^n = f^{m + n}$$ for some $$m \le 0$$. Then

```math
\begin{aligned}
f^{m - 1} \circ f^n &= g \circ (f^m \circ f^n) && \text{step 2, and grouping} \\
&= g \circ f^{m + n} && \text{the assumption} \\
&= f^{m + n - 1} && \text{step 2}
\end{aligned}
```

Starting from $$m = 0$$ and stepping down by $$1$$ reaches every negative integer, so induction on
$$-m$$ proves the rule for every $$m \le 0$$. Together with step 3, that is every integer $$m$$.

:::

Check with $$a(x) = x + 5$$: $$a^{-5}(a^2(x)) = (x + 10) - 25 = x - 15$$, which is $$a^{-3}(x)$$.

## Coming back to the start

The playlist has $$N^5 = I$$: five presses leave every song where it was. So four presses of next do
the same as one press of previous, because four more after the one already made complete the round
of five. A mapping that comes home after $$n$$ presses can always be undone by pressing $$n - 1$$
more times.

:::proposition[Undoing by repeating]{#repeating-inverse}

Let $$f: S \to S$$ be a mapping, and $$n \ge 1$$ a whole number with $$f^n = I_S$$. Then $$f$$ has
an inverse, and

```math
f^{-1} = f^{n - 1}
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Inverse_Element_is_Power_of_Order_Less_1"}

Since $$n - 1 \ge 0$$, :ref{#powers-add} applies with the exponents $$n - 1$$ and $$1$$, in both
orders:

```math
f^{n - 1} \circ f = f^n = I_S \qquad \text{and} \qquad f \circ f^{n - 1} = f^n = I_S
```

So $$f^{n - 1}$$ undoes $$f$$ from both sides, which is what :ref{#inverse-mapping} asks of an
inverse.

:::

This is the fact :ref{#isometries/inverse-by-repeating} proved for moves of the plane, now for any
mapping. For the turn $$G$$ by $$\frac{2\pi}{5}$$, $$G^5 = I$$, so $$G^{-1} = G^4$$, the turn by
$$\frac{8\pi}{5}$$. That agrees with turning back by $$\frac{2\pi}{5}$$, since
$$-\frac{2\pi}{5} + 2\pi = \frac{8\pi}{5}$$ (part 3 of :ref{#trigonometry/radian-turns}).

When $$f^n = I_S$$, only the remainder of the number of presses on dividing by $$n$$ matters. Take
$$k$$ presses and divide by $$n$$ (:ref{#numbers/division-remainder}): $$k = nq + r$$ with
$$0 \le r < n$$. Then

```math
\begin{aligned}
f^k &= f^{nq} \circ f^r && \text{adding exponents} \\
&= (f^n)^q \circ f^r && \text{multiplying exponents} \\
&= I_S^q \circ f^r = f^r && \text{since } f^n = I_S
\end{aligned}
```

using :ref{#powers-add} and :ref{#powers-multiply}, and the fact that every power of $$I_S$$ is
$$I_S$$ (by induction, since $$I_S \circ I_S = I_S$$). On the playlist, $$23 = 5 \cdot 4 + 3$$, so
$$23$$ presses from song $$2$$ end where $$3$$ presses do, at song $$5$$.

Equal powers carry information too. Suppose $$f^4 = I_S$$ and $$f^6 = I_S$$. Then, by adding
exponents,

```math
f^6 = f^2 \circ f^4 = f^2 \circ I_S = f^2
```

so $$f^2 = f^6 = I_S$$. The half turn $$(x, y) \mapsto (-x, -y)$$ is a mapping like this, while the
quarter turn has $$f^4 = I$$ but $$f^6$$ is the half turn, not $$I$$. In the same way, if $$f$$ has
an inverse and $$f^m = f^n$$, then composing both sides with $$f^{-n}$$ on the right gives
$$f^{m - n} = f^0 = I_S$$, by :ref{#powers-add-integers}.

Following one element as a mapping repeats, as we followed song $$2$$, traces out the places it
visits. For permutations those places are called the orbit of the element. Orbits are the subject of
the guide [Cycles](./cycles.mdx).

## Following a point

The mapping below acts on the plane, and each press of "apply again" adds the next point $$f^k(X)$$
to the trail. The turn by $$\frac{2\pi}{5}$$ and the mapping $$(x, y) \mapsto (y, -x)$$, which is
the quarter turn clockwise, come home after $$5$$ and $$4$$ presses. The slide moves the point the
same step further each time, and the zoom by $$\frac12$$ halves its distance to $$O$$ each time, so
neither ever returns.

<IterateMap />

The turn by $$1$$ radian never comes home either, though it gets close: after $$44$$ presses the
point is within $$0.06$$ of $$X$$. Coming home after $$k$$ presses would need $$k$$ radians to be a
whole number $$m$$ of turns, $$k = 2\pi m$$, and then $$\pi = \frac{k}{2m}$$ would be a fraction.
But $$\pi$$ is irrational, a fact we take as given here, so the point never comes home. The trail
also spreads all the way round the circle, as the tracer shows, but proving that takes an argument
we won't give here.

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on repeating a mapping, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-repeating-a-mapping-values}

Let $$f(x) = 3x - 1$$ on the numbers.

1. Find $$f(1)$$, $$f^2(1)$$ and $$f^3(1)$$.
2. Find formulas for $$f^2(x)$$ and $$f^3(x)$$, and check the second one at $$x = 1$$.

:::solution

1. $$f(1) = 3 - 1 = 2$$, then $$f^2(1) = f(2) = 5$$ and $$f^3(1) = f(5) = 14$$.
2. $$f^2(x) = f(3x - 1) = 3(3x - 1) - 1 = 9x - 4$$, and
   $$f^3(x) = f(9x - 4) = 3(9x - 4) - 1 = 27x - 13$$. Check: $$27 - 13 = 14$$, as in part 1.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-repeating-a-mapping-playlist}

A playlist of $$7$$ songs is on repeat, and the next button $$N$$ sends song $$n$$ to song $$n + 1$$
for $$n \le 6$$, and song $$7$$ to song $$1$$.

1. You're on song $$3$$ and press next $$10$$ times. Find the song that is playing.
2. Find the song playing after $$100$$ presses from song $$1$$.
3. Find a positive power of $$N$$ that does the same as one press of previous.

:::solution

1. Seven presses bring every song home, so $$N^7 = I$$. Since $$10 = 7 \cdot 1 + 3$$,
   $$N^{10} = N^3$$, as in [Coming back to the start](#coming-back-to-the-start). Three presses from
   song $$3$$ reach song $$6$$.
2. $$100 = 7 \cdot 14 + 2$$, so $$N^{100} = N^2$$, and two presses from song $$1$$ reach song $$3$$.
3. By :ref{#repeating-inverse} with $$n = 7$$, $$N^{-1} = N^6$$: six presses of next do the same as
   one press of previous.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-repeating-a-mapping-induction}

Let $$h(x) = 2x + 1$$ on the numbers.

1. Prove by induction that $$h^k(x) = 2^k(x + 1) - 1$$ for every whole number $$k \ge 0$$.
2. Find $$h^{-1}$$, and check that the formula also holds for $$k = -1$$.

:::solution

1. **Base case.** For $$k = 0$$, $$2^0(x + 1) - 1 = x = h^0(x)$$. **Inductive step.** If
   $$h^k(x) = 2^k(x + 1) - 1$$, then
   $$h^{k + 1}(x) = h(h^k(x)) = 2\big(2^k(x + 1) - 1\big) + 1 = 2^{k + 1}(x + 1) - 1$$. So the
   formula holds for every $$k \ge 0$$ (:ref{#basic-proofs/induction}, started at $$0$$). Check at
   $$k = 2$$, $$x = 1$$: $$h(1) = 3$$ and $$h(3) = 7$$, while $$4 \cdot 2 - 1 = 7$$.
2. Solving $$y = 2x + 1$$ gives $$x = \frac{y - 1}{2}$$, so $$h^{-1}(x) = \frac{x - 1}{2}$$. The
   formula with $$k = -1$$ gives $$\frac{1}{2}(x + 1) - 1 = \frac{x + 1 - 2}{2} = \frac{x - 1}{2}$$,
   the same.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-repeating-a-mapping-five-eight}

Let $$f: S \to S$$ be a mapping with $$f^5 = I_S$$ and $$f^8 = I_S$$. Prove that $$f = I_S$$.

:::solution

Every step uses :ref{#powers-add}, which needs no inverse.

1. $$f^8 = f^3 \circ f^5 = f^3 \circ I_S = f^3$$, so $$f^3 = f^8 = I_S$$.
2. $$f^5 = f^2 \circ f^3 = f^2 \circ I_S = f^2$$, so $$f^2 = f^5 = I_S$$.
3. $$f^3 = f \circ f^2 = f \circ I_S = f$$, so $$f = f^3 = I_S$$.

Each step takes the smaller exponent from the larger, so the exponents go $$8, 5, 3, 2, 1$$. Taking
the smaller from the larger keeps the common divisors the same, so the run ends at the greatest
common divisor of $$5$$ and $$8$$ (:ref{#numbers/gcd}), which is $$1$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-repeating-a-mapping-sixth-turn}

Let $$G$$ be the turn by $$\frac{\pi}{3}$$ about $$O$$. Write each of $$G^{-4}$$, $$G^{10}$$ and
$$G^{-1}$$ as $$G^r$$ with $$0 \le r < 6$$, and say which turn each one is.

:::solution

Six turns by $$\frac{\pi}{3}$$ make the turn by $$2\pi$$, so $$G^6 = I$$
(:ref{#trigonometry/radian-turns}). By :ref{#powers-add-integers}, adding a multiple of $$6$$ to the
exponent changes nothing, since $$G^{k + 6} = G^k \circ G^6 = G^k$$.

- $$G^{-4} = G^{-4 + 6} = G^2$$, the turn by $$\frac{2\pi}{3}$$. Check:
  $$-\frac{4\pi}{3} + 2\pi = \frac{2\pi}{3}$$.
- $$G^{10} = G^{10 - 6} = G^4$$, the turn by $$\frac{4\pi}{3}$$.
- $$G^{-1} = G^5$$, the turn by $$\frac{5\pi}{3}$$, as :ref{#repeating-inverse} also says.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-repeating-a-mapping-shear}

Let $$F(x, y) = (x + y, y)$$, a mapping of the plane to itself.

1. Prove by induction that $$F^k(x, y) = (x + ky, y)$$ for every whole number $$k \ge 0$$, and find
   $$F^3(2, 5)$$.
2. Show that $$F^k \ne I$$ for every $$k \ge 1$$.
3. Find $$F^{-1}$$.

:::solution

1. For $$k = 0$$, $$(x + 0y, y) = (x, y)$$. If $$F^k(x, y) = (x + ky, y)$$, then
   $$F^{k + 1}(x, y) = F(x + ky, y) = (x + ky + y, y) = (x + (k + 1)y, y)$$. So
   $$F^3(2, 5) = (2 + 15, 5) = (17, 5)$$.
2. $$F^k(0, 1) = (k, 1)$$, which is not $$(0, 1)$$ when $$k \ge 1$$. One point that moves is enough.
3. $$F$$ adds $$y$$ to the first coordinate, so the inverse takes it away:
   $$F^{-1}(x, y) = (x - y, y)$$. Check: $$F(x - y, y) = (x - y + y, y) = (x, y)$$, and
   $$F^{-1}(x + y, y) = (x, y)$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-repeating-a-mapping-commuting}

1. Let $$f(x) = x + 2$$ and $$g(x) = 2x$$. Show that $$(f \circ g)^2 \ne f^2 \circ g^2$$.
2. Let $$f, g: S \to S$$ be mappings with $$f \circ g = g \circ f$$. Prove that
   $$(f \circ g)^k = f^k \circ g^k$$ for every whole number $$k \ge 0$$. First show
   $$g \circ f^k = f^k \circ g$$ by induction.

:::solution

1. $$(f \circ g)(x) = 2x + 2$$, so $$(f \circ g)^2(x) = 2(2x + 2) + 2 = 4x + 6$$. But
   $$g^2(x) = 4x$$ and $$f^2(x) = x + 4$$, so $$(f^2 \circ g^2)(x) = 4x + 4$$. At $$x = 0$$ these
   are $$6$$ and $$4$$.
2. **First,** $$g \circ f^k = f^k \circ g$$. For $$k = 0$$ both sides are $$g$$. If it holds for
   $$k$$, then
   $$g \circ f^{k + 1} = (g \circ f) \circ f^k = f \circ (g \circ f^k) = f \circ f^k \circ g = f^{k + 1} \circ g$$,
   using grouping (:ref{#composite-laws}), $$g \circ f = f \circ g$$ and the assumption. **Then,**
   for $$k = 0$$ both sides are $$I_S$$. If $$(f \circ g)^k = f^k \circ g^k$$, then
   $$(f \circ g)^{k + 1} = f \circ g \circ f^k \circ g^k = f \circ f^k \circ g \circ g^k = f^{k + 1} \circ g^{k + 1}$$,
   using the first part in the middle.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-repeating-a-mapping-three-steps}

Let $$S$$ be the set of numbers other than $$0$$ and $$1$$, and let $$f(x) = \frac{1}{1 - x}$$ for
$$x$$ in $$S$$.

1. Show that $$f(x)$$ is in $$S$$ for every $$x$$ in $$S$$, so $$f: S \to S$$.
2. Show that $$f^2(x) = \frac{x - 1}{x}$$ and $$f^3 = I_S$$, and check with $$x = 3$$.
3. Find $$f^{-1}$$ and $$f^{100}$$.

:::solution

1. The bottom $$1 - x$$ is not $$0$$, since $$x \ne 1$$. The value $$\frac{1}{1 - x}$$ is never
   $$0$$, and it equals $$1$$ exactly when $$1 - x = 1$$, that is $$x = 0$$, which is not in $$S$$.
2. $$f^2(x) = \frac{1}{1 - \frac{1}{1 - x}} = \frac{1 - x}{(1 - x) - 1} = \frac{1 - x}{-x} = \frac{x - 1}{x}$$,
   multiplying top and bottom by $$1 - x$$. Then
   $$f^3(x) = f(f^2(x)) = \frac{1}{1 - \frac{x - 1}{x}} = \frac{x}{x - (x - 1)} = x$$. Check:
   $$f(3) = -\frac12$$, $$f(-\frac12) = \frac{1}{3/2} = \frac23$$ and
   $$f(\frac23) = \frac{1}{1/3} = 3$$.
3. By :ref{#repeating-inverse} with $$n = 3$$, $$f^{-1} = f^2$$, so $$f^{-1}(x) = \frac{x - 1}{x}$$.
   Since $$100 = 3 \cdot 33 + 1$$, $$f^{100} = f^1 = f$$.

:::
