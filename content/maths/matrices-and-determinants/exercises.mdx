These exercises use the whole chapter, and most need ideas from more than one guide. They start
gently and get harder towards the end. Try each one before you open its solution, and name the rule
or result behind each step, as the guides do.

One habit carries most of them: ask what the determinant measures before calculating it. Thinking of
it as an area or a volume, with a sign for flipping, can tell you the answer, or at least whether it
should be $$0$$, before any arithmetic.

## Areas and products

::::exercise{#ex-matrices-and-determinants-ch-triangle}

Find the area of the triangle with corners $$(1, 2)$$, $$(4, 3)$$ and $$(2, 6)$$.

:::solution

Slide the first corner to $$O$$: the other two become $$(3, 1)$$ and $$(1, 4)$$, and sliding keeps
area. The triangle is half of the parallelogram on those two points, whose area is
$$\lvert \det \rvert$$ of the matrix with those columns (:ref{#signed-area}):

```math
\begin{vmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 4 \end{vmatrix} = 12 - 1 = 11
```

So the triangle has area $$\frac{11}{2} = 5.5$$. The determinant is positive, so turning from
$$(3, 1)$$ to $$(1, 4)$$ is anticlockwise.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-matrices-and-determinants-ch-product}

Let $$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$$ and
$$B = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$$. Work out $$AB$$, and check that
$$\det(AB) = \det A \det B$$. Say what this means for areas.

:::solution

By :ref{#matrix-product}, row times column:

```math
AB = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot 1 & 1 \cdot 0 + 2 \cdot 3 \\ 3 \cdot 2 + 4 \cdot 1 & 3 \cdot 0 + 4 \cdot 3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 6 \\ 10 & 12 \end{pmatrix}
```

$$\det(AB) = 48 - 60 = -12$$, while $$\det A = 4 - 6 = -2$$ and $$\det B = 6 - 0 = 6$$, and
$$(-2) \cdot 6 = -12$$ (:ref{#det2-product}). The mapping for $$B$$ multiplies areas by $$6$$, then
the mapping for $$A$$ multiplies them by $$2$$ and flips the plane, so doing both multiplies areas
by $$12$$ with one flip.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-matrices-and-determinants-ch-undo-itself}

A 3 by 3 matrix $$A$$ has $$A^2 = I$$: doing its mapping twice puts every point back. Prove that
$$\det A$$ is $$1$$ or $$-1$$, and give an example of each.

:::solution

By :ref{#det3-product}, $$(\det A)^2 = \det(AA) = \det I = 1$$, using D3 of :ref{#det3-properties}
for $$\det I = 1$$. A number whose square is $$1$$ is $$1$$ or $$-1$$. The identity itself has
$$I^2 = I$$ and determinant $$1$$. The mirror in the floor, with rows $$(1, 0, 0)$$, $$(0, 1, 0)$$
and $$(0, 0, -1)$$, also undoes itself, and it is triangular with determinant
$$1 \cdot 1 \cdot (-1) = -1$$ (:ref{#det3-triangular}). A mapping that undoes itself can't change
volumes, but it may flip.

:::

## Three by three

::::exercise{#ex-matrices-and-determinants-ch-line}

Show that the points $$(x, y)$$ for which

```math
\begin{vmatrix} x & y & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 3 & 5 & 1 \end{vmatrix} = 0
```

form the line through $$(1, 2)$$ and $$(3, 5)$$.

:::solution

Expand along the first row (:ref{#det3}):

```math
x(2 - 5) - y(1 - 3) + 1(5 - 6) = -3x + 2y - 1
```

So the condition is $$-3x + 2y - 1 = 0$$, the equation of a line. Putting in $$(1, 2)$$ gives
$$-3 + 4 - 1 = 0$$, and $$(3, 5)$$ gives $$-9 + 10 - 1 = 0$$, so it is the line through both points.
There is a reason to expect it without expanding: at $$(x, y) = (1, 2)$$ the first two rows are
equal, and at $$(3, 5)$$ the first and third are, so the determinant is $$0$$ at both points (D4 for
rows, :ref{#det3-properties} with :ref{#det3-transpose}).

:::

::::exercise{#ex-matrices-and-determinants-ch-cramer}

Solve by Cramer's rule, and check your answer:

```math
\begin{aligned}
x + 2y + z &= 8 \\
2x - y + 3z &= 9 \\
3x + y - z &= 2
\end{aligned}
```

:::solution

The determinant of the coefficients, expanded along the first row (:ref{#det3}):

```math
\begin{vmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 2 & -1 & 3 \\ 3 & 1 & -1 \end{vmatrix} = 1(1 - 3) - 2(-2 - 9) + 1(2 + 3) = -2 + 22 + 5 = 25
```

It isn't $$0$$, so there is exactly one solution (:ref{#cramer}). Putting the right-hand column
$$(8, 9, 2)$$ in place of each column in turn gives the numerators $$25$$, $$50$$ and $$75$$, so
$$x = 1$$, $$y = 2$$ and $$z = 3$$. Check: $$1 + 4 + 3 = 8$$, $$2 - 2 + 9 = 9$$ and
$$3 + 2 - 3 = 2$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-matrices-and-determinants-ch-parameter}

Find the numbers $$k$$ for which the system

```math
\begin{aligned}
kx + y + z &= 1 \\
x + ky + z &= 1 \\
x + y + kz &= 1
\end{aligned}
```

has exactly one solution, and say what happens for the other values.

:::solution

The determinant of the coefficients is

```math
\begin{vmatrix} k & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & k & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & k \end{vmatrix} = k(k^2 - 1) - (k - 1) + (1 - k) = k^3 - 3k + 2 = (k - 1)^2(k + 2)
```

expanding along the first row (:ref{#det3}); check the factoring at $$k = 0$$: both sides are $$2$$.
By :ref{#cramer} there is exactly one solution when $$k \ne 1$$ and $$k \ne -2$$. For the other two
values the determinant is $$0$$, so there is no solution or infinitely many
(:ref{#cramer-converse}):

- $$k = 1$$: all three equations are $$x + y + z = 1$$, which has infinitely many solutions.
- $$k = -2$$: adding the three equations gives $$0 = 3$$, so there is no solution.

:::

## Proofs

::::exercise{#ex-matrices-and-determinants-ch-skew}

A 3 by 3 matrix $$A$$ has $$A^{\mathsf T} = -A$$. Prove that $$\det A = 0$$, and give an example of
such a matrix that isn't all zeros.

:::solution

By :ref{#det3-transpose} and :ref{#properties-scale} with $$c = -1$$,

```math
\det A = \det A^{\mathsf T} = \det(-A) = (-1)^3 \det A = -\det A
```

So $$2\det A = 0$$ and $$\det A = 0$$. An example is the matrix with rows $$(0, 1, 2)$$,
$$(-1, 0, 3)$$ and $$(-2, -3, 0)$$; expanding along the first row gives
$$0 - 1(0 + 6) + 2(3 - 0) = 0$$. The same argument fails for 2 by 2 matrices, where
$$\det(-A) = (-1)^2\det A = \det A$$: the matrix with rows $$(0, 1)$$ and $$(-1, 0)$$ has
determinant $$1$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-matrices-and-determinants-ch-flip}

Let $$F$$ be the 3 by 3 matrix with rows $$(1, 0, 0)$$, $$(0, 1, 0)$$ and $$(0, 0, -1)$$, the mirror
in the floor. Find $$\det F$$ and $$\det(FAF)$$ in terms of $$\det A$$, and explain the answer with
volumes.

:::solution

$$F$$ is triangular, so $$\det F = 1 \cdot 1 \cdot (-1) = -1$$ (:ref{#det3-triangular}). By
:ref{#det3-product} twice, $$\det(FAF) = (-1)\det A\,(-1) = \det A$$. The two reflections keep every
volume and flip twice between them, so the change of volume and the orientation are the same as for
$$A$$ (:ref{#signed-volume}).

:::
