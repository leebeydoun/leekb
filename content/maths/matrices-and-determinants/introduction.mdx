  Matrices as grids of numbers and as moves of the plane and space, determinants as signed areas and

Picture three shopping receipts from the same bakery, each listing how many croissants, muffins and
cookies were bought and the total paid, with the prices smudged out. Three receipts give three
equations in three unknown prices, and laid out neatly the numbers form a grid: a **matrix**. One
number worked out from that grid, its **determinant**, tells you at once whether the prices can be
recovered, and a formula built from determinants gives them. In this chapter we'll learn to work
with matrices, see what a determinant measures, and solve three equations in three unknowns with it.

One idea carries the chapter: a matrix moves the unit square to a parallelogram, or the unit cube to
a slanted box, and the determinant is the area or volume of what you get, with a sign that says
whether the picture was flipped. Its rules are the rules you would expect of an area, and the
formulas are how you calculate the picture.

## What this chapter builds on

[Linear equations](../linear-equations/special-cases.mdx) met the 2 by 2 determinant and used it to
decide when two equations have one solution, and [Trigonometry](../trigonometry/rotations.mdx) met
matrices and how to multiply them. This chapter takes both further. Areas and volumes come from
[Area and scaling](../area-and-scaling/introduction.mdx), and a matrix acting on points is a mapping
in the sense of [Mappings and permutations](../mappings-and-permutations/mappings.mdx).

One fact is taken as given: sliding the layers of a box sideways, as you might push a stack of
cards, doesn't change its volume (:ref{#properties-shear}). Two smaller points are taken from the
picture and said so where they come up: that a parallelogram's diagonal splits it into two
triangles, and which way round a box is when its determinant is positive. The step from
parallelograms and boxes to every other shape, by covering it with a fine grid, is described but not
written out. Everything else is proved.

Along the way the chapter keeps two promises from earlier chapters:

- **When two equations have one solution**:
  [Linear equations](../linear-equations/special-cases.mdx) showed that a non-zero determinant gives
  exactly one solution. Here the determinant gets its meaning, an area (:ref{#signed-area}), and the
  same test is proved for three equations (:ref{#cramer}, :ref{#cramer-converse}).
- **Matrices**: [Trigonometry](../trigonometry/rotations.mdx) multiplied 2 by 2 matrices to combine
  turns. Here matrices of any size are added, multiplied and transposed (:ref{#matrix-product}).

## Guides in this chapter

1. [Matrices](./matrices.mdx): rows, columns and entries, a matrix times a column, products, and a

**Definitions**

| Term                          | Meaning                                                                          |
| ----------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| :ref{#matrix-size}            | An $$m$$ by $$n$$ grid of numbers, with $$a_{ij}$$ in row $$i$$ and column $$j$$ |
| :ref{#matrix-operations}      | Add and scale entry by entry                                                     |
| :ref{#transpose}              | $$A^{\mathsf T}$$ has the rows of $$A$$ as its columns                           |
| :ref{#matrix-times-column}    | Entry $$i$$ of $$AX$$ is $$a_{i1}x_1 + \cdots + a_{in}x_n$$                      |
| :ref{#matrix-product}         | Column $$j$$ of $$AB$$ is $$A$$ times column $$j$$ of $$B$$                      |
| :ref{#matrices-parallelogram} | The points $$sP + tQ$$ with $$0 \le s, t \le 1$$                                 |
| :ref{#det3}                   | $$\det A = a_{11}\det M_{11} - a_{12}\det M_{12} + a_{13}\det M_{13}$$           |
| :ref{#properties-box}         | The points $$sP + tQ + uR$$ with $$0 \le s, t, u \le 1$$                         |
| :ref{#cramers-rule-cofactors} | $$C_{ij} = (-1)^{i + j}\det M_{ij}$$                                             |

**Taken as given**

| Rule                    | Statement                                                                                       |
| ----------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| :ref{#properties-shear} | The box on $$P$$, $$Q$$ and $$R + cP$$ has the same volume as the box on $$P$$, $$Q$$ and $$R$$ |

**Matrices**

| Result                             | Statement                                                                     |
| ---------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| :ref{#transpose-rules}             | $$(A^{\mathsf T})^{\mathsf T} = A$$, and transposing keeps sums and multiples |
| :ref{#matrices-column-combination} | $$AX = x_1A^1 + \cdots + x_nA^n$$                                             |
| :ref{#columns-images}              | $$AE^j = A^j$$: the columns are where the unit columns go                     |
| :ref{#matrix-product-rules}        | $$(AB)C = A(BC)$$, $$AI = A = IA$$, and products distribute over sums         |
| :ref{#matrices-unit-square}        | $$X \mapsto AX$$ sends the unit square to the parallelogram on the columns    |

**Determinants of order 2**

| Result                 | Statement                                                                                          |
| ---------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| :ref{#signed-area}     | The parallelogram on the columns has area $$\lvert \det A \rvert$$; the sign gives the orientation |
| :ref{#det2-properties} | D1 to D6: adding and scaling a column, $$D(E^1, E^2) = 1$$, equal columns, shears, swaps           |
| :ref{#det2-transpose}  | $$\det A^{\mathsf T} = \det A$$                                                                    |
| :ref{#det2-product}    | $$\det(AB) = \det A \det B$$                                                                       |
| :ref{#det2-inverse}    | $$A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det A}\begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix}$$ when $$\det A \ne 0$$  |
| :ref{#cramer-two}      | $$x = \frac{D(B, A^2)}{D(A^1, A^2)}$$ and $$y = \frac{D(A^1, B)}{D(A^1, A^2)}$$                    |

**Determinants of order 3 and Cramer's rule**

| Result                            | Statement                                                                              |
| --------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| :ref{#det3-any-row}               | Expanding along any row or column gives the same number                                |
| :ref{#sarrus}                     | Three down-diagonals minus three up-diagonals, for 3 by 3 only                         |
| :ref{#det3-transpose}             | $$\det A^{\mathsf T} = \det A$$                                                        |
| :ref{#det3-triangular}            | A triangular matrix has determinant $$a_{11}a_{22}a_{33}$$                             |
| :ref{#det3-properties}            | D1 to D6 for three columns, and so for rows                                            |
| :ref{#properties-zeros}           | Make zeros in a column with D5, then expand along it                                   |
| :ref{#properties-scale}           | $$\det(cA) = c^3\det A$$                                                               |
| :ref{#signed-volume}              | The box on the columns has volume $$\lvert \det A \rvert$$                             |
| :ref{#det3-zero}                  | $$\det A = 0$$ exactly when a column is a combination of the others                    |
| :ref{#det3-product}               | $$\det(AB) = \det A \det B$$                                                           |
| :ref{#cramers-rule-cofactor-rows} | A row times its own cofactors gives $$\det A$$; times another row's, $$0$$             |
| :ref{#cramer}                     | If $$\det A \ne 0$$, $$AX = B$$ has exactly one solution, $$x_j = \frac{D_j}{\det A}$$ |
| :ref{#det3-inverse}               | $$A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det A}C^{\mathsf T}$$                                             |
| :ref{#cramer-converse}            | If $$\det A = 0$$, $$AX = B$$ has no solution or infinitely many                       |
