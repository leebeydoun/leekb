Imagine sending a photo to a printer that has gone a little wrong. In the first print, every row of
the picture has slid sideways, a little more the higher up it is, so the rectangle comes out leaning
like a parallelogram. It looks different, but it covers the same amount of paper and uses the same
amount of ink. In the second print the picture is stretched to twice its width, and now every part
of it takes twice the ink.

<PhotoPrints caption="One photo printed three ways. Slanting it keeps the area of every part, and stretching it to twice the width doubles the area of every part." />

Both printers multiply the area of every shape by the same number: 1 for the slanted print and 2 for
the stretched one. For a printer that moves the picture by a $$2$$ by $$2$$ matrix, that number is
the **determinant** of the matrix, with a minus sign when the picture comes out flipped over. In
this guide we'll see why the determinant measures area, and use the picture to explain each of its
rules.

## The determinant of a 2 by 2 matrix

You met the determinant in [No solution, or infinitely many](../linear-equations/special-cases.mdx)
as a number worked out from the coefficients of two equations (:ref{#linear-equations/determinant}).
Written for a matrix, it is top-left times bottom-right, minus top-right times bottom-left:

```math
\det A = \begin{vmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{vmatrix} = ad - bc \qquad\text{for}\qquad A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}
```

The vertical bars are another way of writing $$\det A$$, read "the determinant of A". For example,

```math
\begin{vmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{vmatrix} = 3 \cdot 2 - 1 \cdot 1 = 5, \qquad \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 1 \end{vmatrix} = 1 \cdot 1 - 3 \cdot 2 = -5
```

There, the determinant decided whether two equations have exactly one solution: they do exactly when
it isn't zero (:ref{#linear-equations/determinant-test}). Here we'll think of it through the columns
$$A^1 = (a, c)$$ and $$A^2 = (b, d)$$, the points where the matrix sends $$E^1$$ and $$E^2$$
(:ref{#columns-images}). When we want to stress the columns we write the determinant as
$$D(A^1, A^2)$$, so that

```math
D\left(\begin{pmatrix} a \\ c \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} b \\ d \end{pmatrix}\right) = ad - bc
```

## The determinant is a signed area

The matrix $$A$$ sends the unit square, which has area $$1$$, to the parallelogram on its columns
(:ref{#matrices-unit-square}). So the area of that parallelogram is the number the area of the
square is multiplied by. The theorem below says that this area is the size of the determinant, and
the sign of the determinant says which way round the two columns are.

Before the proof, here is the area worked out by hand for the columns $$(3, 1)$$ and $$(1, 2)$$. The
parallelogram sits inside the rectangle from $$(0, 0)$$ to $$(4, 3)$$, which has area $$12$$. The
rest of the rectangle is four right triangles and two unit squares, with areas $$\frac{3}{2}$$,
$$1$$, $$1$$, $$\frac{3}{2}$$, $$1$$ and $$1$$ (:ref{#distance-and-angles/right-triangle-area}).
Those add up to $$7$$, so the parallelogram has area $$12 - 7 = 5$$, which is the determinant.

<BoxAndSubtract caption="The parallelogram on A¹ = (3, 1) and A² = (1, 2) inside the 4 by 3 rectangle. The grey pieces add up to 7, so the parallelogram has area 12 − 7 = 5." />

Cutting up a rectangle works well for numbers, but the pieces change shape from one example to the
next. The proof uses a turn instead. Turning the plane about $$O$$ keeps every area, and a
well-chosen turn lays $$A^1$$ along the $$x$$-axis, where the area is a base times a height.

The proof leans on the turns and the sine from [Trigonometry](../trigonometry/rotations.mdx). If
those still feel shaky, read the worked numbers after the proof first: they show the same idea for
the columns $$(3, 1)$$ and $$(1, 2)$$, and the details can wait.

:::theorem[The determinant is a signed area]{#signed-area}

Let $$A$$ be a $$2$$ by $$2$$ matrix with columns $$A^1$$ and $$A^2$$.

1. The parallelogram on $$A^1$$ and $$A^2$$ has area $$|\det A|$$.
2. $$\det A = 0$$ exactly when $$O$$, $$A^1$$ and $$A^2$$ lie on one line.
3. Otherwise, $$\det A > 0$$ when the shorter turn from the direction of $$A^1$$ to the direction of
   $$A^2$$ is anticlockwise, and $$\det A < 0$$ when it is clockwise.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Determinant#Parallelogram_area"}

Write $$A^1 = (a, c)$$ and $$A^2 = (b, d)$$, so that $$\det A = ad - bc$$.

**When $$A^1 = O$$.** Then $$a = c = 0$$, so $$\det A = 0 \cdot d - b \cdot 0 = 0$$. The
parallelogram is the set of points $$sO + tA^2 = tA^2$$ for $$0 \le t \le 1$$
(:ref{#matrices-parallelogram}). That is the segment from $$O$$ to $$A^2$$, or the single point
$$O$$, and it covers no area. The three points $$O$$, $$O$$ and $$A^2$$ lie on one line, so parts 1
and 2 hold, and part 3 has nothing to say.

**Turning $$A^1$$ onto the $$x$$-axis.** From now on $$A^1 \ne O$$. Let $$r = |A^1| > 0$$
(:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/norm}). By :ref{#trigonometry/polar-exist} there is an angle
$$\theta$$ with $$A^1 = (r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta)$$, so

```math
\cos\theta = \frac{a}{r}, \qquad \sin\theta = \frac{c}{r}
```

Let $$G$$ be the turn about $$O$$ by $$-\theta$$. By :ref{#trigonometry/rotation-formula}, with
$$\cos(-\theta) = \cos\theta$$ and $$\sin(-\theta) = -\sin\theta$$
(:ref{#trigonometry/negative-angle}),

```math
G(x, y) = (x\cos\theta + y\sin\theta,\ -x\sin\theta + y\cos\theta) = \left(\frac{ax + cy}{r},\ \frac{ay - cx}{r}\right)
```

Put in the two columns. Since $$a^2 + c^2 = r^2$$,

```math
\begin{aligned}
G(A^1) &= \left(\frac{a^2 + c^2}{r},\ \frac{ac - ca}{r}\right) = (r, 0) \\
G(A^2) &= \left(\frac{ab + cd}{r},\ \frac{ad - bc}{r}\right)
\end{aligned}
```

Write $$G(A^2) = (p, q)$$. The second line says that $$q = \dfrac{\det A}{r}$$, so $$\det A = rq$$.
This is the heart of the proof: after the turn, the determinant is the length of the first side
times the height of the second column above the $$x$$-axis.

**The turn carries the parallelogram along.** The formula for $$G$$ is a $$2$$ by $$2$$ matrix times
$$(x, y)$$ (:ref{#trigonometry/rotation-matrix}). A column is a matrix with one column, so parts 4
and 5 of :ref{#matrix-product-rules} say that a matrix respects adding and scaling columns. So for
all numbers $$s$$ and $$t$$,

```math
G(sA^1 + tA^2) = sG(A^1) + tG(A^2) = s(r, 0) + t(p, q)
```

So $$G$$ sends the parallelogram on $$A^1$$ and $$A^2$$ to the parallelogram on $$(r, 0)$$ and
$$(p, q)$$ (:ref{#matrices-parallelogram}). A turn is an isometry
(:ref{#isometries/basic-isometries}), so the two parallelograms have the same area
(:ref{#isometries/isometry-area}).

**The area of the turned parallelogram.** If $$q = 0$$, all four corners $$O$$, $$(r, 0)$$,
$$(r + p, 0)$$ and $$(p, 0)$$ lie on the $$x$$-axis. The parallelogram is a segment of the axis and
covers no area, and $$|\det A| = r \cdot 0 = 0$$. If $$q \ne 0$$, call the corners $$O$$,
$$R = (r, 0)$$, $$R + Q = (r + p, q)$$ and $$Q = (p, q)$$. The diagonal from $$R$$ to $$Q$$ cuts the
parallelogram into two triangles that overlap only along it, as the second picture under this proof
shows. This is taken from the picture.

- The triangle $$ORQ$$ has base $$OR$$ of length $$r$$ on the $$x$$-axis. Its height is the distance
  from $$Q$$ to the $$x$$-axis, measured to the foot $$(p, 0)$$, which is $$|q|$$. So its area is
  $$\frac{1}{2}r|q|$$ (:ref{#distance-and-angles/triangle-area}).
- The triangle with corners $$R$$, $$R + Q$$ and $$Q$$ has base from $$Q$$ to $$R + Q$$, of length
  $$r$$, on the line $$y = q$$. Its height is the distance from $$R$$ to that line, which is also
  $$|q|$$. So its area is $$\frac{1}{2}r|q|$$ as well.

By rule 2 of :ref{#distance-and-angles/area-rules}, the parallelogram has area
$$\frac{1}{2}r|q| + \frac{1}{2}r|q| = r|q| = |\det A|$$. That proves part 1.

**On one line.** If $$\det A = 0$$, then $$q = 0$$, so $$G(A^2)$$ lies on the $$x$$-axis, the line
through $$O$$ and $$G(A^1)$$. Turning back by $$\theta$$ undoes $$G$$ and sends lines to lines
(:ref{#isometries/isometry-lines}), so $$A^2$$ lies on the line through $$O$$ and $$A^1$$. In the
other direction, if $$A^2$$ lies on the line through $$O$$ and $$A^1$$, then $$G$$ sends it to the
line through $$O$$ and $$(r, 0)$$, the $$x$$-axis. So $$q = 0$$ and $$\det A = 0$$. That proves
part 2.

**The sign.** Suppose $$\det A \ne 0$$, so $$A^2 \ne O$$. By :ref{#trigonometry/polar-exist},
$$A^2 = (s\cos\varphi, s\sin\varphi)$$ with $$s = |A^2| > 0$$. Turning by $$-\theta$$ takes
$$\theta$$ off every angle (:ref{#trigonometry/polar-turn}), so

```math
G(A^2) = \big(s\cos(\varphi - \theta),\ s\sin(\varphi - \theta)\big)
```

Comparing second coordinates, $$q = s\sin(\varphi - \theta)$$, and so

```math
\det A = rq = rs\sin(\varphi - \theta)
```

Since $$r$$ and $$s$$ are positive, $$\det A$$ has the same sign as $$\sin(\varphi - \theta)$$.
Turning anticlockwise from the direction of $$A^1$$ by $$\varphi - \theta$$, or by that plus any
whole number of turns, reaches the direction of $$A^2$$. By :ref{#trigonometry/quadrant-signs}, with
$$\sin\frac{\pi}{2} = 1$$ and $$\sin 0 = \sin\pi = 0$$ (:ref{#trigonometry/quarter-values}), the
sine is positive exactly when that turn can be taken strictly between $$0$$ and $$\pi$$. That means
the shorter turn is anticlockwise. The sine is negative exactly when the turn can be taken strictly
between $$\pi$$ and $$2\pi$$, and then the shorter turn, the other way, is clockwise. That proves
part 3.

:::

<TurnedParallelogram caption="The parallelogram on A¹ = (3, 1) and A² = (1, 2), before and after the turn that lays A¹ along the x-axis at (r, 0), with r = √10. The dashed diagonal cuts it into two triangles, each with base r and height q." />

For the columns $$(3, 1)$$ and $$(1, 2)$$, the turn gives $$r = \sqrt{10}$$ and
$$q = \frac{5}{\sqrt{10}} \approx 1.58$$, as in the second picture. The area is
$$r \cdot q = \sqrt{10} \cdot \frac{5}{\sqrt{10}} = 5$$, matching the rectangle we cut up. The proof
also found a formula worth keeping: $$\det A = |A^1|\,|A^2| \sin(\varphi - \theta)$$, the two
lengths times the sine of the angle from the first column to the second.

### What the sign means

Swap the two columns and the parallelogram doesn't change, but the determinant changes from $$5$$ to
$$-5$$. The sign records the order of the columns. Going from $$(3, 1)$$ to $$(1, 2)$$ is a turn
anticlockwise, and going from $$(1, 2)$$ to $$(3, 1)$$ is the same turn clockwise.

<TwoOrientations caption="With A¹ = (3, 1) and A² = (1, 2), the shorter turn from A¹ to A² is anticlockwise and the determinant is 5. With the same columns in the other order, the turn is clockwise and the determinant is −5." />

In the plane moved by $$A$$, the sign says whether the picture comes out flipped. The unit square
turns anticlockwise from $$E^1$$ to $$E^2$$, and its image turns from $$A^1$$ to $$A^2$$. A negative
determinant means the image turns the other way, so the F inside the square comes out as its mirror
image, as a flip in a line does (:ref{#isometries/reflection}). For example, the flip in the
$$x$$-axis has matrix with rows $$(1, 0)$$ and $$(0, -1)$$, and determinant $$-1$$.

### Every area is multiplied by the same number

The theorem is about the unit square, but the same number works for every shape. A small square with
sides $$h$$ and one corner at $$P$$ goes to a parallelogram on $$hA^1$$ and $$hA^2$$, slid to start
at $$AP$$. The parallelogram on $$hA^1$$ and $$hA^2$$ has determinant
$$(ha)(hd) - (hb)(hc) = h^2(ad - bc)$$, so by the theorem its area is $$h^2|\det A|$$, and a slide
doesn't change area. That is $$|\det A|$$ times the area of the small square. Covering a shape with
a fine grid of squares, as in [Measuring area with a grid](../area-and-scaling/grids.mdx), then
shows that its area is multiplied by $$|\det A|$$ too. We won't write out the grid argument here, so
for shapes other than parallelograms this is taken from the picture.

Two cases from [Area and scaling](../area-and-scaling/introduction.mdx) agree with it. The matrix
with rows $$(k, 0)$$ and $$(0, 1)$$, for $$k > 0$$, stretches the plane by $$k$$ away from the
$$y$$-axis, and its determinant is $$k$$, as :ref{#area-and-scaling/stretch-area} says it should be.
The zoom by $$k$$ has rows $$(k, 0)$$ and $$(0, k)$$, and its determinant is $$k^2$$, as in
:ref{#area-and-scaling/area-scaling}.

Try it: set a matrix, and compare the shaded area with $$ad - bc$$.

## Rules that areas obey

Since the determinant is an area with a sign, it should behave the way areas do. Scaling one side of
a parallelogram scales its area. Sliding the far side along, keeping the base and the height, keeps
its area. A parallelogram with both sides along the same line is flat, with no area. Each of these
becomes a rule about $$D(A^1, A^2)$$, and each one can be checked from $$ad - bc$$ in a line or two.

:::theorem[Properties of 2 by 2 determinants]{#det2-properties}

For all columns $$A^1$$, $$A^2$$, $$B$$ and $$C$$ with two entries, and every number $$t$$:

1. **D1, adding in one column**: $$D(B + C, A^2) = D(B, A^2) + D(C, A^2)$$, and
   $$D(A^1, B + C) = D(A^1, B) + D(A^1, C)$$
2. **D2, scaling a column**: $$D(tA^1, A^2) = t\,D(A^1, A^2)$$, and
   $$D(A^1, tA^2) = t\,D(A^1, A^2)$$
3. **D3, the unit columns**: $$D(E^1, E^2) = 1$$
4. **D4, equal columns**: $$D(A^1, A^1) = 0$$
5. **D5, adding a multiple of the other column**: $$D(A^1 + tA^2, A^2) = D(A^1, A^2)$$, and
   $$D(A^1, A^2 + tA^1) = D(A^1, A^2)$$
6. **D6, swapping the columns**: $$D(A^2, A^1) = -D(A^1, A^2)$$

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Determinant#Two_by_two_matrices"}

Write $$A^1 = (a, c)$$, $$A^2 = (b, d)$$, $$B = (u, v)$$ and $$C = (w, z)$$. Each rule comes from
the formula $$D = ad - bc$$, top of the first column times bottom of the second, minus top of the
second times bottom of the first.

**D1.** $$B + C = (u + w, v + z)$$, so

```math
D(B + C, A^2) = (u + w)d - b(v + z) = (ud - bv) + (wd - bz) = D(B, A^2) + D(C, A^2)
```

The second column works the same way: $$a(v + z) - (u + w)c = (av - uc) + (az - wc)$$.

**D2.** $$D(tA^1, A^2) = (ta)d - b(tc) = t(ad - bc)$$, and
$$D(A^1, tA^2) = a(td) - (tb)c = t(ad - bc)$$.

**D3.** $$E^1 = (1, 0)$$ and $$E^2 = (0, 1)$$, so $$D(E^1, E^2) = 1 \cdot 1 - 0 \cdot 0 = 1$$.

**D4.** $$D(A^1, A^1) = ac - ac = 0$$.

**D6.** $$D(A^2, A^1) = bc - ad = -(ad - bc)$$.

**D5.** By D1, then D2, then D4,

```math
D(A^1 + tA^2, A^2) = D(A^1, A^2) + D(tA^2, A^2) = D(A^1, A^2) + t\,D(A^2, A^2) = D(A^1, A^2) + 0
```

The second column works the same way, using the second halves of D1 and D2.

:::

Each rule has a picture, and you've seen most of them already:

- **D1** says areas add. Put the parallelogram on $$B$$ and $$A^2$$ next to the one on $$C$$ and
  $$A^2$$, slid along by $$B$$. Together they cover the parallelogram on $$B + C$$ and $$A^2$$,
  except that a triangle at one end is swapped for an equal triangle at the other. This picture fits
  the case where all three determinants are positive, and the signs take care of the rest.
- **D2** is stretching one side. Making one side $$t$$ times as long multiplies the area by $$t$$,
  and a negative $$t$$ turns the side round and changes the sign.
- **D3** is the unit square, with area $$1$$ and its sides in the anticlockwise order.
- **D4** is a parallelogram squashed flat onto a line, with no area.
- **D5** is a shear, the slanted print from the start of this guide. The far side slides along a
  line parallel to the base, so the base and the height stay the same.
- **D6** is a flip: the same parallelogram with its sides in the other order.

<ShearKeepsArea caption="With A¹ = (3, 1) and A² = (1, 2): the blue parallelogram on A¹ and A², and the orange one on A¹ and A² + A¹. Both stand on the side OA¹ and reach the same dashed line, so they have the same height and both have area 5." />

In numbers, $$D((3, 1), (1, 2)) = 5$$. Adding the first column to the second gives the columns
$$(3, 1)$$ and $$(4, 3)$$, and $$D((3, 1), (4, 3)) = 3 \cdot 3 - 4 \cdot 1 = 5$$, as D5 says.

## Rows behave like columns

Everything so far has been about the columns. Turning the matrix on its side swaps rows and columns,
and the formula $$ad - bc$$ doesn't notice.

:::proposition[The transpose has the same determinant]{#det2-transpose}

For every $$2$$ by $$2$$ matrix $$A$$, $$\det A^{\mathsf T} = \det A$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Determinant_of_Transpose"}

If $$A$$ has rows $$(a, b)$$ and $$(c, d)$$, then $$A^{\mathsf T}$$ has rows $$(a, c)$$ and
$$(b, d)$$ (:ref{#transpose}). So $$\det A^{\mathsf T} = ad - cb = ad - bc = \det A$$.

:::

The rows of $$A$$ are the columns of $$A^{\mathsf T}$$. So each of the rules D1 to D6 for the
columns of $$A^{\mathsf T}$$ is the same rule for the rows of $$A$$. For example, swapping the two
rows changes the sign, and adding a multiple of one row to the other changes nothing. So the step of
elimination that adds a multiple of one equation to the other leaves the determinant of the system
as it was.

## The determinant of a product

Moving the plane by $$B$$ multiplies every area by $$|\det B|$$. Moving the result by $$A$$
multiplies every area again, by $$|\det A|$$. Doing one after the other is moving by $$AB$$
(:ref{#matrix-product-rules}), so the area factor of $$AB$$ should be the product of the two. The
signs follow the same pattern, since two flips make a picture the right way round again.

:::theorem[The determinant of a product]{#det2-product}

For all $$2$$ by $$2$$ matrices $$A$$ and $$B$$,

```math
\det(AB) = \det A \cdot \det B
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Determinant_of_Matrix_Product"}

Let $$A$$ have rows $$(a, b)$$ and $$(c, d)$$, and $$B$$ rows $$(e, f)$$ and $$(g, h)$$. By
:ref{#matrix-product},

```math
AB = \begin{pmatrix} ae + bg & af + bh \\ ce + dg & cf + dh \end{pmatrix}
```

Multiply out the determinant, one product at a time:

```math
\begin{aligned}
\det(AB) &= (ae + bg)(cf + dh) - (af + bh)(ce + dg) \\
&= (acef + adeh + bcfg + bdgh) - (acef + adfg + bceh + bdgh) \\
&= adeh + bcfg - adfg - bceh \\
&= ad(eh - fg) - bc(eh - fg) \\
&= (ad - bc)(eh - fg)
\end{aligned}
```

The third line cancels $$acef$$ and $$bdgh$$, which appear in both brackets. The last line is
$$\det A \cdot \det B$$.

:::

For example, take

```math
A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad AB = \begin{pmatrix} 7 & -1 \\ 4 & -2 \end{pmatrix}
```

Here $$\det A = 5$$ and $$\det B = -2$$, and $$\det(AB) = 7 \cdot (-2) - (-1) \cdot 4 = -10$$, which
is $$5 \cdot (-2)$$. In pictures, $$B$$ doubles every area and flips it over, then $$A$$ multiplies
the area by $$5$$, so the total is $$10$$ times the area, flipped.

Adding matrices is a different story, and a slip to watch for: $$\det(A + B)$$ need not be
$$\det A + \det B$$. With $$A = B = I$$, $$\det(I + I) = \det(2I) = 2 \cdot 2 = 4$$, while
$$\det I + \det I = 2$$. The same example shows a second slip: $$\det(cA) = c^2\det A$$, not
$$c\det A$$, because scaling the matrix by $$c$$ scales both columns, and each one multiplies the
area by $$c$$ (D2).

### The inverse matrix

A matrix $$B$$ with $$AB = I$$ and $$BA = I$$ undoes $$A$$, and it is called an **inverse** of
$$A$$. There can be only one: if $$C$$ is another, then $$B = BI = B(AC) = (BA)C = IC = C$$, using
:ref{#matrix-product-rules}. So we call it the inverse and write it $$A^{-1}$$. The determinant says
exactly when it exists.

:::corollary[Inverse of a 2 by 2 matrix]{#det2-inverse}

A $$2$$ by $$2$$ matrix $$A$$ with rows $$(a, b)$$ and $$(c, d)$$ has an inverse exactly when
$$\det A \ne 0$$, and then

```math
A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det A}\begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix}
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://www.mathsisfun.com/algebra/matrix-inverse.html"}

**With a zero determinant, there is no inverse.** If $$AB = I$$, then by :ref{#det2-product},

```math
\det A \cdot \det B = \det(AB) = \det I = 1 \cdot 1 - 0 \cdot 0 = 1
```

so $$\det A \ne 0$$. Turned round, if $$\det A = 0$$ then no matrix $$B$$ has $$AB = I$$.

**With a non-zero determinant, the formula works.** Write $$C$$ for the matrix with rows $$(d, -b)$$
and $$(-c, a)$$. Multiplying out (:ref{#matrix-product}),

```math
AC = \begin{pmatrix} ad - bc & -ab + ba \\ cd - dc & -cb + da \end{pmatrix} = (\det A)\,I, \qquad CA = \begin{pmatrix} da - bc & db - bd \\ -ca + ac & -cb + ad \end{pmatrix} = (\det A)\,I
```

So by part 5 of :ref{#matrix-product-rules},
$$A\left(\frac{1}{\det A}C\right) = \frac{1}{\det A}(\det A)I = I$$, and the same holds in the other
order.

:::

To find the inverse, swap the two entries on the diagonal, change the sign of the other two, and
divide by the determinant. For the matrix with rows $$(3, 1)$$ and $$(1, 2)$$, whose determinant is
$$5$$,

```math
A^{-1} = \frac{1}{5}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad AA^{-1} = \frac{1}{5}\begin{pmatrix} 6 - 1 & -3 + 3 \\ 2 - 2 & -1 + 6 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{5}\begin{pmatrix} 5 & 0 \\ 0 & 5 \end{pmatrix} = I
```

In pictures, when $$\det A \ne 0$$ the mapping $$X \mapsto A^{-1}X$$ undoes $$X \mapsto AX$$, so by
:ref{#mappings-and-permutations/inverse-exists-bijective} the mapping $$X \mapsto AX$$ is one to one
and onto. When $$\det A = 0$$, the columns lie on one line through $$O$$ (:ref{#signed-area}), so
every point $$xA^1 + yA^2$$ lands on that line too. The whole plane is squashed onto a line, or onto
the point $$O$$, and no mapping can spread it out again.

## Cramer's rule for two unknowns

A system of two equations in two unknowns is one equation $$AX = B$$, and by
:ref{#matrices-column-combination} it says $$B = xA^1 + yA^2$$. Put $$B$$ in place of the first
column, and the rules D1, D2 and D4 strip away the second column, leaving $$x$$ times the
determinant. In pictures, the parallelogram on $$B$$ and $$A^2$$ is a shear of the one on $$xA^1$$
and $$A^2$$, so it has $$x$$ times the area of the parallelogram on the columns.

:::proposition[Cramer's rule for two unknowns]{#cramer-two}

Let $$A$$ be a $$2$$ by $$2$$ matrix with $$\det A \ne 0$$, and $$B$$ a column with two entries. The
system $$AX = B$$ has exactly one solution, with entries

```math
x = \frac{D(B, A^2)}{D(A^1, A^2)}, \qquad y = \frac{D(A^1, B)}{D(A^1, A^2)}
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cramer%27s_rule#A_short_proof"}

**A solution must be this one.** Suppose $$X$$, with entries $$x$$ and $$y$$, solves the system. By
:ref{#matrices-column-combination}, $$B = AX = xA^1 + yA^2$$. Then

```math
\begin{aligned}
D(B, A^2) &= D(xA^1 + yA^2, A^2) \\
&= D(xA^1, A^2) + D(yA^2, A^2) && \text{D1} \\
&= x\,D(A^1, A^2) + y\,D(A^2, A^2) && \text{D2} \\
&= x\,D(A^1, A^2) && \text{D4}
\end{aligned}
```

Dividing by $$D(A^1, A^2) = \det A \ne 0$$ gives the formula for $$x$$. The same steps in the second
column give $$D(A^1, B) = y\,D(A^1, A^2)$$, and the formula for $$y$$. These rules are
:ref{#det2-properties}.

**This one is a solution.** Write $$B = (u, v)$$ and let $$A$$ have rows $$(a, b)$$ and $$(c, d)$$.
Then $$D(B, A^2) = ud - bv$$ and $$D(A^1, B) = av - uc$$, so the two formulas are
$$x = \frac{du - bv}{ad - bc}$$ and $$y = \frac{av - cu}{ad - bc}$$. These are the numbers that
:ref{#linear-equations/determinant-test} showed do solve the system.

:::

For example, take $$3x + y = 7$$ and $$x + 2y = 9$$. The columns are $$A^1 = (3, 1)$$ and
$$A^2 = (1, 2)$$, with determinant $$5$$, and $$B = (7, 9)$$:

```math
x = \frac{\begin{vmatrix} 7 & 1 \\ 9 & 2 \end{vmatrix}}{5} = \frac{14 - 9}{5} = 1, \qquad y = \frac{\begin{vmatrix} 3 & 7 \\ 1 & 9 \end{vmatrix}}{5} = \frac{27 - 7}{5} = 4
```

As a check, $$3 \cdot 1 + 4 = 7$$ and $$1 + 2 \cdot 4 = 9$$. The rule needs $$\det A \ne 0$$: with a
zero determinant there is nothing to divide by, and the system has no solution or infinitely many
(:ref{#linear-equations/determinant-zero}). The same idea, with three columns, is
[Cramer's rule](./cramers-rule.mdx) for three unknowns.

::::exercise{#ex-order-two-compute}

Work out the determinant of each matrix. Say which ones flip the plane over, and which ones squash
it onto a line.

```math
\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & -1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -6 \\ -1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}
```

:::solution

- $$4 \cdot 3 - 1 \cdot 2 = 10$$. It is positive, so the plane is not flipped, and every area is
  multiplied by $$10$$.
- $$1 \cdot (-1) - 3 \cdot 2 = -7$$. It is negative, so the plane is flipped over, and every area is
  multiplied by $$7$$.
- $$2 \cdot 3 - (-6) \cdot (-1) = 6 - 6 = 0$$. The plane is squashed onto a line. The second column
  $$(-6, 3)$$ is $$-3$$ times the first column $$(2, -1)$$, so both lie on one line through $$O$$
  (:ref{#signed-area}).

:::

::::exercise{#ex-order-two-parallelogram}

Find the area of the parallelogram with corners $$O$$, $$(4, 1)$$, $$(5, 4)$$ and $$(1, 3)$$, and
the area of the triangle with corners $$O$$, $$(4, 1)$$ and $$(1, 3)$$.

:::solution

The corners are $$O$$, $$P$$, $$P + Q$$ and $$Q$$ with $$P = (4, 1)$$ and $$Q = (1, 3)$$, since
$$(4, 1) + (1, 3) = (5, 4)$$. So this is the parallelogram on $$P$$ and $$Q$$, and by
:ref{#signed-area} its area is

```math
\left|\begin{vmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{vmatrix}\right| = |12 - 1| = 11
```

The diagonal from $$P$$ to $$Q$$ cuts the parallelogram into two triangles of equal area, as in the
proof of :ref{#signed-area}, and one of them is the triangle with corners $$O$$, $$P$$ and $$Q$$. So
the triangle has area $$\frac{11}{2}$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-order-two-triangle}

Find the area of the triangle with corners $$P = (1, 1)$$, $$Q = (5, 2)$$ and $$R = (2, 4)$$, by
first sliding it so that $$P$$ moves to $$O$$.

:::solution

The slide by $$-P$$ sends each point $$X$$ to $$X - P$$. It is an isometry
(:ref{#isometries/basic-isometries}), so it keeps area (:ref{#isometries/isometry-area}). It sends
the corners to

```math
P - P = O, \qquad Q - P = (4, 1), \qquad R - P = (1, 3)
```

That is the triangle of the exercise on the parallelogram, with area $$\frac{11}{2}$$. So the
triangle $$PQR$$ has area $$\frac{11}{2}$$ too.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-order-two-scaling}

Show that $$\det(cA) = c^2\det A$$ for every $$2$$ by $$2$$ matrix $$A$$ and every number $$c$$, in
two ways: from the rules D1 to D6, and from the formula. Check it with $$c = 3$$ and the matrix with
rows $$(3, 1)$$ and $$(1, 2)$$.

:::solution

**From the rules.** The columns of $$cA$$ are $$cA^1$$ and $$cA^2$$ (:ref{#matrix-operations}).
Using D2 once for each column (:ref{#det2-properties}),

```math
D(cA^1, cA^2) = c\,D(A^1, cA^2) = c \cdot c\,D(A^1, A^2) = c^2\det A
```

**From the formula.** To keep the letter $$c$$ for the number, let $$A$$ have rows $$(a, b)$$ and
$$(e, f)$$. Then $$cA$$ has rows $$(ca, cb)$$ and $$(ce, cf)$$, so
$$\det(cA) = (ca)(cf) - (cb)(ce) = c^2(af - be) = c^2\det A$$.

**The check.** $$3A$$ has rows $$(9, 3)$$ and $$(3, 6)$$, so $$\det(3A) = 54 - 9 = 45$$, and
$$3^2 \cdot 5 = 45$$. In the picture, zooming by $$3$$ makes both sides of the parallelogram $$3$$
times as long, so the area is $$9$$ times as big, not $$3$$ times.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-order-two-flat}

Find every number $$t$$ for which the matrix with rows $$(t, 2)$$ and $$(3, t + 1)$$ has determinant
$$0$$. For each one, check that the two columns lie on one line through $$O$$.

:::solution

The determinant is $$t(t + 1) - 2 \cdot 3 = t^2 + t - 6 = (t + 3)(t - 2)$$. A product is zero
exactly when one of its factors is (:ref{#numbers/zero-product}), so $$t = -3$$ or $$t = 2$$.

- **$$t = 2$$:** the columns are $$(2, 3)$$ and $$(2, 3)$$, the same point, so they lie on the line
  through $$O$$ and $$(2, 3)$$.
- **$$t = -3$$:** the columns are $$(-3, 3)$$ and $$(2, -2)$$. The second is $$-\frac{2}{3}$$ times
  the first, so both lie on the line through $$O$$ and $$(-1, 1)$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-order-two-inverse}

Find the inverse of the matrix with rows $$(4, 3)$$ and $$(1, 1)$$, and use it to solve
$$4x + 3y = 5$$, $$x + y = 2$$.

:::solution

The determinant is $$4 \cdot 1 - 3 \cdot 1 = 1$$. By :ref{#det2-inverse}, swap the diagonal entries,
change the sign of the other two, and divide by $$1$$:

```math
A^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -3 \\ -1 & 4 \end{pmatrix}
```

The system is $$AX = B$$ with $$B = (5, 2)$$. Multiplying both sides by $$A^{-1}$$ on the left, and
using :ref{#matrix-product-rules}, gives $$X = A^{-1}AX = A^{-1}B$$:

```math
X = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -3 \\ -1 & 4 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 - 6 \\ -5 + 8 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}
```

So $$x = -1$$ and $$y = 3$$. **Check:** $$-4 + 9 = 5$$ and $$-1 + 3 = 2$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-order-two-turns}

Show that the matrix of every turn about $$O$$ (:ref{#trigonometry/rotation-matrix}) has determinant
$$1$$, and say what that means in the picture. Then show that $$\det(A^{-1}) = \dfrac{1}{\det A}$$
whenever $$\det A \ne 0$$.

:::solution

The turn by $$\varphi$$ has rows $$(\cos\varphi, -\sin\varphi)$$ and $$(\sin\varphi, \cos\varphi)$$,
so its determinant is

```math
\cos\varphi \cdot \cos\varphi - (-\sin\varphi) \cdot \sin\varphi = \cos^2\varphi + \sin^2\varphi = 1
```

by :ref{#trigonometry/pythagorean-identity}. In the picture, a turn keeps every area and never flips
a shape over, so its area factor is $$1$$ with a plus sign.

For the inverse, $$AA^{-1} = I$$, so by :ref{#det2-product},
$$\det A \cdot \det(A^{-1}) = \det I = 1$$. Dividing by $$\det A \ne 0$$ gives
$$\det(A^{-1}) = \frac{1}{\det A}$$. In the picture, undoing a mapping that multiplies areas by
$$k$$ must divide them by $$k$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-order-two-cramer}

Use Cramer's rule to solve $$2x - 3y = -1$$, $$5x + y = 23$$, and check your answer.

:::solution

The columns are $$A^1 = (2, 5)$$ and $$A^2 = (-3, 1)$$, and $$B = (-1, 23)$$. The determinant is
$$2 \cdot 1 - (-3) \cdot 5 = 17$$, which isn't zero, so :ref{#cramer-two} applies:

```math
x = \frac{\begin{vmatrix} -1 & -3 \\ 23 & 1 \end{vmatrix}}{17} = \frac{-1 + 69}{17} = 4, \qquad y = \frac{\begin{vmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 5 & 23 \end{vmatrix}}{17} = \frac{46 + 5}{17} = 3
```

**Check:** $$2 \cdot 4 - 3 \cdot 3 = -1$$ and $$5 \cdot 4 + 3 = 23$$.
