---
title: Combining moves
description: Doing one move after another, why the order matters, and what two mirrors make.
---

Follow a set of driving directions: turn left, then go straight on for a mile. Swap the two steps
and you end up somewhere else. Moves of the tracing paper behave the same way, so the order you do
them in matters. In this guide we'll learn to write one move after another, and find that two flips
in mirrors always combine into a slide or a turn.

## One move after another

Lay the tracing paper over the page and move it twice: first slide it, then turn it about a pin.
Wherever a point of the sheet started, it ends up somewhere after both moves. So the two moves
together are a single move of their own, which sends each point to wherever the second move puts the
point that the first move produced.

:::definition[Composite]{#composite}

Let $$F$$ and $$G$$ be maps of the plane (:ref{#map}). The **composite** "first $$F$$, then $$G$$"
is the map that sends each point $$P$$ to $$G(F(P))$$: first $$F$$ sends $$P$$ to $$F(P)$$, and then
$$G$$ sends that point on. It is written $$G \circ F$$ and read "$$G$$ after $$F$$":

```math
(G \circ F)(P) = G(F(P))
```

:::

The order of the letters looks backwards, since $$F$$ acts first but is written second. The reason
lies in the brackets: in $$G(F(P))$$ the map next to $$P$$ is $$F$$, so $$F$$ is applied first, and
$$G$$ is applied to its result. Reading $$G \circ F$$ from right to left, starting next to the
point, gives the order of the moves.

:::example[A slide after a turn]{#composite-example}

Let $$G_{90}$$ be the quarter turn anticlockwise about a point $$O$$ (:ref{#rotation}), and let
$$T$$ be the slide $$3$$ units to the right (:ref{#translation}). Take a point $$P$$ that is $$2$$
units to the right of $$O$$. Then $$T \circ G_{90}$$, "first turn, then slide", moves $$P$$ in two
steps:

1. The turn sends $$P$$ to the point $$2$$ units straight above $$O$$.
2. The slide sends that point $$3$$ units to the right.

So $$(T \circ G_{90})(P)$$ is $$2$$ units above $$O$$ and $$3$$ units to the right of it.

:::

A composite of moves that keep distances keeps distances too. Each move on its own leaves every
distance as it was, so two of them in a row leave it as it was twice over.

:::proposition[Composites of isometries are isometries]{#composite-isometry}

If $$F$$ and $$G$$ are isometries (:ref{#isometry}), then so is $$G \circ F$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_plane_isometry#Group_structure"}

Take any two points $$P$$ and $$Q$$. Since $$G$$ is an isometry, it keeps the distance between the
two points $$F(P)$$ and $$F(Q)$$. Since $$F$$ is an isometry, it keeps the distance between $$P$$
and $$Q$$. So

```math
d\big(G(F(P)), G(F(Q))\big) = d\big(F(P), F(Q)\big) = d(P, Q)
```

The left side is the distance between $$(G \circ F)(P)$$ and $$(G \circ F)(Q)$$, so $$G \circ F$$
keeps every distance.

:::

With three moves there seem to be two ways to group them: do $$F$$, then the pair "$$G$$ then
$$H$$"; or do the pair "$$F$$ then $$G$$", then $$H$$. Either way the sheet makes the same three
moves in the same order, so the groupings can't differ.

:::proposition[Grouping doesn't matter]{#composite-associative}

For any maps $$F$$, $$G$$ and $$H$$ of the plane,

```math
H \circ (G \circ F) = (H \circ G) \circ F
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Function_composition#Properties"}

Two maps are equal when they send every point to the same place (:ref{#map-equality}). Take any
point $$P$$ and follow it through each side:

```math
\big(H \circ (G \circ F)\big)(P) = H\big((G \circ F)(P)\big) = H(G(F(P)))
```

```math
\big((H \circ G) \circ F\big)(P) = (H \circ G)(F(P)) = H(G(F(P)))
```

Both sides send $$P$$ to $$H(G(F(P)))$$, so they are equal.

:::

So the brackets can be left out, and $$H \circ G \circ F$$ means "first $$F$$, then $$G$$, then
$$H$$", however you group it.

## Order matters

With numbers, $$2 \cdot 3 = 3 \cdot 2$$, so it is tempting to expect $$G \circ F = F \circ G$$ for
moves as well. But for moves, the order can change the result. Think of directions in a city: "turn
left, then walk a block" and "walk a block, then turn left" leave you facing the same way, but on
different streets.

:::example[A turn and a flip in both orders]{#order-matters}

Let $$O$$ be a point, $$L$$ the horizontal line through $$O$$, $$R$$ the flip in $$L$$
(:ref{#reflection}), and $$G_{90}$$ the quarter turn anticlockwise about $$O$$. Take $$P$$ on $$L$$,
$$2$$ units to the right of $$O$$:

- **Turn, then flip**: the turn sends $$P$$ to the point $$2$$ units above $$O$$, and the flip sends
  that to the point $$2$$ units below $$O$$. So $$(R \circ G_{90})(P)$$ is below $$O$$.
- **Flip, then turn**: $$P$$ is on the mirror, so the flip leaves it where it is, and the turn sends
  it to the point $$2$$ units above $$O$$. So $$(G_{90} \circ R)(P)$$ is above $$O$$.

The two composites send $$P$$ to different points, so $$R \circ G_{90} \neq G_{90} \circ R$$.

:::

```plane
point O 0 0 sw
point P 2 0 s
point P' 0 2 w
point P'' 0 -2 w
point E -3 0 hidden
point W 3 0 hidden
line E W color=blue dashed
angle P O P' arrow color=orange r=0.7
segment P' P'' color=muted dashed
text 2.9 0.35 "L"
caption "Turn, then flip: the turn sends P to P′, and the flip sends P′ to P″, below O"
```

The diagram below does the same with a whole flag, so you can see where every part of it goes. The
two orders don't only move the flag to different places; they leave it pointing different ways.

<OrderMatters />

Some pairs of moves do give the same result in either order. Two turns about the same centre are one
such pair: by :ref{#rotation-rules}, turning by $$x^\circ$$ then $$y^\circ$$ is turning by
$$(x + y)^\circ$$, and $$x + y = y + x$$. But it has to be checked each time, never assumed.

## Repeating a move

Some patterns come from doing the same move several times in a row: a wheel turning notch by notch,
a row of tiles slid along a wall, a mirror image of a mirror image. Writing it out as
$$F \circ F \circ F$$ gets long, so it gets a shorter name, written like a power of a number.

:::definition[Powers of a map]{#map-powers}

For a map $$F$$ of the plane, write

```math
F^2 = F \circ F, \qquad F^3 = F \circ F \circ F, \qquad \ldots
```

so that $$F^n$$ means "do $$F$$, $$n$$ times in a row". Doing it no times leaves everything where it
is, so $$F^0 = I$$, the identity map.

:::

Since grouping doesn't matter (:ref{#composite-associative}), doing $$F$$ first $$n$$ times and then
$$m$$ times is doing it $$m + n$$ times. So the rule for powers of numbers carries over:

```math
F^m \circ F^n = F^{m + n}
```

:::example[Powers of turns, slides and flips]{#map-powers-example}

- **A quarter turn**: $$G_{90}^2 = G_{180}$$, a half turn, and $$G_{90}^4 = G_{360} = I$$ by
  :ref{#rotation-rules}. Then $$G_{90}^5 = G_{90}^4 \circ G_{90} = G_{90}$$, so the powers repeat
  every four steps.
- **A slide**: if $$T$$ slides every point $$1$$ unit to the right, then $$T^3$$ slides every point
  $$3$$ units to the right. No power of $$T$$ other than $$T^0$$ is the identity.
- **A flip**: $$R^2 = I$$ by :ref{#reflection-twice}. So $$R^3 = R^2 \circ R = R$$, and in general
  an even power of $$R$$ is $$I$$ and an odd power is $$R$$, as with powers of $$-1$$.

:::

## Two parallel mirrors

Stand between two parallel mirrors, as in some lifts or changing rooms, and you see copies of
yourself lined up into the distance, each a fixed step further away. Each copy is an image of an
image, so it comes from two flips in a row. The copies are spaced evenly in a straight line, which
suggests that two flips in parallel mirrors make a slide. The next theorem says so, and says how
far: twice the gap between the mirrors.

```plane
point L1 0 -1 hidden
point L2 0 3.2 hidden
point K1 2 -1 hidden
point K2 2 3.2 hidden
point P -1 1 s
point P' 1 1 s
point P'' 3 1 s
point Q 0.8 2.4 n
point Q' -0.8 2.4 n
point Q'' 4.8 2.4 n
segment L1 L2 color=blue
segment K1 K2 color=blue
segment P P' color=orange dashed
segment P' P'' color=purple dashed
segment Q Q' color=orange dashed
segment Q' Q'' color=purple dashed
text -0.25 3.4 "L"
text 2.25 3.4 "K"
caption "Mirrors L and K are 2 apart. Flipping in L and then in K moves P and Q both 4 to the right"
```

:::theorem[Two parallel mirrors make a slide]{#parallel-mirrors}

Let $$L$$ and $$K$$ be parallel lines a distance $$d$$ apart
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/parallel-distance}), and let $$R_L$$ and $$R_K$$ be the flips in them.
Then "first flip in $$L$$, then flip in $$K$$", $$R_K \circ R_L$$, is the slide that moves every
point a distance $$2d$$, square to the mirrors, in the direction from $$L$$ towards $$K$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_plane_isometry#Mirror_combinations"}

Take any point $$P$$, and let $$N$$ be the line through $$P$$ perpendicular to $$L$$. It is
perpendicular to $$K$$ as well, by rule 2 of :ref{#distance-and-angles/perpendicular-rules}. Let
$$N$$ meet $$L$$ at $$A$$ and $$K$$ at $$B$$, so that $$d(A, B) = d$$.

**Both flips keep the point on $$N$$.** The mirror $$L$$ is the perpendicular bisector of $$PP'$$,
where $$P' = R_L(P)$$ (:ref{#reflection}). So the line $$PP'$$ is the perpendicular to $$L$$ through
$$P$$, which is $$N$$ (rule 1 of :ref{#distance-and-angles/perpendicular-rules}), and $$L$$ crosses
it at its midpoint, which is $$A$$. In the same way $$P'' = R_K(P')$$ lies on $$N$$, and $$B$$ is
the midpoint of $$P'P''$$. (If $$P$$ is on $$L$$, then $$P' = P$$; the same reasoning applies.)

**Measure along $$N$$ like a ruler.** Give each point of $$N$$ a position: $$0$$ at $$A$$, and
otherwise its distance from $$A$$, counted positive on $$K$$'s side of $$A$$ and negative on the
other side. Then $$B$$ is at $$d$$. Flipping in $$L$$ sends the point at position $$x$$ to the point
the same distance from $$A$$ on the other side, at $$-x$$. Flipping in $$K$$ sends the point at
position $$x$$ to the point the same distance from $$B$$ on the other side, at $$2d - x$$, since the
midpoint of positions $$x$$ and $$2d - x$$ is $$d$$.

**Follow $$P$$.** If $$P$$ is at position $$p$$, then $$P'$$ is at $$-p$$, and $$P''$$ is at

```math
2d - (-p) = p + 2d
```

So $$P''$$ is $$2d$$ further along $$N$$ than $$P$$, in the direction from $$A$$ towards $$B$$, that
is from $$L$$ towards $$K$$. Every line square to the mirrors points the same way, so every point
moves the same distance $$2d$$ in the same direction. That is a slide (:ref{#translation}).

:::

:::example[Mirrors 3 apart]{#parallel-mirrors-example}

Let $$L$$ and $$K$$ be parallel and $$3$$ apart, and measure positions along a line square to them,
with $$L$$ at $$0$$ and $$K$$ at $$3$$:

| Point starts at | After the flip in $$L$$ | After the flip in $$K$$ | Moved by |
| --------------- | ----------------------- | ----------------------- | -------- |
| $$-1$$          | $$1$$                   | $$6 - 1 = 5$$           | $$6$$    |
| $$2$$           | $$-2$$                  | $$6 + 2 = 8$$           | $$6$$    |
| $$7$$           | $$-7$$                  | $$6 + 7 = 13$$          | $$6$$    |

Points behind $$L$$, between the mirrors and beyond $$K$$ all move $$6 = 2 \cdot 3$$ towards $$K$$'s
side.

:::

The order matters here too. Flipping in $$K$$ first and then in $$L$$ is the slide by $$2d$$ in the
opposite direction, from $$K$$ towards $$L$$: the same proof with the roles of the mirrors swapped.
So $$R_L \circ R_K$$ undoes $$R_K \circ R_L$$.

## Two crossing mirrors

Now tilt one mirror so the two meet at a point $$O$$, like the two halves of a hinged mirror on a
dressing table. Flipping in one and then the other leaves $$O$$ where it is, since $$O$$ is on both
mirrors, and keeps every distance from $$O$$. A move that keeps a centre still and keeps distances
from it looks like a turn about that centre. The theorem says it is one, by twice the angle between
the mirrors.

```plane
point O 0 0 sw
point A 3 0 hidden
point B 2.457 1.721 hidden
point P 2.349 -0.855 se
point P' 2.349 0.855 e
point P'' 1.607 1.915 n
line O A color=blue
line O B color=blue
angle A O B color=blue r=0.8 label="θ"
angle P O P'' color=purple r=1.5 label="2θ"
segment P P' color=orange dashed
segment P' P'' color=purple dashed
text 3.3 0.3 "L"
text 2.8 2.15 "K"
caption "The mirrors meet at θ = 35°. Flipping in L and then in K turns P by 70° about O"
```

:::theorem[Two crossing mirrors make a turn]{#crossing-mirrors}

Let $$L$$ and $$K$$ be lines meeting at a point $$O$$, and let $$\theta$$ be the angle you turn
through, anticlockwise, from $$L$$ to $$K$$. Then "first flip in $$L$$, then flip in $$K$$",
$$R_K \circ R_L$$, is the turn about $$O$$ by $$2\theta$$ anticlockwise.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_plane_isometry#Mirror_combinations"}

**Distances from $$O$$ are kept.** The point $$O$$ is on both mirrors, so neither flip moves it. A
flip keeps the distance from any point of its mirror (:ref{#mirror-distances}), so for any point
$$P$$, with $$P' = R_L(P)$$ and $$P'' = R_K(P')$$,

```math
d(O, P'') = d(O, P') = d(O, P)
```

**Directions around $$O$$.** Describe the direction of each point from $$O$$ by the angle you turn
through, anticlockwise, from the line $$L$$ to reach it, so that $$K$$ is at $$\theta$$. Whole turns
make no difference to where a point ends up (:ref{#rotation-whole-turns}), so angles that differ by
$$360^\circ$$ give the same direction.

Now see what one flip does to directions. A flip in a line through $$O$$ leaves its own mirror in
place, keeps the size of every angle (:ref{#isometry-angles}), and sends each point to the other
side of the mirror (:ref{#reflection}). So a point $$a^\circ$$ anticlockwise of the mirror goes to
the point $$a^\circ$$ clockwise of it, at the same distance from $$O$$:

- The flip in $$L$$, which is at $$0^\circ$$, sends direction $$\alpha$$ to direction $$-\alpha$$.
- The flip in $$K$$, which is at $$\theta$$, sends direction $$\beta$$, which is $$\beta - \theta$$
  past $$K$$, to $$\theta - (\beta - \theta) = 2\theta - \beta$$.

**Follow $$P$$.** If $$P$$ is in direction $$\alpha$$, then $$P'$$ is in direction $$-\alpha$$, and
$$P''$$ is in direction

```math
2\theta - (-\alpha) = \alpha + 2\theta
```

So $$P''$$ is the same distance from $$O$$ as $$P$$, turned $$2\theta$$ further anticlockwise. That
is where the turn about $$O$$ by $$2\theta$$ sends $$P$$ (:ref{#rotation}). The two maps agree at
every point, so they are equal (:ref{#map-equality}).

:::

Move the second mirror below and watch $$P''$$ go round twice as fast as the mirror turns.

<TwoMirrors />

:::example[Mirrors at a right angle]{#corner-mirrors}

Two mirrors that meet square on, at $$90^\circ$$, make a turn by $$180^\circ$$: a half turn about
the corner, which is the same as reflecting through the corner point (:ref{#half-turn}). This is why
a corner mirror shows your face the way other people see it. One mirror swaps your left and right;
two square mirrors flip twice, and the result is a turn, which swaps nothing.

:::

A kaleidoscope uses this. Inside the tube, two long mirrors meet at $$60^\circ$$, with a few loose
beads at the end between them. Flipping in one mirror and then the other turns the beads by
$$120^\circ$$, and the images of images fill the whole circle with copies. You see the beads turned
by $$0^\circ$$, $$120^\circ$$ and $$240^\circ$$, and flipped in three mirror lines: six copies in
all.

<Kaleidoscope caption="Two mirrors at 60° turn and flip the beads between them into six copies around the circle" />

## Glide reflections

Walk along a beach and look back at your footprints. A left print and a right print are mirror
images of each other, and each one is also a step further along. So each print is the one before it
flipped across the line of the walk and then slid along that line. This combination is the fourth
kind of isometry, after slides, turns and flips.

<GlideFootprints caption="Each footprint is the one before it, flipped across the line of the walk and slid along it" />

:::definition[Glide reflection]{#glide-reflection}

Let $$L$$ be a line, $$R_L$$ the flip in $$L$$, and $$T$$ a slide along $$L$$, that is, by an arrow
parallel to $$L$$. The composite "first flip in $$L$$, then slide along $$L$$", $$T \circ R_L$$, is
a **glide reflection** along $$L$$.

:::

```plane
point L1 -1 0 hidden
point L2 5 0 hidden
point P 0 1.2 n
point P' 0 -1.2 s
point P'' 3 -1.2 s
line L1 L2 color=blue
segment P P' color=orange dashed
segment P' P'' color=purple dashed
text 4.9 0.3 "L"
caption "A glide along L: the flip sends P to P′, and the slide by 3 along L sends P′ to P″"
```

:::example[Where a glide sends a point]{#glide-example}

Take the glide along a horizontal line $$L$$ that slides $$3$$ units to the right. A point $$P$$
that is $$1.2$$ units above $$L$$ is flipped to the point $$1.2$$ units below $$L$$, straight under
$$P$$, and then slid $$3$$ units to the right. A point on $$L$$ is not moved by the flip, so the
glide slides it $$3$$ along $$L$$.

Every point moves, so a glide by a non-zero slide has no fixed points (:ref{#fixed-point}). That
tells it apart from a flip, which fixes its whole mirror, and from a turn, which fixes its centre.

:::

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on combining moves, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-combining-moves-follow-point}

Let $$O$$ be a point and $$L$$ the horizontal line through it. Let $$R$$ be the flip in $$L$$,
$$G_{90}$$ the quarter turn anticlockwise about $$O$$, and $$T$$ the slide $$1$$ unit to the right.
The point $$P$$ is $$3$$ units straight above $$O$$. Describe where each of these sends $$P$$:

1. $$T \circ R$$
2. $$R \circ T$$
3. $$G_{90} \circ R$$

:::solution

1. First $$R$$: the flip sends $$P$$ to the point $$3$$ units below $$O$$. Then $$T$$ slides it
   $$1$$ to the right. So $$P$$ ends $$3$$ below $$O$$ and $$1$$ to the right.
2. First $$T$$: $$P$$ goes to the point $$3$$ above $$O$$ and $$1$$ to the right. Then the flip in
   $$L$$ sends it to $$3$$ below $$O$$ and $$1$$ to the right. This is the same point as in part 1.
3. First $$R$$: $$P$$ goes to $$3$$ below $$O$$. Then the quarter turn anticlockwise sends the point
   straight below $$O$$ to the point straight to the right of $$O$$, at the same distance. So $$P$$
   ends $$3$$ units to the right of $$O$$.

Parts 1 and 2 agree here because the slide runs along the mirror. A slide that crossed the mirror
would give different answers in the two orders.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-combining-moves-flip-three-times}

Let $$R$$ be a flip in a line. Simplify $$R^3$$, $$R^4$$ and $$R^{11}$$.

:::solution

By :ref{#reflection-twice}, $$R^2 = I$$. Using $$F^m \circ F^n = F^{m+n}$$:

- $$R^3 = R^2 \circ R = I \circ R = R$$
- $$R^4 = R^2 \circ R^2 = I \circ I = I$$
- $$R^{11} = R^{10} \circ R$$, and $$R^{10} = (R^2)^5 = I$$, so $$R^{11} = R$$

An odd number of flips in the same mirror is one flip, and an even number leaves everything in
place.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-combining-moves-parallel-gap}

The mirrors $$L$$ and $$K$$ are parallel and $$5$$ apart. A point $$P$$ is $$2$$ units from $$L$$,
on the side away from $$K$$.

1. How far is $$P$$ from $$K$$?
2. Where does $$R_K \circ R_L$$ send $$P$$? And $$R_L \circ R_K$$?

:::solution

Measure positions along the line through $$P$$ square to the mirrors, with $$L$$ at $$0$$ and $$K$$
at $$5$$. Then $$P$$ is at $$-2$$.

1. $$P$$ is at $$-2$$ and $$K$$ at $$5$$, so $$P$$ is $$7$$ from $$K$$.
2. By :ref{#parallel-mirrors}, $$R_K \circ R_L$$ slides every point $$2 \cdot 5 = 10$$ towards
   $$K$$'s side, so $$P$$ goes to position $$8$$. Step by step: the flip in $$L$$ sends $$-2$$ to
   $$2$$, and the flip in $$K$$ sends $$2$$ to $$10 - 2 = 8$$. The other order slides $$10$$ the
   other way: the flip in $$K$$ sends $$-2$$ to $$10 - (-2) = 12$$, and the flip in $$L$$ sends
   $$12$$ to $$-12$$. So $$R_L \circ R_K$$ sends $$P$$ to position $$-12$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-combining-moves-mirror-angle}

Two mirrors $$L$$ and $$K$$ cross at $$O$$, and the angle from $$L$$ to $$K$$ is $$45^\circ$$
anticlockwise.

1. What single move is $$R_K \circ R_L$$?
2. Simplify $$(R_K \circ R_L)^4$$.
3. What angle between two mirrors would make $$R_K \circ R_L$$ a turn by $$120^\circ$$?

:::solution

1. By :ref{#crossing-mirrors}, it is the turn about $$O$$ by $$2 \cdot 45^\circ = 90^\circ$$
   anticlockwise, $$G_{90}$$.
2. $$(G_{90})^4$$ is four quarter turns, $$G_{360}$$, which is $$I$$ by :ref{#rotation-rules}.
3. The turn is twice the angle, so the angle is $$\frac{120^\circ}{2} = 60^\circ$$. This is the
   angle between the mirrors of a kaleidoscope with three-fold patterns.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-combining-moves-two-flips-not-flip}

Explain why two flips, one after the other, can never be a single flip.

:::solution

Look at the points each move leaves in place (:ref{#fixed-point}). A flip in a line leaves exactly
the points of its mirror in place: a point on the mirror stays, and any other point goes to the
other side.

Now take two flips $$R_L$$ and $$R_K$$. There are three cases:

- **The same mirror**: $$R_L \circ R_L = I$$ (:ref{#reflection-twice}), which leaves every point in
  place, not only one line.
- **Parallel mirrors**, a distance $$d > 0$$ apart: $$R_K \circ R_L$$ is a slide by $$2d$$
  (:ref{#parallel-mirrors}), which moves every point, so it leaves no point in place.
- **Crossing mirrors** at an angle $$\theta$$ between $$0^\circ$$ and $$180^\circ$$: it is a turn by
  $$2\theta$$ about the crossing point $$O$$ (:ref{#crossing-mirrors}). Since $$2\theta$$ is between
  $$0^\circ$$ and $$360^\circ$$, every point other than $$O$$ moves round the circle, so only $$O$$
  stays in place.

In no case are the points left in place exactly a line, so the result is never a flip.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-combining-moves-glide-twice}

Let $$L$$ be a horizontal line, $$R$$ the flip in $$L$$, and $$T$$ the slide $$2$$ units to the
right, so that $$T \circ R$$ is a glide along $$L$$. Follow a point $$P$$ that is $$1$$ unit above
$$L$$ through the glide done twice, $$(T \circ R)^2$$. What single move does it look like?

:::solution

Write the positions as "$$a$$ to the right of a starting mark, $$b$$ above $$L$$", starting from
$$P$$ at $$0$$ to the right, $$1$$ above:

1. $$R$$: $$0$$ to the right, $$1$$ below.
2. $$T$$: $$2$$ to the right, $$1$$ below.
3. $$R$$: $$2$$ to the right, $$1$$ above.
4. $$T$$: $$4$$ to the right, $$1$$ above.

So $$P$$ ends $$4$$ to the right of where it started, at the same height. The two flips cancel and
the two slides add up, so the glide done twice looks like the slide $$T^2$$, by $$4$$ to the right.
This is why every second footprint is a copy of the first, moved two steps along.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-combining-moves-order-check}

Let $$G_{30}$$ and $$G_{50}$$ be turns about the same point $$O$$. Show that
$$G_{30} \circ G_{50} = G_{50} \circ G_{30}$$. Then give a pair of moves for which the two orders
give different results, and say which point shows it.

:::solution

By :ref{#rotation-rules}, turning by $$50^\circ$$ and then $$30^\circ$$ is turning by $$80^\circ$$,
and turning by $$30^\circ$$ and then $$50^\circ$$ is also turning by $$80^\circ$$. So both
composites equal $$G_{80}$$.

For a pair that doesn't agree, use the quarter turn $$G_{90}$$ about $$O$$ and the flip $$R$$ in the
horizontal line $$L$$ through $$O$$, as in :ref{#order-matters}. The point $$P$$ on $$L$$, $$2$$ to
the right of $$O$$, goes below $$O$$ under $$R \circ G_{90}$$ and above $$O$$ under
$$G_{90} \circ R$$. One point sent to different places is enough to show the maps are different
(:ref{#map-equality}).

:::

:::::
