---
title: What an isometry keeps
description: Moves that keep every distance, what else they keep, and how few pins hold one still.
---

Watch a robot arm in a factory pick up a part, swing it round and set it down somewhere else. The
part is in a new place, but its holes are still the same distance apart and its corners are still
square. Slides, turns and flips all move things this way, keeping every distance the same, and a
move like that is called an **isometry**.

In this guide we'll see how much follows from that one property. Straight lines stay straight,
angles and areas keep their size, and an isometry that fixes three points not on one line moves
nothing at all.

## Moves that keep distances

Lay a sheet of tracing paper over the page and mark two dots on it. Slide the sheet, turn it about a
pin, or lift it and flip it over, and then measure between the dots again. The distance is the same,
because the paper doesn't stretch. A photocopier set to zoom works differently: it moves every point
too, but the dots end up further apart. Try the four moves below and compare the side lengths before
and after each one.

<RigidOrNot />

:::definition[Isometry]{#isometry}

A map $$F$$ of the plane (:ref{#map}) is an **isometry** if it keeps every distance: for every two
points $$P$$ and $$Q$$,

```math
d\big(F(P), F(Q)\big) = d(P, Q)
```

In words: wherever $$F$$ sends $$P$$ and $$Q$$, their images are exactly as far apart as $$P$$ and
$$Q$$ were. The name comes from Greek: "iso" means equal and "metry" means measure.

:::

The moves of the earlier guides are the main examples. The tracing paper shows that they keep
distances, and this guide takes that as given.

:::remark[Slides, turns and flips keep distances]{#basic-isometries}

These maps are isometries:

1. **Slides**: every translation (:ref{#translation})
2. **Turns**: every rotation (:ref{#rotation})
3. **Flips**: every reflection in a line (:ref{#reflection}) and every reflection through a point
   (:ref{#point-reflection})

These are taken as given.

::see-also{src="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_plane_isometry#Informal_discussion"}

:::

:::example[Two maps that are not isometries]

**A zoom.** Take the dilation by $$2$$ about a point $$O$$ (:ref{#dilation}), and two points $$P$$
and $$Q$$ that are $$3$$ apart. Their images are $$6$$ apart, since the zoom doubles every distance.
So the zoom is not an isometry. One pair of points whose distance changes is enough to rule a map
out.

**Squashing to a point.** Take the map that sends every point to one point $$O$$. Two points $$3$$
apart both land on $$O$$, and their images are $$0$$ apart. So this map is not an isometry either.

:::

The squash fails in a telling way: it sends two different points to the same place. An isometry
never does that, since the two images must be as far apart as the points were.

:::proposition[Different points stay different]{#isometry-distinct}

Let $$F$$ be an isometry. If $$P \neq Q$$, then $$F(P) \neq F(Q)$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isometry#Definition"}

Since $$P \neq Q$$, the distance $$d(P, Q)$$ is not $$0$$
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/distance-rules}, rule 1). The isometry keeps it, so
$$d\big(F(P), F(Q)\big) = d(P, Q)$$ is not $$0$$ either. By the same rule, $$F(P)$$ and $$F(Q)$$ are
different points.

:::

## Segments and lines

A straight stick drawn on tracing paper is still straight after you slide, turn or flip the sheet.
The aim now is to see why from distances alone, since that is all an isometry promises. The key is
that "being on a segment" can be said with distances. A point $$X$$ lies on the segment $$PM$$
exactly when the trip from $$P$$ to $$M$$ through $$X$$ is no longer than the direct trip: the two
short distances add up to the long one. An isometry keeps all three distances, so it keeps that
equation, and so it keeps $$X$$ on the segment.

```plane
point P 0 0 sw
point X 2 0.8 s
point M 5 2 se
point P' 7 3 w
point X' 7.86 1.08 w
point M' 9.15 -1.6 se
segment P M
segment P' M' color=blue
text 2.5 2.4 "d(P, X) + d(X, M) = d(P, M)"
caption "X lies on PM because its two distances add up to d(P, M); the images satisfy the same equation"
```

:::theorem[Segments go to segments]{#isometry-segments}

Let $$F$$ be an isometry and $$PM$$ a segment, with images $$P' = F(P)$$ and $$M' = F(M)$$. Then
$$F$$ sends the segment $$PM$$ onto the segment $$P'M'$$:

1. **Nothing leaves**: every point of $$PM$$ lands on $$P'M'$$
2. **Nothing is missed**: every point of $$P'M'$$ is the image of a point of $$PM$$

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triangle_inequality#Euclidean_geometry"}

If $$P = M$$, both segments are single points and there is nothing to show, so take $$P \neq M$$.
Then $$P' \neq M'$$ by :ref{#isometry-distinct}.

**Nothing leaves.** Let $$X$$ be a point of the segment $$PM$$, and write $$X' = F(X)$$. By rule 1
of :ref{#distance-and-angles/segment-rules},

```math
d(P, X) + d(X, M) = d(P, M)
```

The isometry keeps each of these three distances, so the same equation holds for the images:

```math
d(P', X') + d(X', M') = d(P', M')
```

By the same rule, read the other way, $$X'$$ lies on the segment $$P'M'$$.

**Nothing is missed.** Let $$Y$$ be a point of the segment $$P'M'$$, and let $$c = d(P', Y)$$. Since
$$d(P', M') = d(P, M)$$, the number $$c$$ is between $$0$$ and $$d(P, M)$$. By rule 2 of
:ref{#distance-and-angles/segment-rules}, the segment $$PM$$ has a point $$X$$ with $$d(P, X) = c$$.
Its image $$X'$$ lies on $$P'M'$$ by the first part, and $$d(P', X') = d(P, X) = c$$. Rule 2 again
says that only one point of $$P'M'$$ is at distance $$c$$ from $$P'$$, and $$Y$$ is such a point, so
$$X' = Y$$.

:::

Lines need one more fact. A segment stops at its two ends, but a line runs on without end in both
directions: however far you go along it from a point, there are points of the line that far away on
either side. The earlier rules don't state this, and the proof below takes it from the picture of a
line. With it, the result for segments carries over to whole lines.

:::corollary[Lines go to lines]{#isometry-lines}

Let $$F$$ be an isometry and $$P \neq M$$. Then $$F$$ sends the line $$PM$$ onto the line $$P'M'$$
through the images: every point of the line $$PM$$ lands on the line $$P'M'$$, and every point of
the line $$P'M'$$ is the image of a point of the line $$PM$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triangle_inequality#Euclidean_geometry"}

**Nothing leaves.** Let $$X$$ be a point of the line $$PM$$ other than $$P$$ and $$M$$. Of three
different points on one line, one lies between the other two (rule 3 of
:ref{#distance-and-angles/segment-rules}). Whichever it is, its two distances to the others add up
to the third distance, and the isometry keeps that equation. So among $$X'$$, $$P'$$ and $$M'$$, the
same point lies on the segment joining the other two. In every case the three images lie on one
line, and the only line through $$P'$$ and $$M'$$ is the line $$P'M'$$
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/line-rules}, rule 1). So $$X'$$ is on it.

**Nothing is missed.** Let $$Y$$ be a point of the line $$P'M'$$, and pick a number $$D$$ bigger
than $$d(P', Y)$$. The line $$PM$$ runs on without end, so it has points $$A$$ and $$B$$ on either
side of $$P$$ with $$d(A, P) = d(P, B) = D$$. Then $$P$$ is on the segment $$AB$$, so
$$d(A, B) = 2D$$ by rule 1 of :ref{#distance-and-angles/segment-rules}. Their images $$A'$$ and
$$B'$$ lie on the line $$P'M'$$, with $$d(A', B') = 2D$$ and $$P'$$ on the segment $$A'B'$$.

Suppose $$Y$$ were not on the segment $$A'B'$$. Then one of $$A'$$ and $$B'$$, say $$A'$$, lies
between $$Y$$ and the other, so $$d(Y, B') = d(Y, A') + d(A', B') \ge 2D$$. But by the triangle
inequality, $$d(Y, B') \le d(Y, P') + d(P', B') < D + D = 2D$$. This is impossible
(:ref{#basic-proofs/contradiction}), so $$Y$$ lies on the segment $$A'B'$$. By
:ref{#isometry-segments}, $$Y$$ is the image of a point of the segment $$AB$$, which is part of the
line $$PM$$.

:::

## Right angles and parallel lines

A carpenter's square corner, the corner of a tile, the corner of a page: move any of them without
bending it and the corner is still square. To prove this from distances, describe a square corner
with distances. The previous chapter already did: a point is on the perpendicular bisector of $$AB$$
exactly when it is as far from $$A$$ as from $$B$$
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/perpendicular-bisector-rule}). So two lines that cross square on can be
seen as a segment and its perpendicular bisector, and an isometry keeps "as far from $$A$$ as from
$$B$$".

```plane
point A -3 0 s
point O 0 0 sw
point B 3 0 s
point X 0 2.5 e
line A B color=muted extend=0.6
line O X color=purple extend=0.6
segment X A color=blue dashed
segment X B color=blue dashed
right B O X color=purple
ticks A O 1
ticks O B 1
text 3.9 0.4 "L"
text 0.4 3.5 "K"
caption "K crosses L square on at O, so K is the perpendicular bisector of AB, and every point X of K is as far from A as from B"
```

:::proposition[Right angles are kept]{#isometry-perpendicular}

Let $$F$$ be an isometry, and let $$K$$ and $$L$$ be perpendicular lines. Then their images $$F(K)$$
and $$F(L)$$ are perpendicular lines.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bisection#Perpendicular_line_segment_bisector"}

The images are lines by :ref{#isometry-lines}. It remains to show that they cross square on.

**Describe the corner with distances.** Let $$O$$ be the point where $$K$$ and $$L$$ cross. Pick two
points $$A$$ and $$B$$ of $$L$$ on either side of $$O$$, each at distance $$1$$ from it. Then $$O$$
is the midpoint of $$AB$$, and $$K$$ is the line through $$O$$ perpendicular to $$AB$$: it is the
perpendicular bisector of $$AB$$ (:ref{#distance-and-angles/perpendicular-bisector}).

**Move it.** The image $$F(L)$$ is the line $$A'B'$$. The image $$O'$$ lies on the segment $$A'B'$$
(:ref{#isometry-segments}) at distance $$1$$ from both ends, so $$O'$$ is the midpoint of $$A'B'$$.
Now take any point $$X$$ of $$K$$. It is as far from $$A$$ as from $$B$$
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/perpendicular-bisector-rule}), and the isometry keeps both distances:

```math
d(X', A') = d(X, A) = d(X, B) = d(X', B')
```

So $$X'$$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $$A'B'$$, by the same theorem.

**Compare the lines.** Every point of the line $$F(K)$$ is the image of a point of $$K$$, so the
whole line $$F(K)$$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $$A'B'$$. Two lines that share two
different points are the same line (:ref{#distance-and-angles/line-rules}, rule 1), so $$F(K)$$ is
the perpendicular bisector of $$A'B'$$. It crosses the line $$A'B'$$, which is $$F(L)$$, square on.

:::

Parallel lines follow at once, because two lines are parallel when both are square to the same third
line.

:::corollary[Parallel lines stay parallel]{#isometry-parallel}

Let $$F$$ be an isometry, and let $$K$$ and $$L$$ be parallel lines. Then $$F(K)$$ and $$F(L)$$ are
parallel.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perpendicular#In_relationship_to_parallel_lines"}

Pick a point of $$K$$ and draw the line $$N$$ through it perpendicular to $$K$$
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/perpendicular-rules}, rule 1). Since $$L$$ is parallel to $$K$$, the line
$$N$$ is perpendicular to $$L$$ as well (rule 2 of the same rules). By
:ref{#isometry-perpendicular}, the line $$F(N)$$ is perpendicular to both $$F(K)$$ and $$F(L)$$. Two
lines perpendicular to the same line are parallel
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/perpendiculars-parallel}), so $$F(K)$$ and $$F(L)$$ are parallel.

:::

:::example[A rectangle stays a rectangle]

Let $$ABCD$$ be a rectangle with $$d(A, B) = 5$$ and $$d(B, C) = 3$$
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/rectangle}), and let $$F$$ be any isometry. Each side lands on a segment
of the same length (:ref{#isometry-segments}), so the image has sides $$5$$, $$3$$, $$5$$ and $$3$$.
Each side was perpendicular to the two sides next to it, and right angles are kept
(:ref{#isometry-perpendicular}). So $$A'B'C'D'$$ is again a rectangle, with the same side lengths
and so the same area, $$5 \cdot 3 = 15$$.

:::

## Area and angles

Slide a rug across a room, turn it, or flip it over, and it covers exactly as much floor as before.
That is the picture behind the next fact, which is taken as given. It is what you would expect: an
isometry carries every small square of a shape to a small square of the same size.

:::remark[Isometries keep area]{#isometry-area}

Let $$F$$ be an isometry and $$S$$ a region of the plane with area $$a$$. Then the image $$F(S)$$ is
a region with the same area $$a$$.

This is taken as given.

::see-also{src="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Area#Formal_definition"}

:::

Angles follow, because the previous chapter measures an angle by area: an angle of $$x^\circ$$ cuts
out $$\frac{x}{360}$$ of any disc centred at its corner (:ref{#distance-and-angles/degree}). An
isometry carries the disc to a disc of the same size and the slice to a slice of the same area, so
the fraction can't change.

```plane
point P 0 0 sw
point Q 3 0 s
point M 1.5 2.6 n
point P' 6 0.5 s
point Q' 8.6 2 e
point M' 6 3.5 n
circle P 2 color=muted dashed
circle P' 2 color=muted dashed
angle Q P M color=blue fill r=2
angle Q' P' M' color=blue fill r=2
ray P Q
ray P M
ray P' Q'
ray P' M'
caption "The slice of the disc inside the angle moves to a slice of the same area, so the angle keeps its size"
```

:::proposition[Angles are kept]{#isometry-angles}

Let $$F$$ be an isometry, and let $$A$$ be an angle of $$x^\circ$$ at the vertex $$P$$, between the
rays $$PQ$$ and $$PM$$. Then $$F$$ sends $$A$$ to the angle at $$P'$$ between the rays $$P'Q'$$ and
$$P'M'$$, and that angle is also $$x^\circ$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congruence_(geometry)"}

This proof uses a fact proved later in the chapter: every isometry can be undone
(:ref{#isometry-inverse}), and the undo $$F^{-1}$$ is an isometry too (:ref{#inverse-isometry}).
Those proofs never use angles, so nothing here goes round in a circle.

**The sides.** The ray $$PQ$$ is made of the points $$X$$ of the line $$PQ$$ that are not on the far
side of $$P$$ from $$Q$$: either $$X$$ is on the segment $$PQ$$, or $$Q$$ is on the segment $$PX$$.
Both are statements about one point lying on a segment, which the isometry keeps
(:ref{#isometry-segments}), so the ray $$PQ$$ lands on the ray $$P'Q'$$. In the same way the ray
$$PM$$ lands on the ray $$P'M'$$.

**The region.** If $$A$$ is a straight angle, then $$P$$ lies between $$Q$$ and $$M$$, so $$P'$$
lies between $$Q'$$ and $$M'$$ (:ref{#isometry-segments}). So the angle at $$P'$$ is a straight
angle too, of $$180^\circ$$ (:ref{#distance-and-angles/straight-angle}). Otherwise take the angle
smaller than a straight angle, as in :ref{#distance-and-angles/angle-name}, and leave the reflex
angle to the end. The region inside it is filled by the segments that join a point of one ray to a
point of the other, as the picture of an angle shows; this description is taken from the picture.
Each such segment $$XY$$ lands on the segment $$X'Y'$$, which joins a point of the ray $$P'Q'$$ to a
point of the ray $$P'M'$$ (:ref{#isometry-segments}). So the angle $$A$$ lands inside the angle
$$A'$$ at $$P'$$ between those rays.

**The disc.** Let $$D$$ be the disc with centre $$P$$ and radius $$1$$. A point within $$1$$ of
$$P$$ lands within $$1$$ of $$P'$$, so $$F(D)$$ lies inside the disc $$D'$$ with centre $$P'$$ and
radius $$1$$. The undo $$F^{-1}$$ also keeps distances and sends $$P'$$ back to $$P$$, so it brings
each point of $$D'$$ back to a point of $$D$$. So every point of $$D'$$ is the image of a point of
$$D$$, and $$F(D)$$ is the whole of $$D'$$.

**The slice.** In the same way, $$F^{-1}$$ sends the rays $$P'Q'$$ and $$P'M'$$ back to $$PQ$$ and
$$PM$$, so it brings each point of $$A'$$ back to a point of $$A$$, and $$F(A)$$ is the whole of
$$A'$$. An isometry never sends two points to one place (:ref{#isometry-distinct}). So the sector
$$S$$, the part of $$D$$ inside $$A$$ (:ref{#distance-and-angles/sector}), lands exactly on the
sector $$S'$$, the part of $$D'$$ inside $$A'$$. Areas are kept (:ref{#isometry-area}), so

```math
\frac{\text{area of } S'}{\text{area of } D'} = \frac{\text{area of } S}{\text{area of } D} = \frac{x}{360}
```

So $$A'$$ is also an angle of $$x^\circ$$ (:ref{#distance-and-angles/degree}). A reflex angle is
what is left of the full turn, $$360 - x$$ degrees, so it is kept too.

:::

One thing does change under a flip: the direction of turning. Take a ray from a point $$O$$ of the
mirror, running along the mirror, and a second ray $$OP$$ reached from it by turning $$a^\circ$$
anticlockwise. The flip leaves the first ray where it is, and keeps the size of the angle,
$$a^\circ$$. But it sends $$P$$ to the other side of the mirror (:ref{#reflection}), so the ray
$$OP'$$ is reached by turning $$a^\circ$$ clockwise instead. The size is the same and the direction
is reversed, the way a right hand looks like a left hand in a mirror.

This completes the argument in the [Reflections](./reflections.mdx) guide that light leaves a mirror
at the same angle it arrived at. That argument needed the flip to keep the angle between the
incoming path and the mirror, and :ref{#isometry-angles} now proves it.

## How few pins hold the sheet still

Push one drawing pin through the tracing paper and the sheet can still turn about it. Push in a
second pin and the sheet can no longer slide or turn, but it isn't quite stuck. You can still lift
it and flip it over the line through the two pins, and the points of that line never move. A door
works the same way: it can swing, but the line through its two hinges stays where it is. Push in a
third pin, off that line, and nothing can move at all.

<HingedDoor caption="However the door swings, the line through its two hinges stays put" />

<PinnedSheet caption="Two pins hold the line through them, but the sheet can still flip over it; three pins not on one line hold the sheet still" />

A point that a map leaves where it is, like a pinned point, is a fixed point (:ref{#fixed-point}).
One fact does all the work in this section. If an isometry moves a point $$X$$ to a different point
$$X'$$, then each fixed point is as far from $$X$$ as from $$X'$$, so it lies on the perpendicular
bisector of $$XX'$$.

```plane
point X -2 0 w
point X' 2 0 e
point O' 0 0 hidden
point P 0 2.4 e
point Q 0 -1.6 e
line P Q color=purple extend=0.6
segment X X' color=muted dashed
right X' O' P color=purple
segment P X color=blue
segment P X' color=blue
ticks P X 1
ticks P X' 1
caption "A fixed point P is as far from X as from its image X', so P is on the perpendicular bisector of XX'"
```

:::lemma[Fixed points lie on a bisector]{#fixed-points-bisector}

Let $$F$$ be an isometry and $$X$$ a point with $$X' = F(X)$$ different from $$X$$. Then every fixed
point of $$F$$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $$XX'$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bisection#Perpendicular_line_segment_bisector"}

Let $$P$$ be a fixed point, so $$F(P) = P$$. The isometry keeps the distance from $$P$$ to $$X$$:

```math
d(P, X) = d\big(F(P), F(X)\big) = d(P, X')
```

By :ref{#distance-and-angles/perpendicular-bisector-rule}, $$P$$ lies on the perpendicular bisector
of $$XX'$$.

:::

:::theorem[Two fixed points fix their line]{#two-fixed-points}

Let $$F$$ be an isometry with two different fixed points $$P$$ and $$Q$$. Then every point of the
line $$PQ$$ is a fixed point of $$F$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_plane_isometry"}

Suppose some point $$X$$ of the line $$PQ$$ were moved, to $$X' \neq X$$
(:ref{#basic-proofs/contradiction}).

**The line is the bisector.** By :ref{#fixed-points-bisector}, both $$P$$ and $$Q$$ lie on the
perpendicular bisector of $$XX'$$. Only one line passes through $$P$$ and $$Q$$
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/line-rules}, rule 1), so the line $$PQ$$ is that bisector.

**So $$X$$ is on the bisector.** The point $$X$$ is on the line $$PQ$$, so it is on the
perpendicular bisector of $$XX'$$. Then it is as far from $$X$$ as from $$X'$$
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/perpendicular-bisector-rule}):

```math
0 = d(X, X) = d(X, X')
```

So $$X = X'$$ (:ref{#distance-and-angles/distance-rules}, rule 1), which contradicts $$X' \neq X$$.
So no point of the line $$PQ$$ moves.

:::

Two fixed points are not enough to stop everything. The flip in the line $$PQ$$ keeps both $$P$$ and
$$Q$$ where they are, yet moves every point off the line. That is the sheet flipped over the line
through its two pins. The [classification](./classification.mdx) guide shows that this flip and the
identity are the only isometries that fix two points (:ref{#fix-two-points}).

:::theorem[Three fixed points fix everything]{#three-fixed-points}

Let $$F$$ be an isometry with three fixed points $$P$$, $$Q$$ and $$M$$ that don't lie on one line.
Then $$F$$ is the identity: every point is a fixed point.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_plane_isometry"}

Suppose some point $$X$$ were moved, to $$X' \neq X$$ (:ref{#basic-proofs/contradiction}). By
:ref{#fixed-points-bisector}, all three fixed points $$P$$, $$Q$$ and $$M$$ lie on the perpendicular
bisector of $$XX'$$. That bisector is one line, so the three points lie on one line. This
contradicts the choice of $$P$$, $$Q$$ and $$M$$, so no point moves.

:::

:::example[Counting fixed points]

The theorems match the moves you already know:

- **A turn** about $$O$$ by $$90^\circ$$ fixes $$O$$ and nothing else. It has one fixed point, which
  is not enough to force anything.
- **A flip** in a line $$L$$ fixes every point of $$L$$ and nothing else. Its fixed points all lie
  on one line, so :ref{#three-fixed-points} doesn't apply, and indeed the flip is not the identity.
- **A slide** by a nonzero arrow fixes no point at all.

So an isometry that is not the identity has all its fixed points on one line, if it has any. Three
fixed points off a line can only belong to the identity.

:::

The [Undoing moves](./undoing-moves.mdx) guide turns this into a practical test: two isometries that
agree at three points not on one line are the same isometry (:ref{#three-points-decide}). To know an
isometry completely, it is enough to know where it sends the three corners of one triangle.

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on what an isometry keeps, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-isometries-which}

Decide whether each map is an isometry, and give a reason:

1. The dilation by $$1$$ about a point $$O$$
2. The dilation by $$\frac{1}{2}$$ about a point $$O$$
3. A flip in a line $$L$$, followed by a slide of $$3$$ along $$L$$
4. The map that sends every point to the point $$O$$

:::solution

1. **Yes.** A dilation by $$1$$ keeps each point at the same distance from $$O$$ on the same ray, so
   it leaves every point where it is (:ref{#dilation}). It is the identity, which keeps every
   distance.
2. **No.** Take $$P$$ and $$Q$$ on one ray from $$O$$ with $$d(O, P) = 2$$ and $$d(O, Q) = 4$$, so
   $$d(P, Q) = 2$$. Their images are at distances $$1$$ and $$2$$ from $$O$$ on the same ray, so
   they are $$1$$ apart. A single pair of points with a changed distance is enough.
3. **Yes.** The flip keeps every distance and the slide keeps every distance
   (:ref{#basic-isometries}). So for any $$P$$ and $$Q$$, the distance is unchanged after the flip,
   and unchanged again after the slide.
4. **No.** Two different points both land on $$O$$, so their images are $$0$$ apart. This breaks
   :ref{#isometry-distinct}.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-isometries-right-triangle}

The triangle $$PQM$$ has a right angle at $$Q$$, with $$d(P, Q) = 5$$ and $$d(Q, M) = 12$$. An
isometry sends it to the triangle $$P'Q'M'$$. Find the three side lengths of the image, show that it
also has a right angle at $$Q'$$, and find its area.

:::solution

**Sides.** By :ref{#distance-and-angles/pythagoras},
$$d(P, M) = \sqrt{25 + 144} = \sqrt{169} = 13$$. The isometry keeps all three distances, so the
image has sides $$d(P', Q') = 5$$, $$d(Q', M') = 12$$ and $$d(P', M') = 13$$.

**Right angle.** The lines $$PQ$$ and $$QM$$ are perpendicular. Their images are the lines $$P'Q'$$
and $$Q'M'$$ (:ref{#isometry-lines}), and these are perpendicular by :ref{#isometry-perpendicular}.
So the image has a right angle at $$Q'$$.

**Area.** The image is a right triangle with legs $$5$$ and $$12$$, so its area is
$$\frac{1}{2} \cdot 5 \cdot 12 = 30$$ (:ref{#distance-and-angles/right-triangle-area}). This agrees
with :ref{#isometry-area}, since the original triangle has the same area.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-isometries-midpoint}

Let $$F$$ be an isometry and $$O$$ the midpoint of a segment $$PQ$$. Prove that $$F(O)$$ is the
midpoint of the segment $$P'Q'$$.

:::solution

The midpoint $$O$$ lies on $$PQ$$ with $$d(P, O) = d(O, Q)$$ (:ref{#distance-and-angles/midpoint}).
By :ref{#isometry-segments}, $$O' = F(O)$$ lies on the segment $$P'Q'$$. The isometry keeps both
distances:

```math
d(P', O') = d(P, O) = d(O, Q) = d(O', Q')
```

So $$O'$$ is the point of $$P'Q'$$ equally far from both ends, which is the midpoint of $$P'Q'$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-isometries-circle}

Let $$F$$ be an isometry, and let $$C$$ be the circle with centre $$O$$ and radius $$r$$. Show that
every point of $$C$$ lands on the circle with centre $$O' = F(O)$$ and radius $$r$$. If a point
$$X$$ is inside the circle, with $$d(O, X) = 2$$ and $$r = 5$$, where does $$X'$$ land?

:::solution

A point $$X$$ of $$C$$ has $$d(O, X) = r$$ (:ref{#distance-and-angles/circle}). The isometry keeps
this distance, so $$d(O', X') = d(O, X) = r$$, and $$X'$$ lies on the circle with centre $$O'$$ and
radius $$r$$.

For the point with $$d(O, X) = 2$$, the same reasoning gives $$d(O', X') = 2$$. So $$X'$$ is inside
the image circle, at distance $$2$$ from its centre. Points inside stay inside, and points on the
circle stay on it.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-isometries-two-pins}

An isometry $$F$$ fixes two points $$P$$ and $$Q$$, but moves a point $$X$$ to a different point
$$X'$$.

1. Explain why $$X$$ can't lie on the line $$PQ$$.
2. Explain why the line $$PQ$$ is the perpendicular bisector of $$XX'$$.
3. If $$d(X, P) = 3$$ and $$d(X, Q) = 4$$, find $$d(X', P)$$ and $$d(X', Q)$$.

:::solution

1. Every point of the line $$PQ$$ is fixed (:ref{#two-fixed-points}). The point $$X$$ is moved, so
   it is not on that line.
2. By :ref{#fixed-points-bisector}, both $$P$$ and $$Q$$ lie on the perpendicular bisector of
   $$XX'$$. Only one line passes through two different points
   (:ref{#distance-and-angles/line-rules}, rule 1), so that bisector is the line $$PQ$$.
3. The isometry keeps distances and fixes $$P$$ and $$Q$$, so
   $$d(X', P) = d(F(X), F(P)) = d(X, P) = 3$$ and in the same way $$d(X', Q) = 4$$.

This is the sheet with two pins, flipped over the line through them: $$X'$$ is the mirror image of
$$X$$ in the line $$PQ$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-isometries-four-pins}

Three points $$A$$, $$B$$ and $$C$$ lie on one line $$L$$, and a fourth point $$D$$ is not on $$L$$.
An isometry fixes all four points. Prove that it is the identity. Would it be enough to know that it
fixes $$A$$, $$B$$ and $$C$$?

:::solution

The points $$A$$, $$B$$ and $$D$$ are three fixed points. They don't lie on one line: the only line
through $$A$$ and $$B$$ is $$L$$ (:ref{#distance-and-angles/line-rules}, rule 1), and $$D$$ is not
on $$L$$. So by :ref{#three-fixed-points}, the isometry is the identity. The point $$C$$ was not
needed.

Fixing $$A$$, $$B$$ and $$C$$ alone is not enough. All three lie on $$L$$, and the flip in $$L$$
fixes every point of $$L$$ while moving every other point. So an isometry fixing three points on one
line need not be the identity.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-isometries-rectangle-diagonal}

A rectangle $$ABCD$$ has $$d(A, B) = 8$$ and $$d(B, C) = 6$$. An isometry sends it to $$A'B'C'D'$$.
Find the length of the diagonal $$A'C'$$ in two ways: by moving first and measuring after, and by
measuring first and moving after.

:::solution

**Measure first.** In the original, the triangle $$ABC$$ has a right angle at $$B$$, so by
:ref{#distance-and-angles/pythagoras} the diagonal is
$$d(A, C) = \sqrt{64 + 36} = \sqrt{100} = 10$$. The isometry keeps this distance, so
$$d(A', C') = 10$$.

**Move first.** The image is a rectangle with sides $$8$$ and $$6$$ (as in the example "A rectangle
stays a rectangle"), so it has a right angle at $$B'$$. By the Pythagoras theorem again,
$$d(A', C') = \sqrt{8^2 + 6^2} = 10$$.

Both ways give $$10$$, as they must: an isometry never changes a length, so it doesn't matter
whether you measure before or after the move.

:::
