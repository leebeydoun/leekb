  Reflection in a line, why reflecting twice undoes it, and the shortest route via a mirror.

Look in a mirror and your image stands as far behind the glass as you stand in front of it, straight
across. Fold a sheet of paper with wet ink on one half, and the blot prints its partner on the other
half the same way. With the tracing paper, this is the third kind of move: lift the sheet, turn it
face down, and lay it back along a fold line. This page makes the idea exact, and then uses it to
answer a practical question: what is the shortest route that has to touch a line on the way, such as
a walk that stops at a river, a billiard shot off a cushion, or light bouncing off a mirror?

## Reflection in a line

Stand $$2$$ metres in front of a mirror and your image appears $$2$$ metres behind it. The image is
not off to one side: it is straight across, on the line through you that meets the mirror square on.
So the mirror line sits exactly halfway between a point and its image, and crosses the segment
between them at a right angle. The [Distance and angles](../distance-and-angles/introduction.mdx)
chapter has a name for such a line: it is the perpendicular bisector of the segment
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/perpendicular-bisector}).

Points on the mirror line itself stay where they are: each one is its own image, like the points
along the crease of a fold. Tilt the mirror below and watch the dashed segments: each joins a point
of the flag to its image, and the mirror always crosses it at its midpoint, square on.

<MirrorLine />

:::definition[Reflection in a line]{#reflection}

Let $$L$$ be a line. The **reflection in** $$L$$ is the map $$R_L$$ (:ref{#map}) that sends each
point $$P$$ to the point $$P' = R_L(P)$$ given as follows:

- **$$P$$ on $$L$$**: $$P' = P$$, so the point stays where it is
- **$$P$$ not on $$L$$**: $$P'$$ is the point for which $$L$$ is the perpendicular bisector of the
  segment $$PP'$$

The line $$L$$ is called the **mirror**. When only one mirror is in play, $$R_L$$ is written $$R$$.

:::

There is always exactly one such point $$P'$$. The line through $$P$$ perpendicular to $$L$$ is the
only one of its kind (:ref{#distance-and-angles/perpendicular-rules}), and it meets $$L$$ at a point
$$F$$. Then $$P'$$ is the point of that line on the other side of $$F$$ from $$P$$, at the same
distance: $$d(F, P') = d(F, P)$$.

```plane
point A -3.5 0 hidden
point B 3.5 0 hidden
point P 0 2 n
point F 0 0 sw
point P' 0 -2 s
line A B color=purple
text 3.3 0.35 "L"
segment P P' dashed
right B F P
ticks P F 1
ticks F P' 1
caption "The mirror L crosses PP' at its midpoint F, square on"
```

:::example[Finding an image]

Take a straight shoreline $$L$$ and a buoy $$P$$ that is $$30$$ metres out from it. The image $$P'$$
is $$30$$ metres inland, straight behind the point $$F$$ of the shore nearest the buoy. So the buoy
and its image are $$30 + 30 = 60$$ metres apart. A post $$Q$$ standing on the shoreline is its own
image: $$R(Q) = Q$$.

:::

A reflection turns things round. A flag pointing right becomes a flag pointing left, and writing
comes out backwards, as in a mirror. A slide or a turn of the tracing paper never does that, since
the sheet stays face up. This difference between flips and the other moves comes up again when maps
are combined and classified later in the chapter.

A reflection in a line is not the same as the reflection through a point (:ref{#point-reflection}).
Reflecting through a point $$O$$ sends each point straight through $$O$$ to the far side, and only
$$O$$ stays put. Reflecting in a line $$L$$ sends each point straight across $$L$$, and every point
of $$L$$ stays put.

## Reflecting twice

Fold a sheet of paper along a crease, then unfold it: every point is back where it started. A mirror
image of a mirror image is the original, as you see with two mirrors facing each other. In tracing
paper terms, flipping the sheet over the same fold twice leaves it face up and exactly where it
began.

:::proposition[Reflecting twice]{#reflection-twice}

Let $$R$$ be the reflection in a line $$L$$. Reflecting twice in $$L$$ puts every point back where
it started:

```math
R(R(P)) = P \quad \text{for every point } P
```

So first $$R$$, then $$R$$ again, is the identity map: $$R \circ R = I$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Involution_(mathematics)#Euclidean_geometry"}

If $$P$$ is on $$L$$, then $$R(P) = P$$, and so $$R(R(P)) = R(P) = P$$.

Suppose $$P$$ is not on $$L$$, and write $$P' = R(P)$$. By the definition (:ref{#reflection}), $$L$$
is the perpendicular bisector of the segment $$PP'$$. Now $$P'$$ is not on $$L$$ either, since it
lies on the other side of $$L$$ from $$P$$. So $$R(P')$$ is the one point $$Y$$ for which $$L$$ is
the perpendicular bisector of $$P'Y$$. The point $$P$$ is such a point, because the segment $$P'P$$
is the same segment as $$PP'$$. So $$R(P') = P$$, that is, $$R(R(P)) = P$$.

:::

## Points on the mirror

Stand anywhere on the mirror line and look at a point and its image. They are the same distance from
you, because the mirror sits exactly halfway between them. This small fact is what makes reflections
useful for measuring routes: a distance to a point can be swapped for the distance to its image,
without changing its length.

:::proposition[Points on the mirror]{#mirror-distances}

Let $$R$$ be the reflection in a line $$L$$, and let $$P' = R(P)$$. Then every point $$X$$ of $$L$$
is as far from $$P$$ as from $$P'$$:

```math
d(X, P) = d(X, P')
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bisection#Perpendicular_bisectors"}

If $$P$$ is on $$L$$, then $$P' = P$$ and there is nothing to prove. Otherwise, $$L$$ is the
perpendicular bisector of $$PP'$$ (:ref{#reflection}). Every point of the perpendicular bisector is
the same distance from both ends of the segment
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/perpendicular-bisector-rule}), so $$d(X, P) = d(X, P')$$.

:::

```plane
point A -3.5 0 hidden
point B 3.5 0 hidden
point P -0.5 2 n
point P' -0.5 -2 s
point X 2.2 0 se
line A B color=purple
text 3.3 0.35 "L"
segment P P' dashed color=muted
segment X P color=blue
segment X P' color=blue
ticks X P 2
ticks X P' 2
caption "Any point X of the mirror is the same distance from P as from its image P'"
```

## The shortest route via a line

You live in a cottage near a river, and every morning you walk down to the river to fill a bucket,
then carry it to your vegetable garden. Where along the bank should you stop, to make the walk as
short as possible? Stopping straight below the cottage makes the first leg short but the second
long; stopping straight below the garden does the opposite. The best place is somewhere in between,
and a reflection finds it exactly.

The trick is to picture the cottage's mirror image across the river. From any point of the bank, the
walk back up to the cottage is as long as the walk to its mirror image (:ref{#mirror-distances}). So
your whole route, cottage to bank to garden, is as long as a route from the mirror image to the bank
to the garden. That second route crosses the river, and the shortest way across is a straight line.
So stop where the straight line from the mirror image to the garden meets the bank.

<RiverWalk caption="Seen from above: stop where the straight line from the cottage's mirror image to the garden crosses the bank" />

:::theorem[The shortest route via a line]{#shortest-route}

Let $$L$$ be a line, and let $$P$$ and $$Q$$ be points on the same side of $$L$$, not on it. Let
$$P' = R(P)$$ be the reflection of $$P$$ in $$L$$, and let $$M$$ be the point where the segment
$$P'Q$$ crosses $$L$$. Then for every point $$X$$ of $$L$$,

```math
d(P, X) + d(X, Q) \ge d(P, M) + d(M, Q) = d(P', Q)
```

and the two sides are equal exactly when $$X = M$$. So the shortest route from $$P$$ to a point of
$$L$$ and on to $$Q$$ goes through $$M$$, and its length is $$d(P', Q)$$.

:::

The point $$M$$ exists because $$P'$$ lies on the other side of $$L$$ from $$Q$$, so the segment
between them crosses $$L$$. This is taken from the picture, like the sides of a line in the
[Angles](../distance-and-angles/angles.mdx) guide.

:::proof{source="https://www.cut-the-knot.org/Curriculum/Geometry/HeronsProblem.shtml"}

**Any route is at least $$d(P', Q)$$.** Let $$X$$ be a point of $$L$$. Since $$X$$ is on the mirror,
$$d(P, X) = d(P', X)$$ (:ref{#mirror-distances}). The triangle inequality
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/distance-rules}) then gives

```math
d(P, X) + d(X, Q) = d(P', X) + d(X, Q) \ge d(P', Q)
```

**The route through $$M$$ has length exactly $$d(P', Q)$$.** The point $$M$$ lies on the segment
$$P'Q$$, so $$d(P', M) + d(M, Q) = d(P', Q)$$ (:ref{#distance-and-angles/segment-rules}, rule 1).
Since $$M$$ is on $$L$$, $$d(P, M) = d(P', M)$$, and so $$d(P, M) + d(M, Q) = d(P', Q)$$.

**No other point does as well.** Suppose $$d(P, X) + d(X, Q) = d(P', Q)$$ for a point $$X$$ of
$$L$$. As in the first step, this says $$d(P', X) + d(X, Q) = d(P', Q)$$, so $$X$$ lies on the
segment $$P'Q$$ (:ref{#distance-and-angles/segment-rules}, rule 1). The line through $$P'$$ and
$$Q$$ is not $$L$$, since $$Q$$ is not on $$L$$, and two different lines meet at most once
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/crossing-lines}). Both $$X$$ and $$M$$ are on both lines, so $$X = M$$.

:::

```plane
point A -0.5 0 hidden
point B 7.5 0 hidden
point P 1 2.5 n
point Q 6 1.5 n
point P' 1 -2.5 s
point M 4.125 0 s
line A B color=purple
text 7.3 0.35 "L"
segment P M color=orange
segment M Q color=orange
segment P' M dashed color=purple
segment P P' dashed color=muted
caption "The route P to M to Q is as long as the straight line from P' to Q"
```

Try it below. Move the stopping point $$X$$ and watch the total length: it is smallest exactly when
$$X$$ sits on the straight dashed line from $$P'$$ to $$Q$$.

<RiverRoute />

:::example[How much the right stop saves]

Put the bank along a straight line $$L$$, with the cottage $$P$$ standing $$2.5$$ units from it and
the garden $$Q$$ standing $$1.5$$ units from it, on the same side. Their nearest points on the bank
are $$5$$ units apart.

**The best route.** The image $$P'$$ is $$2.5$$ units on the far side of the bank, so it is
$$2.5 + 1.5 = 4$$ units from the garden measured square to the bank, and $$5$$ units along it. These
are the legs of a right triangle with hypotenuse $$P'Q$$, so by the
:ref{#distance-and-angles/pythagoras},

```math
d(P', Q) = \sqrt{5^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{41} \approx 6.40
```

**Stopping straight below the cottage.** The first leg is $$2.5$$. The second leg is the hypotenuse
of a right triangle with legs $$5$$ and $$1.5$$, so the route is

```math
2.5 + \sqrt{5^2 + 1.5^2} = 2.5 + \sqrt{27.25} \approx 2.5 + 5.22 = 7.72
```

So stopping at the right place saves about $$1.3$$ units on every trip.

:::

## Bank shots and mirrors

The same trick works whenever something bounces off a straight edge. A billiard player who wants to
hit the red ball off a cushion aims the white ball at the red ball's mirror image beyond the
cushion. The white ball rolls straight towards that image until it meets the cushion. The bounce
then sends it along the mirror image of the rest of that straight line, and since the line ended at
the red ball's image, its mirror image ends at the red ball itself.

<BilliardBank caption="Seen from above: aim the white ball at the red ball's mirror image beyond the cushion" />

Light does the same. When you see a lamp in a wall mirror, the light has travelled from the lamp to
the mirror and then to your eye, and your eye traces it back in a straight line to a lamp behind the
glass. Light takes the shortest route from the lamp to your eye that touches the mirror, so it
bounces at the point $$M$$ of :ref{#shortest-route}.

At that point the light meets the mirror at the same angle on the way in as on the way out. Here is
why. The reflection sends the incoming path $$PM$$ to the dashed path $$P'M$$, and reflections keep
angles (:ref{#isometry-angles}, proved on the [What an isometry keeps](./isometries.mdx) page), so
the incoming path and $$P'M$$ make equal angles with the mirror. The dashed path and the outgoing
path $$MQ$$ lie on one straight line through $$M$$, so they make opposite angles with the mirror,
and opposite angles are equal (:ref{#distance-and-angles/opposite-angles}).

<MirrorLight caption="Light from the lamp bounces off the mirror at equal angles, as if it came in a straight line from the lamp's image behind the glass" />

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on reflections, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-reflections-find-image}

A line $$L$$ is drawn on a page. A point $$P$$ is $$4$$ cm from $$L$$, and $$F$$ is the point of
$$L$$ nearest to $$P$$. A point $$Q$$ of $$L$$ is $$3$$ cm from $$F$$. Let $$P'$$ be the reflection
of $$P$$ in $$L$$. Find $$d(P, P')$$, $$d(Q, P)$$ and $$d(Q, P')$$.

:::solution

The image $$P'$$ is on the line through $$P$$ and $$F$$, on the other side of $$F$$, with
$$d(F, P') = d(F, P) = 4$$ (:ref{#reflection}). Since $$F$$ lies between $$P$$ and $$P'$$,

```math
d(P, P') = d(P, F) + d(F, P') = 4 + 4 = 8 \text{ cm}
```

The triangle $$PFQ$$ has a right angle at $$F$$, with legs $$4$$ and $$3$$, so by the
:ref{#distance-and-angles/pythagoras}, $$d(Q, P) = \sqrt{4^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{25} = 5$$ cm. Since
$$Q$$ is on the mirror, $$d(Q, P') = d(Q, P) = 5$$ cm (:ref{#mirror-distances}).

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-reflections-fixed}

Show that the fixed points (:ref{#fixed-point}) of the reflection in a line $$L$$ are exactly the
points of $$L$$.

:::solution

Every point of $$L$$ is fixed, by the definition (:ref{#reflection}).

Now let $$P$$ be a point not on $$L$$, and let $$F$$ be the point where the perpendicular from $$P$$
meets $$L$$. The image $$P'$$ is on the other side of $$F$$ at the same distance, so $$F$$ is the
midpoint of $$PP'$$ and

```math
d(P, P') = 2\,d(P, F)
```

Since $$P$$ is not on $$L$$, $$P \neq F$$, so $$d(P, F) > 0$$
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/distance-rules}). So $$d(P, P') > 0$$, and $$P' \neq P$$. No point off
$$L$$ is fixed.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-reflections-swap}

The reflection in a line $$L$$ sends a point $$A$$ to a point $$B$$. Where does it send $$B$$?
Explain using :ref{#reflection-twice}.

:::solution

It sends $$B$$ to $$A$$. Here $$R(A) = B$$, so

```math
R(B) = R(R(A)) = A
```

by :ref{#reflection-twice}. So a reflection swaps each point with its image. This is why a mirror
image of your left hand is a right hand, and the mirror image of that is a left hand again.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-reflections-route-numbers}

A path runs along a straight hedge $$L$$. Ana's tent $$P$$ is $$3$$ m from the hedge and the car
$$Q$$ is $$5$$ m from it, on the same side, and their nearest points on the hedge are $$6$$ m apart.
She wants to walk from the tent to the hedge to pick berries, then to the car.

1. How long is the shortest such walk?
2. How long is the walk if she goes to the hedge straight from the tent, then on to the car?

:::solution

1. Reflect the tent in the hedge. The image $$P'$$ is $$3$$ m behind the hedge, so measured square
   to the hedge it is $$3 + 5 = 8$$ m from the car, and $$6$$ m along it. By :ref{#shortest-route}
   and the :ref{#distance-and-angles/pythagoras}, the shortest walk is

   ```math
   d(P', Q) = \sqrt{6^2 + 8^2} = \sqrt{100} = 10 \text{ m}
   ```

2. The first leg is $$3$$ m. The second is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs $$6$$ and
   $$5$$, so the walk is $$3 + \sqrt{36 + 25} = 3 + \sqrt{61} \approx 3 + 7.81 = 10.81$$ m. That is
   about $$0.81$$ m longer than the best walk.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-reflections-opposite-sides}

Let $$P$$ and $$Q$$ be on opposite sides of a line $$L$$, so that the segment $$PQ$$ crosses $$L$$
at a point $$M$$. What is the shortest route from $$P$$ to a point of $$L$$ and on to $$Q$$? Why is
no reflection needed?

:::solution

The route through $$M$$ is the shortest, with length $$d(P, Q)$$. For any point $$X$$ of $$L$$, the
triangle inequality gives $$d(P, X) + d(X, Q) \ge d(P, Q)$$
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/distance-rules}). The route through $$M$$ reaches this bound, because
$$M$$ is on the segment $$PQ$$, so $$d(P, M) + d(M, Q) = d(P, Q)$$
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/segment-rules}, rule 1).

No reflection is needed because the straight segment from $$P$$ to $$Q$$ already touches $$L$$ on
the way. The reflection in :ref{#shortest-route} is a way to make the line lie between the two
points, so that a straight path has to cross it.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-reflections-other-image}

In the river problem, a friend reflects the garden $$Q$$ instead of the cottage $$P$$, and stops
where the segment from $$P$$ to $$Q' = R(Q)$$ crosses the bank. Show that the friend finds the same
stopping point and the same shortest length.

:::solution

For every point $$X$$ of the bank, $$d(X, Q) = d(X, Q')$$ (:ref{#mirror-distances}), so

```math
d(P, X) + d(X, Q) = d(P, X) + d(X, Q') \ge d(P, Q')
```

by the triangle inequality. Let $$N$$ be the point where the segment $$PQ'$$ crosses the bank. As in
the proof of :ref{#shortest-route}, the route through $$N$$ has length exactly $$d(P, Q')$$, and no
other point of the bank does as well. So $$N$$ gives the shortest route, of length $$d(P, Q')$$.

But :ref{#shortest-route} also says the shortest route goes through $$M$$ and through no other point
of the bank, with length $$d(P', Q)$$. Both points give the shortest route, so $$N = M$$, and the
two lengths agree: $$d(P, Q') = d(P', Q)$$.

:::
