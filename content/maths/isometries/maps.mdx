Picture a video game sliding a character across the screen. Every dot of the picture moves at once,
and one rule says where each dot goes. A rule like that, which sends every point of the plane
somewhere, is called a **map of the plane**.

The **plane** here is the one from [Distance and angles](../distance-and-angles/introduction.mdx): a
flat surface, like a sheet of paper with no edges, that runs on without end in every direction. In
this guide we'll build the words the rest of the chapter needs:

- **The image**: where a map sends a point, or a whole shape
- **Equal maps**: when two maps count as the same
- **Fixed points**: points that a map leaves where they are

We'll also meet the zoom, which changes sizes. The rest of the chapter is about the moves that
don't.

## Moving a sheet of tracing paper

Draw a letter F on a page, lay a sheet of tracing paper over it and trace the F. Now move the sheet.
You can slide it across the page, push a pin through it and turn it, or lift it and put it back the
other way up. Each time, every point of the page has a new position: the place where its traced copy
has ended up.

<TracingPaper caption="The three ways to move a sheet of tracing paper: slide it, turn it about a pin, or flip it over. The blue F shows where each point of the page has gone." />

This is the picture to keep in mind for the whole chapter. A move of the sheet doesn't treat one
point differently from another: it sends every point somewhere, all at once. The rule that says
where each point goes is called a map.

## Maps

A map is a rule with one job: for each point of the plane, it names one point where that point ends
up. It doesn't have to come from tracing paper. "Send every point to the point $$2$$ cm to its
right" is a map, and so is "send every point to the corner of the page".

:::definition[Map of the plane]{#map}

A **map** $$F$$ of the plane is a rule that sends each point $$P$$ of the plane to exactly one
point, written $$F(P)$$ and read "$$F$$ of $$P$$". The point $$F(P)$$ is the **image** of $$P$$, and
you also say that $$F$$ **sends** $$P$$ to $$F(P)$$. A map is also called a **mapping**.

:::

Two things are required of a map, and one thing is not:

- **Every point goes somewhere**: the rule can't leave any point out
- **Each point goes to one place**: a point can't be sent to two places at once
- **Points may share a destination**: two different points are allowed to land on the same point

The image is often written with a prime, $$P' = F(P)$$, when it's clear which map is meant. So
$$P'$$ means "where $$P$$ ends up".

:::example[Two maps that hardly move anything]

**The identity.** The map that leaves every point where it is sends each point $$P$$ to $$P$$
itself. It is called the **identity** and written $$I$$, so $$I(P) = P$$ for every point $$P$$. On
the tracing paper, it is putting the sheet straight back where it was.

**A constant map.** Fix a point $$O$$, and send every point of the plane to $$O$$. This is a map:
every point goes to exactly one place, which happens to be the same place for all of them. It
squashes the whole plane down to a single dot, which no sheet of paper can do.

:::

## The image of a shape

A shape, such as a triangle, a circle or a letter F, is made of points. Moving the sheet moves every
point of the traced shape, and together the moved points make a new shape. That new shape is the
image of the old one.

:::definition[Image of a shape]{#image}

Let $$F$$ be a map and $$S$$ a set of points (:ref{#basic-proofs/set}). The **image** of $$S$$,
written $$F(S)$$, is the set of all the images $$F(P)$$ for $$P$$ in $$S$$.

:::

```plane
point P 0 0 sw
point Q 3 0 se
point M 0.5 2 nw
point P' 4 1 sw
point Q' 7 1 se
point M' 4.5 3 nw
polygon P Q M color=blue
segment P Q
segment Q M
segment M P
polygon P' Q' M' color=orange
segment P' Q' color=orange
segment Q' M' color=orange
segment M' P' color=orange
segment P P' color=muted dashed
segment Q Q' color=muted dashed
segment M M' color=muted dashed
caption "A map that moves every point 4 to the right and 1 up sends the triangle PQM to the triangle P'Q'M'"
```

:::example[Images of shapes]

- **The identity**: $$I(S) = S$$ for every shape $$S$$, since no point moves
- **A constant map**: if $$F$$ sends every point to $$O$$, the image of any shape is the single
  point $$O$$. A whole circle and a single dot have the same image.
- **The map in the figure**: it sends every point $$4$$ to the right and $$1$$ up. The image of the
  triangle $$PQM$$ is the triangle $$P'Q'M'$$, the same shape in a new place.

:::

## When two maps are the same

Two different instructions can have the same result. "Turn the sheet a quarter turn to the left" and
"turn it three quarter turns to the right" leave it in the same position. For a map, only the result
matters: where each point ends up, not the words used to describe it.

:::definition[Equal maps]{#map-equality}

Two maps $$F$$ and $$G$$ are **equal**, written $$F = G$$, when $$F(P) = G(P)$$ for every point
$$P$$ of the plane.

:::

So to show that two maps are equal, you check every point. To show that they are different, one
point is enough: a point $$P$$ with $$F(P) \ne G(P)$$ is a counterexample
(:ref{#basic-proofs/counterexample}).

:::example[Same result, different descriptions]

Fix two different points $$O$$ and $$Q$$. Let $$F$$ send every point to $$O$$, and let $$G$$ send
every point to $$Q$$. Then $$F \ne G$$: at any point $$P$$ at all, $$F(P) = O$$ but $$G(P) = Q$$,
and these are different points.

On the other hand, turning the sheet a full turn about a pin and not moving it at all are two
descriptions of one map, the identity. The [Slides and turns](./slides-and-turns.mdx) guide makes
this exact.

:::

## Fixed points

Push a pin through the tracing paper and turn the sheet. Every point moves except one: the point
under the pin stays where it was. A point that a map doesn't move is called a **fixed point**.

Fixed points help tell maps apart. A turn has one fixed point, the pin. A slide moves everything, so
it has none.

:::definition[Fixed point]{#fixed-point}

A point $$P$$ is a **fixed point** of a map $$F$$ when $$F(P) = P$$.

:::

:::example[Fixed points of the maps so far]

- **The identity**: every point is fixed, since $$I(P) = P$$ for all $$P$$
- **A constant map to $$O$$**: $$O$$ is fixed, since $$F(O) = O$$. No other point is: for
  $$P \ne O$$, $$F(P) = O \ne P$$.
- **Moving every point $$4$$ to the right and $$1$$ up**, the map in the triangle figure under
  [The image of a shape](#the-image-of-a-shape): no point is fixed, since every point moves

:::

The next guide looks at turns and slides in detail. There is one exception to "a turn fixes only the
pin": a whole number of full turns puts every point back where it started, so every point is fixed.
Likewise, a slide by a distance of zero moves nothing.

## Zooming: a map that changes sizes

A photocopier set to $$200\%$$, a map drawn to scale and two fingers spreading a photo on a phone
all do the same thing. Everything spreads out from a centre, and every length doubles, but the shape
stays the same. The centre itself stays put, and every other point slides straight out along the
line from the centre.

:::definition[Dilation]{#dilation}

Fix a point $$O$$ and a number $$r > 0$$. The **dilation** by $$r$$ about $$O$$ is the map that
sends $$O$$ to itself and sends each other point $$P$$ to the point $$P'$$ on the ray $$OP$$
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/ray}) with

```math
d(O, P') = r \cdot d(O, P)
```

It is also called a **zoom** or a **stretch** by $$r$$.

:::

There is exactly one such point $$P'$$. A ray starts at $$O$$ and runs on without end, and it has
exactly one point at each distance from $$O$$. For distances up to $$d(O, P)$$, this is rule 2 of
:ref{#distance-and-angles/segment-rules}, applied to the segment $$OP$$. For longer distances, it is
taken from the picture of a ray that never stops.

:::example[Zooming by 2 and by a half]

Let $$P$$ be a point at distance $$3$$ from $$O$$.

- **By 2**: the dilation by $$2$$ about $$O$$ sends $$P$$ to the point $$P'$$ on the ray $$OP$$ at
  distance $$2 \cdot 3 = 6$$ from $$O$$. The point $$P$$ is the midpoint of $$OP'$$.
- **By a half**: the dilation by $$\frac{1}{2}$$ sends $$P$$ to the point on the ray at distance
  $$\frac{3}{2}$$, the midpoint of $$OP$$
- **By 1**: the dilation by $$1$$ sends every point to the point on its own ray at its own distance,
  which is the point itself. So it is the identity.

For $$r \ne 1$$, the only fixed point is $$O$$: any other point $$P$$ ends up at distance
$$r \cdot d(O, P) \ne d(O, P)$$ from $$O$$, so it has moved.

:::

<DilationZoom />

In the diagram, the zoom multiplies every distance on the flag by $$r$$, not only the distances from
$$O$$. So a zoom by $$2$$ doubles every distance, and a zoom by $$\frac{1}{2}$$ halves every
distance. The [Zooming and length](../area-and-scaling/scaling-lengths.mdx) guide, in the Area and
scaling chapter, looks at why this holds.

Moving the tracing paper is different: it never changes a distance. Maps like this are called
**isometries**, and they are the subject of the rest of this chapter. The next guide,
[Slides and turns](./slides-and-turns.mdx), starts with two of them.

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on maps of the plane, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-maps-fixed-points}

Find every fixed point of each map:

1. The dilation by $$3$$ about a point $$O$$
2. The dilation by $$1$$ about $$O$$
3. The map that sends every point to a fixed point $$Q$$
4. The map that sends every point $$5$$ to the left

:::solution

1. **Only $$O$$.** The dilation fixes $$O$$ by definition (:ref{#dilation}). A point $$P \ne O$$
   lands at distance $$3 \cdot d(O, P)$$ from $$O$$, which is not $$d(O, P)$$ since $$d(O, P) > 0$$,
   so $$P$$ moves.
2. **Every point.** The dilation by $$1$$ is the identity, so every point is fixed
   (:ref{#fixed-point}).
3. **Only $$Q$$.** The point $$Q$$ is sent to $$Q$$. Any other point $$P$$ is sent to $$Q \ne P$$.
4. **No point.** Every point ends up $$5$$ away from where it was, so none stays put.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-maps-zoom-back}

A point $$P$$ is at distance $$4$$ from $$O$$. First zoom by $$2$$ about $$O$$, then zoom the result
by $$\frac{1}{2}$$ about $$O$$. Where does $$P$$ end up? What happens to every other point?

:::solution

The zoom by $$2$$ sends $$P$$ to the point $$P'$$ on the ray $$OP$$ at distance $$8$$ from $$O$$
(:ref{#dilation}). The ray $$OP'$$ is the same ray as $$OP$$, so the zoom by $$\frac{1}{2}$$ sends
$$P'$$ to the point of that ray at distance $$\frac{1}{2} \cdot 8 = 4$$. That point is $$P$$, since
a ray has only one point at each distance from its start (:ref{#distance-and-angles/segment-rules}).
So $$P$$ ends up back where it started.

The same happens to every point $$X \ne O$$: its distance becomes $$2 \cdot d(O, X)$$ and then
$$\frac{1}{2} \cdot 2 \cdot d(O, X) = d(O, X)$$, on the same ray. And $$O$$ never moves. So the two
zooms together leave every point where it was: they undo each other.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-maps-two-constants}

Let $$F$$ send every point to $$O$$, and let $$G$$ send every point to $$Q$$. For which points $$O$$
and $$Q$$ is $$F = G$$?

:::solution

Exactly when $$O = Q$$. If $$O = Q$$, then $$F(P) = O = Q = G(P)$$ for every point $$P$$, so
$$F = G$$ (:ref{#map-equality}). If $$O \ne Q$$, then at any point $$P$$, $$F(P) = O$$ and
$$G(P) = Q$$ are different, so $$F \ne G$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-maps-zoom-equal}

Show that the dilation by $$r$$ about $$O$$ and the dilation by $$s$$ about $$O$$ are equal maps
exactly when $$r = s$$.

:::solution

If $$r = s$$, the two definitions are word for word the same, so the maps agree at every point.

Now suppose $$r \ne s$$. Take any point $$P \ne O$$. The first dilation sends it to a point at
distance $$r \cdot d(O, P)$$ from $$O$$, and the second to a point at distance $$s \cdot d(O, P)$$.
Since $$d(O, P) > 0$$, these distances are different, so the two images are different points. One
point where the maps disagree is enough to show they are not equal (:ref{#map-equality}).

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-maps-midpoint-map}

A map $$F$$ sends $$O$$ to itself and sends every other point $$P$$ to the midpoint of the segment
$$OP$$. Which map from this guide is $$F$$?

:::solution

The dilation by $$\frac{1}{2}$$ about $$O$$. The midpoint $$M$$ of $$OP$$ lies on the segment
$$OP$$, so on the ray $$OP$$, and $$d(O, M) = d(M, P)$$ (:ref{#distance-and-angles/midpoint}). These
two distances add up to $$d(O, P)$$ (:ref{#distance-and-angles/segment-rules}), so
$$d(O, M) = \frac{1}{2} d(O, P)$$. That is exactly where the dilation by $$\frac{1}{2}$$ sends $$P$$
(:ref{#dilation}), and both maps fix $$O$$. So they agree at every point, and are equal
(:ref{#map-equality}).

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-maps-circle-image}

Let $$C$$ be the circle with centre $$O$$ and radius $$2$$, and let $$F$$ be the dilation by $$3$$
about $$O$$. Show that $$F(C)$$ is the circle with centre $$O$$ and radius $$6$$.

:::solution

The image $$F(C)$$ is the set of images of the points of $$C$$ (:ref{#image}). Two sets are equal
when each is a subset of the other (:ref{#basic-proofs/set-equality}), so there are two things to
show.

**Every image is on the bigger circle.** A point $$P$$ of $$C$$ has $$d(O, P) = 2$$, so $$F(P)$$ is
at distance $$3 \cdot 2 = 6$$ from $$O$$ (:ref{#dilation}).

**Every point of the bigger circle is an image.** Let $$Q$$ be at distance $$6$$ from $$O$$. The ray
$$OQ$$ has a point $$P$$ at distance $$2$$ from $$O$$ (:ref{#distance-and-angles/segment-rules}),
and $$P$$ is on $$C$$. Then $$F(P)$$ is the point of the ray $$OP$$, which is the ray $$OQ$$, at
distance $$6$$ from $$O$$. That point is $$Q$$, so $$Q = F(P)$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-maps-identity}

A map $$F$$ fixes every point of the plane. Show that $$F$$ is the identity. Then give a map, other
than the identity, whose only fixed point is a given point $$O$$.

:::solution

Every point is fixed, so $$F(P) = P = I(P)$$ for every point $$P$$. So $$F = I$$
(:ref{#map-equality}).

For the second part, the dilation by $$2$$ about $$O$$ works: it fixes $$O$$, and every other point
moves to twice its distance from $$O$$. The constant map to $$O$$ works too.

:::
