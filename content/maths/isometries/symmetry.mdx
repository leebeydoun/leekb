A butterfly, the letter A, a give way sign and a snowflake all look balanced, and the word for that
balance is symmetry. This page makes the idea exact: a symmetry of a shape is a move of the plane
that puts the shape back exactly where it was. With that one definition, the moves from the earlier
pages explain everyday facts. A mattress has exactly four ways to lie on its bed, a square has eight
symmetries, and a fan with curved blades can be turned onto itself but never mirrored.

## What a symmetry is

Trace a shape onto a sheet of tracing paper laid over the page. Now move the sheet: slide it, turn
it about a pin, or lift it and flip it over. If the tracing lands exactly on top of the shape again,
that move is a symmetry of the shape. For the letter A, flipping the sheet over in an upright line
through the middle works. For the letter N, turning the sheet half way round about its middle works.

Leaving the sheet where it is always works too, so every shape has at least one symmetry: the move
that does nothing. A shape is called symmetric when it has some other symmetry as well.

:::definition[Symmetry of a shape]{#symmetry}

Let $$S$$ be a shape, that is, a set of points in the plane. A **symmetry** of $$S$$ is an isometry
$$F$$ (:ref{#isometry}) whose image of $$S$$ is $$S$$ itself (:ref{#image}):

```math
F(S) = S
```

In words: every point of $$S$$ lands on a point of $$S$$, and every point of $$S$$ is landed on by
some point of $$S$$.

- A **line of symmetry**, or mirror line, of $$S$$ is a line $$L$$ whose flip $$R_L$$ is a symmetry
  of $$S$$.
- $$S$$ has **turn symmetry** about a point $$O$$ when some turn about $$O$$, other than the
  identity, is a symmetry of $$S$$.

:::

The words "every point of $$S$$ is landed on" matter. Squashing the whole shape into one of its own
points sends every point of $$S$$ into $$S$$, but it misses the rest of $$S$$, and it isn't an
isometry anyway. A symmetry has to cover the shape exactly, the way the tracing covers the drawing.

:::example[A rectangle]

Take a rectangle $$6$$ wide and $$4$$ high, with its centre at $$O$$. It has four symmetries:

1. **The identity**: nothing moves.
2. **The half turn about $$O$$**: each corner goes to the opposite corner, so the rectangle lands on
   itself.
3. **The flip in the level line through $$O$$**: the top edge and the bottom edge swap places.
4. **The flip in the upright line through $$O$$**: the left edge and the right edge swap places.

A quarter turn is not a symmetry. It would put a long side where a short side was, and an isometry
keeps lengths, so the long side can't fit there. The flips in the diagonals fail too. The flip in
the diagonal $$AC$$ keeps $$A$$ and $$C$$ where they are, so it sends $$B$$ to a point still $$6$$
from $$A$$ and $$4$$ from $$C$$. The corner $$D$$ is the wrong way round, $$4$$ from $$A$$ and $$6$$
from $$C$$, and a careful drawing shows the image of $$B$$ lands above the top edge, outside the
rectangle.

:::

### Letters, signs and logos

Capital letters make good practice, because you can check each one by eye:

- **A**, **M**, **T**, **U**, **V**, **W** and **Y** have one upright mirror line
- **B**, **C**, **D**, **E** and **K** have one level mirror line
- **H**, **I**, **O** and **X** have both, and a half turn as well
- **N**, **S** and **Z** have no mirror line, but a half turn puts each one back on itself
- **F**, **G**, **J**, **L**, **P**, **Q** and **R** have no symmetry except the identity

These depend a little on the lettering: in many fonts the two halves of a B or a K differ in size,
which breaks the mirror. Road signs are designed with symmetry so that they read the same from
either side of the road. A triangular warning sign has three mirror lines and two turns, by
$$120^\circ$$ and $$240^\circ$$. Many logos, like the three chasing arrows of the recycling symbol,
have turns but no mirror lines, because the arrows all point the same way round.

## Combining and undoing symmetries

If one move puts the tracing back on the shape, and a second move also does, then doing one and then
the other still puts it back. Each move starts and ends with the tracing on top of the shape. In the
same way, undoing a symmetry is a symmetry, because the shape was in place before the move too. So
the symmetries of a shape are not a loose list: they hang together, and combining any two of them
gives another one.

:::proposition[Symmetries combine and undo]{#symmetry-combine}

Let $$S$$ be a shape, and let $$F$$ and $$G$$ be symmetries of $$S$$. Then:

1. **Combining**: first $$F$$, then $$G$$, that is $$G \circ F$$ (:ref{#composite}), is a symmetry
   of $$S$$
2. **Undoing**: the inverse $$F^{-1}$$ (:ref{#inverse-map}) is a symmetry of $$S$$
3. **Doing nothing**: the identity $$I$$ is a symmetry of $$S$$

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry_group"}

**Combining.** First, $$G \circ F$$ is an isometry, by :ref{#composite-isometry}. The points of its
image of $$S$$ are the points $$G(F(X))$$ for $$X$$ in $$S$$. These are the images under $$G$$ of
the points of $$F(S)$$. Since $$F(S) = S$$ and $$G(S) = S$$,

```math
(G \circ F)(S) = G(F(S)) = G(S) = S
```

**Undoing.** $$F$$ is an isometry, so it has an inverse, and the inverse is an isometry too
(:ref{#isometry-inverse}). For the image, $$S = F(S)$$, so the points of $$F^{-1}(S)$$ are the
points $$F^{-1}(F(X))$$ for $$X$$ in $$S$$. Each of these is $$X$$ itself, so $$F^{-1}(S) = S$$.

**Doing nothing.** The identity keeps every distance and sends every point to itself, so
$$I(S) = S$$.

:::

So repeating a symmetry, $$F^2 = F \circ F$$ or $$F^3$$ (:ref{#map-powers}), always gives a
symmetry. This is how a shape with one turn symmetry ends up with several: a snowflake that can be
turned by $$60^\circ$$ can also be turned by $$120^\circ$$, $$180^\circ$$ and so on.

### Turning a mattress

Mattress makers advise moving a mattress every few months, so that it wears evenly. Seen from above,
a mattress is a rectangle, so it has the four symmetries of a rectangle. Each one is a way of
putting the mattress back on the bed:

- **Stay**: leave it as it is (the identity)
- **Half turn**: swing it round end to end, keeping the same side up
- **Long flip**: roll it over sideways, so the head stays at the head end
- **Short flip**: turn it over end to end, so the head becomes the foot

Seen from above, the half turn is the half turn about the centre, the long flip is the flip in the
line along the middle of its length, and the short flip is the flip in the line across its middle.
Sew a tag into one corner and each move puts the tag in a different corner.

<MattressPositions caption="Seen from above: each of the four moves leaves the tag in a different corner" />

Doing one move and then another gives one of the four again, by :ref{#symmetry-combine}. The table
says which. Find the first move on the left and the second move along the top:

| First, then | Stay       | Half turn  | Long flip  | Short flip |
| ----------- | ---------- | ---------- | ---------- | ---------- |
| Stay        | Stay       | Half turn  | Long flip  | Short flip |
| Half turn   | Half turn  | Stay       | Short flip | Long flip  |
| Long flip   | Long flip  | Short flip | Stay       | Half turn  |
| Short flip  | Short flip | Long flip  | Half turn  | Stay       |

Every entry can be checked by following the tag. For example, after a half turn the tag is in the
opposite corner; a long flip then moves it across the long middle line, which leaves it where a
short flip on its own would have put it. Two crossing mirrors make a turn by twice the angle between
them (:ref{#crossing-mirrors}), and that explains the last two rows: the two middle lines cross at
$$90^\circ$$, so the two flips together make a turn by $$180^\circ$$.

The table answers a practical question. If every few months you swap between a half turn and a long
flip, the mattress goes through stay, half turn, short flip, long flip and back to stay. So it
visits all four positions. Doing the half turn every time would only ever use two of them.

## Symmetries of regular shapes

A square, an equilateral triangle and the hexagon at the heart of a snowflake are all **regular**:
their corners sit equally spaced round a circle, joined in order. Turning one of them by the angle
between neighbouring corners moves each corner on to the next one, so the shape lands on itself.
Repeat that turn and the corners keep stepping round, until after a whole lap everything is back
where it started.

:::proposition[Turning a regular shape]{#regular-turns}

Let $$S$$ be a regular shape with $$n$$ corners, for some $$n \ge 3$$: its corners lie on a circle
with centre $$O$$, and going round anticlockwise, each corner is reached from the one before by
turning $$\frac{360}{n}^\circ$$ about $$O$$. Its sides join each corner to the next. Let $$G$$ be
the turn about $$O$$ by $$\frac{360}{n}^\circ$$. Then:

1. $$G$$ is a symmetry of $$S$$
2. $$G^n = I$$, while $$G, G^2, \ldots, G^{n-1}$$ are all different from $$I$$ and from each other
3. $$G^{-1} = G^{n-1}$$

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotational_symmetry"}

**A symmetry.** Each corner is at the same distance from $$O$$ as the next, with an angle of
$$\frac{360}{n}^\circ$$ between them at $$O$$, so $$G$$ sends each corner to the next one
(:ref{#rotation}), and the last corner to the first. $$G$$ is an isometry (:ref{#basic-isometries}),
so it sends each side, a segment between two neighbouring corners, to the segment between their
images (:ref{#isometry-segments}). That is the next side. Every side is the image of the side before
it, so $$G$$ sends the sides of $$S$$ onto all the sides of $$S$$, and $$G(S) = S$$.

**Powers.** Turning by $$x^\circ$$ and then by $$y^\circ$$ is turning by $$(x + y)^\circ$$
(:ref{#rotation-rules}), so $$G^k$$ is the turn by $$k \cdot \frac{360}{n}^\circ$$. For $$k = n$$
that is a turn by $$360^\circ$$, which is $$I$$. For $$k = 1, \ldots, n - 1$$ the angles
$$k \cdot \frac{360}{n}$$ are all different and lie between $$0$$ and $$360$$. Take a corner $$P$$.
By :ref{#rotation-rules} exactly one turn by an angle from $$0^\circ$$ up to $$360^\circ$$ takes
$$P$$ to any given point at its distance from $$O$$, so different angles send $$P$$ to different
points. So $$I, G, \ldots, G^{n-1}$$ all send $$P$$ to different places, and no two of them are
equal.

**The inverse.** $$G^n = I$$, so $$G^{-1} = G^{n-1}$$ by :ref{#inverse-by-repeating}.

:::

:::example[The square]

For a square, $$n = 4$$ and $$G$$ is a quarter turn. Its turn symmetries are $$I$$ and the turns by
$$90^\circ$$, $$180^\circ$$ and $$270^\circ$$, and undoing a quarter turn is the same as doing three
more: $$G^{-1} = G^3$$. The square also has four mirror lines: the two lines through the middles of
opposite sides, and the two diagonals. That makes eight symmetries.

There are no more, and here is why, taking one fact from the picture. The two diagonals are the
longest segments that fit inside a square, and their ends are the corners. A symmetry keeps
distances and puts the square back on itself, so it must send the ends of a longest segment to the
ends of a longest segment: corners go to corners. Proving this fact carefully takes more work, so
here it is taken as given.

With that, count. A corner $$A$$ can go to any of the four corners. Its neighbour $$B$$ is one side
length away, so it must go to a corner one side length from wherever $$A$$ went: one of the two
neighbours. Once $$A$$ and $$B$$ are placed, the corner $$D$$ on the other side of $$A$$ has only
one place left. An isometry is decided by where it sends three points not on one line
(:ref{#three-points-decide}), so there are at most $$4 \cdot 2 = 8$$ symmetries, and all eight are
listed above.

:::

Try it below. The flag makes every position look different, so you can see when you have found a new
one. Notice that a quarter turn and then a flip don't give the same as a flip and then a quarter
turn: for a square, unlike the mattress, the order matters.

<SquareSymmetries />

The same count works for any regular shape with $$n$$ corners: it has $$2n$$ symmetries, the $$n$$
turns from :ref{#regular-turns}, counting the identity, and $$n$$ flips. The argument is the one
used for the square, but this page only carries it out for the square, so for other regular shapes
treat it as a fact to check by eye. The equilateral triangle has $$6$$, the square $$8$$, and the
regular hexagon $$12$$.

## Mirrors force turns

When a shape has two mirror lines that cross, it automatically has a turn symmetry too. The two
flips are symmetries, so doing one and then the other is a symmetry (:ref{#symmetry-combine}). Two
crossing mirrors make a turn about their crossing point by twice the angle between them
(:ref{#crossing-mirrors}). So mirrors that meet at $$\theta^\circ$$ give a turn by
$$2\theta^\circ$$.

:::example[Mirrors and turns]

- **The letter H**: its two mirror lines cross at $$90^\circ$$, so the H has a turn by
  $$180^\circ$$, the half turn.
- **The equilateral triangle**: neighbouring mirror lines cross at $$60^\circ$$, which gives a turn
  by $$120^\circ$$, the turn that moves each corner on to the next.
- **The snowflake**: a snowflake has six arms, and so six mirror lines, three along the arms and
  three between them. Neighbouring mirror lines cross at $$30^\circ$$, which gives a turn by
  $$60^\circ$$. Repeating it gives the turns by $$120^\circ$$, $$180^\circ$$, $$240^\circ$$ and
  $$300^\circ$$, so with the identity the snowflake has six turns and six flips: twelve symmetries.

:::

point M 1.732 -1 se

The reverse is not true: a shape can have turns without any mirror. A turn keeps the direction in
which things go round, while a flip reverses it (:ref{#isometry-angles}). A fan whose blades curve
round in one direction looks the same after a turn by a third of a full turn, but in a mirror its
blades would curve the other way. A hubcap with straight spokes has the same turns, and mirror lines
as well.

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on symmetry, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-symmetry-letters}

Drawn in plain block capitals, which symmetries do the letters E, N, X and the digit 8 have? For
each, list the mirror lines and the turns, other than the identity.

:::solution

- **E**: one level mirror line through the middle bar. No turn: after a half turn the bars would
  point left.
- **N**: no mirror line, since a mirror would make the slanted stroke lean the other way. The half
  turn about its centre puts it back on itself.
- **X**: an upright and a level mirror line. Two crossing mirrors make a turn by twice the angle
  between them (:ref{#crossing-mirrors}), so it also has the half turn.
- **8**: drawn as two equal loops, it has an upright and a level mirror line, and so the half turn
  as well.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-symmetry-rectangle-quarter}

A rectangle is $$5$$ wide and $$2$$ high. Explain why a quarter turn about its centre is not a
symmetry. Does the same argument work for a square?

:::solution

A quarter turn is an isometry, so it keeps lengths (:ref{#isometry}). It would send the bottom side,
of length $$5$$, to an upright segment of length $$5$$. But the upright sides of the rectangle have
length $$2$$, and the rectangle has no other upright segment of length $$5$$ on its edge, so the
image of the rectangle is not the rectangle.

For a square every side has the same length, so the argument fails, and indeed a quarter turn is a
symmetry of a square (:ref{#regular-turns}).

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-symmetry-mattress-plans}

Use the mattress table to answer these.

1. If you always do a long flip, which positions does the mattress ever reach?
2. If you alternate a short flip and a half turn, starting with a short flip, list the positions in
   order until the mattress is back where it started.
3. Is there a single move that, repeated, reaches all four positions?

:::solution

1. Long flip, then long flip again gives stay, by the table. So the mattress swaps between stay and
   long flip, and only ever reaches those two positions.
2. Start at stay. A short flip gives short flip. A half turn after a short flip gives long flip. A
   short flip after a long flip gives half turn. A half turn after a half turn gives stay. So the
   order is stay, short flip, long flip, half turn, stay: all four positions.
3. No. The table shows that each move done twice gives stay, so repeating one move only ever swaps
   between two positions.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-symmetry-pentagon}

Let $$G$$ be the turn by $$72^\circ$$ about the centre of a regular pentagon.

1. What is the smallest positive $$n$$ with $$G^n = I$$?
2. Write $$G^{-1}$$ as a positive power of $$G$$, and say which turn it is.
3. How many symmetries does the regular pentagon have?

:::solution

1. A regular pentagon has $$5$$ corners and $$\frac{360}{5} = 72$$. By :ref{#regular-turns},
   $$G^5 = I$$ while $$G, G^2, G^3, G^4$$ are not $$I$$, so $$n = 5$$.
2. $$G^{-1} = G^4$$, which is the turn by $$4 \cdot 72 = 288^\circ$$. Turning a further
   $$288^\circ$$ anticlockwise is the same as turning back $$72^\circ$$, as it should be.
3. Five turns (counting the identity) and five mirror lines, one through each corner and the middle
   of the opposite side: $$10$$ symmetries.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-symmetry-powers}

Let $$F$$ be a symmetry of a shape $$S$$. Prove that $$F^3$$ and $$F^{-2}$$, that is
$$F^{-1} \circ F^{-1}$$, are symmetries of $$S$$.

:::solution

By :ref{#symmetry-combine}, combining two symmetries gives a symmetry. So $$F^2 = F \circ F$$ is a
symmetry, and then $$F^3 = F \circ F^2$$ is too.

By the same proposition, $$F^{-1}$$ is a symmetry. Combining it with itself, $$F^{-1} \circ F^{-1}$$
is a symmetry as well.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-symmetry-two-mirrors}

A shape has two mirror lines that cross at its centre at an angle of $$45^\circ$$.

1. Which turn symmetry must it have?
2. How many different turn symmetries does that give, counting the identity?

:::solution

1. The two flips are symmetries, so one after the other is a symmetry (:ref{#symmetry-combine}). Two
   crossing mirrors make a turn about their crossing point by twice the angle between them
   (:ref{#crossing-mirrors}), so the shape has the turn by $$90^\circ$$ about its centre.
2. Repeating the quarter turn gives the turns by $$90^\circ$$, $$180^\circ$$, $$270^\circ$$ and
   $$360^\circ$$, which is the identity (:ref{#rotation-rules}). So there are at least $$4$$ turn
   symmetries. A square with its two diagonal mirror lines and a middle line is an example.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-symmetry-strip}

A border pattern runs along a wall without end. It has two upright mirror lines that are $$15$$ cm
apart. Show that sliding the pattern along the wall by $$30$$ cm puts it back on itself.

:::solution

The two flips are symmetries, so flipping in the first and then in the second is a symmetry
(:ref{#symmetry-combine}). Two parallel mirrors make a slide square to the mirrors, by twice the gap
between them (:ref{#parallel-mirrors}). The mirrors are upright, so the slide runs along the wall,
and its length is $$2 \cdot 15 = 30$$ cm.

This is why a pattern with two parallel mirror lines has to repeat for ever: a finite drawing can't
be put back on itself by a slide.

:::
