---
title: Slides and turns
description:
  Translations, which slide the plane along an arrow, and rotations, which turn it about a point.
---

Imagine pushing a book across a table without letting it twist. Every corner moves the same way, by
the same amount. Now imagine opening a door instead: the hinges stay put and the rest of the door
swings round them. Mathematicians call the first move a translation and the second a rotation. In
this guide we'll get a feel for both, and then describe them exactly.

Both are ways to move the sheet of tracing paper from [Maps of the plane](./maps.mdx). One turn gets
special attention: the half turn, which carries every point straight through the centre to the other
side.

## Slides

Slide the tracing paper across the page without letting it turn. Every traced point moves, and they
all move the same distance in the same direction. That is what happens to the boxes on a conveyor
belt: big or small, near the front or near the back, each one is carried the same way by the same
amount.

<ConveyorBelt caption="On a conveyor belt every box is carried the same distance in the same direction" />

So a whole slide is described by one arrow: how far, and which way. Any point can play the part of
the arrow's start, since every point makes the same trip.

:::definition[Translation]{#translation}

Let $$O$$ and $$M$$ be points, and think of the **arrow** from $$O$$ to $$M$$: it has length
$$d(O, M)$$ and points from $$O$$ towards $$M$$. The **translation** by this arrow is the map $$T$$
that sends each point $$P$$ to the point $$T(P)$$ reached from $$P$$ by moving the distance
$$d(O, M)$$ in the direction from $$O$$ to $$M$$. It is also called a **slide**.

:::

"Direction" is used here in its everyday sense, the same way as on a compass. The
[Rays and direction](../segments-rays-and-lines/rays.mdx) guide makes it exact with numbers, but
nothing in this chapter needs more than the picture.

Three things follow straight from the definition:

- **The arrow's start goes to its tip**: $$T(O) = M$$, since moving from $$O$$ along the arrow ends
  at $$M$$
- **The empty arrow**: the arrow from $$O$$ to $$O$$ has length $$0$$, so it moves nothing. Its
  translation is the identity $$I$$.
- **No fixed points**: a translation by an arrow of length more than $$0$$ moves every point that
  distance, so no point stays where it was (:ref{#fixed-point})

<TranslationSlide />

Two facts about slides are taken as given. The first says that a slide is decided by where it sends
one point. The second says that two slides in a row make one slide: put the second arrow's start on
the first arrow's tip, and the slide from the first start to the last tip does both at once.

:::remark[Rules for translations]{#translation-rules}

1. **One slide for each pair**: for any points $$P$$ and $$Q$$, there is exactly one translation
   that sends $$P$$ to $$Q$$, the translation by the arrow from $$P$$ to $$Q$$
2. **Slides combine**: a translation followed by another translation is a translation

These are taken as given.

::see-also{src="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translation_(geometry)"}

:::

:::example[A robot's two slides]

A warehouse robot slides $$6$$ m east, then $$8$$ m north, without turning. By
:ref{#translation-rules}, the two slides together are one slide, by the arrow from where it started
to where it ended. East and north are at right angles, so its start, its corner and its finish make
a right triangle with legs $$6$$ and $$8$$. By :ref{#distance-and-angles/pythagoras}, the single
slide has length

```math
\sqrt{6^2 + 8^2} = \sqrt{100} = 10 \text{ m}
```

Every part of the robot, not only its centre, moves $$10$$ m in that one direction.

:::

## Turns

Now push a pin through the tracing paper at a point $$O$$ and turn the sheet. The pin stays put.
Every other point travels round a circle centred at $$O$$, keeping its distance from the pin, and
all of them turn through the same angle. The tip of a clock's minute hand does this, and so does
every point of a steering wheel.

Angles are measured as on the [Measuring angles](../distance-and-angles/measuring-angles.mdx) page,
in degrees, as a share of a full turn. By convention a turn goes **anticlockwise**, the opposite way
to a clock's hands, unless it says otherwise.

:::definition[Rotation]{#rotation}

Fix a point $$O$$, the **centre**, and a number $$x$$ with $$0 \le x < 360$$. The **rotation** by
$$x^\circ$$ about $$O$$, written $$G_x$$, sends $$O$$ to itself and sends each other point $$P$$ to
the point $$P' = G_x(P)$$ with:

- **Same distance**: $$d(O, P') = d(O, P)$$
- **Turned by $$x^\circ$$**: the angle turning anticlockwise from the ray $$OP$$ to the ray $$OP'$$
  measures $$x^\circ$$ (:ref{#distance-and-angles/degree})

It is also called a **turn**.

:::

```plane
point O 0 0 sw
point P 3 0 se
point P' 1.5 2.6 n
circle O 3 color=muted dashed
segment O P
segment O P'
angle P O P' color=orange arrow label="x°"
caption "The turn by x° about O sends P to P', the same distance from O and turned anticlockwise by x°"
```

The definition covers one trip of less than a full turn, anticlockwise. A turn that goes the other
way, or goes round more than once, is written with a number outside $$0 \le x < 360$$:

- **Clockwise**: a turn by $$x^\circ$$ **clockwise** is written $$G_{-x}$$, with a negative number.
  So $$G_{-90}$$ is a quarter turn clockwise.
- **More than a full turn**: for $$x \ge 360$$, $$G_x$$ keeps turning anticlockwise until it has
  gone round $$x^\circ$$ in all: some whole turns, and then a part of one. So $$G_{450}$$ is one
  whole turn and then a quarter turn.

The facts below are taken as given. The first says that a turn about $$O$$ can take any point to any
other point at the same distance from $$O$$, and in only one way. The second says that turning twice
adds the angles, and it holds for these negative and large angles too. The third says that a full
turn changes nothing.

:::remark[Rules for rotations]{#rotation-rules}

Fix a centre $$O$$.

1. **One turn for each pair**: if $$d(O, P) = d(O, Q)$$ and $$P \ne O$$, there is exactly one turn
   $$G_x$$ about $$O$$ with $$0 \le x < 360$$ that sends $$P$$ to $$Q$$
2. **Turns add**: turning by $$x^\circ$$ and then by $$y^\circ$$ has the same effect as turning by
   $$(x + y)^\circ$$, for any numbers $$x$$ and $$y$$
3. **A full turn**: $$G_{360}$$ is the identity, and so is $$G_0$$

These are taken as given.

::see-also{src="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_(mathematics)"}

:::

## Whole turns make no difference

After a minute hand has gone right round, it points where it started. So turning by $$500^\circ$$
ends where turning by $$140^\circ$$ does: the first $$360^\circ$$ bring every point back home, and
only the last $$140^\circ$$ show. In the same way, turning $$90^\circ$$ clockwise leaves things
where turning $$270^\circ$$ anticlockwise does, since the two together make a full turn.

:::proposition[Turns that differ by whole turns]{#rotation-whole-turns}

Let $$x$$ be a number and $$n$$ an integer. Then $$G_{x + 360n} = G_x$$. So every turn equals a turn
$$G_w$$ with $$0 \le w < 360$$, where $$x = 360n + w$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turn_(angle)"}

**When $$n$$ is $$0$$ or positive.** By rule 2 of :ref{#rotation-rules}, turning by
$$(x + 360n)^\circ$$ has the same effect as turning by $$x^\circ$$ and then by $$360^\circ$$, $$n$$
times over. Each turn by $$360^\circ$$ is the identity (rule 3), so it leaves every point where it
was. What is left is the turn by $$x^\circ$$, so $$G_{x + 360n} = G_x$$ (:ref{#map-equality}).

**When $$n$$ is negative.** Write $$y = x + 360n$$ and $$m = -n$$, which is positive. Then
$$x = y + 360m$$, and the first case, applied to $$y$$ and $$m$$, gives
$$G_x = G_{y + 360m} = G_y$$. That is $$G_x = G_{x + 360n}$$.

**The last part.** Every number $$x$$ can be written as $$x = 360n + w$$ with $$n$$ an integer and
$$0 \le w < 360$$: take $$n$$ to be the largest integer with $$360n \le x$$. Then $$G_x = G_w$$ by
what was shown above, with $$w = x - 360n$$.

:::

:::example[Writing turns as whole turns plus a part]

- $$500 = 360 + 140$$, so $$G_{500} = G_{140}$$
- $$-90 = -360 + 270$$, so $$G_{-90} = G_{270}$$: a quarter turn clockwise is three quarter turns
  anticlockwise
- $$-210 = -360 + 150$$, so $$G_{-210} = G_{150}$$
- $$720 = 2 \cdot 360 + 0$$, so $$G_{720} = G_0 = I$$: two full turns change nothing

:::

## Half turns

Turn a king from a pack of cards upside down and it looks the same. Each point of the design has
gone round half a circle about the centre of the card, which carries it straight through the centre
to the point opposite, as far out on the other side.

<HalfTurnCard caption="A half turn about the centre O sends each point P straight through O to the point P' opposite, the same distance from O" />

That description, "straight through the centre to the same distance on the other side", doesn't
mention turning at all. It is a map in its own right, and the next proposition shows it is the same
map as the half turn.

:::definition[Reflection through a point]{#point-reflection}

Fix a point $$O$$. The **reflection through** $$O$$ sends $$O$$ to itself and sends each other point
$$P$$ to the point $$P'$$ on the line $$OP$$, on the other side of $$O$$ from $$P$$, with
$$d(O, P') = d(O, P)$$. In other words, $$O$$ is the midpoint of $$PP'$$
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/midpoint}).

:::

:::proposition[A half turn is a reflection through the centre]{#half-turn}

The rotation $$G_{180}$$ about $$O$$ equals the reflection through $$O$$. So does $$G_{-180}$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_reflection"}

Both maps send $$O$$ to $$O$$. Let $$P \ne O$$, and let $$P' = G_{180}(P)$$.

By the definition of a turn (:ref{#rotation}), $$d(O, P') = d(O, P)$$, and the angle from the ray
$$OP$$ to the ray $$OP'$$ measures $$180^\circ$$. An angle of $$180^\circ$$ is a straight angle
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/straight-angle}), so the rays $$OP$$ and $$OP'$$ point in opposite
directions along one line (:ref{#distance-and-angles/special-angles}). So $$P'$$ is on the line
$$OP$$, on the other side of $$O$$ from $$P$$, at the same distance from $$O$$. That is exactly
where the reflection through $$O$$ sends $$P$$ (:ref{#point-reflection}).

The two maps agree at every point, so they are equal (:ref{#map-equality}). Finally,
$$-180 = -360 + 180$$, so $$G_{-180} = G_{180}$$ by :ref{#rotation-whole-turns}.

:::

So the direction of a half turn makes no difference: half a turn clockwise and half a turn
anticlockwise end in the same place. Its only fixed point is the centre $$O$$, since every other
point lands on the far side of $$O$$.

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on slides and turns, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-slides-turns-reduce}

Write each turn as $$G_w$$ with $$0 \le w < 360$$:

1. $$G_{750}$$
2. $$G_{-45}$$
3. $$G_{-400}$$
4. $$G_{1080}$$

:::solution

Each time, write the angle as $$360n + w$$ with $$0 \le w < 360$$, and use
:ref{#rotation-whole-turns}:

1. $$750 = 2 \cdot 360 + 30$$, so $$G_{750} = G_{30}$$
2. $$-45 = -360 + 315$$, so $$G_{-45} = G_{315}$$
3. $$-400 = -2 \cdot 360 + 320$$, so $$G_{-400} = G_{320}$$
4. $$1080 = 3 \cdot 360 + 0$$, so $$G_{1080} = G_0$$, the identity

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-slides-turns-clock}

In $$25$$ minutes, how far does a clock's minute hand turn, and in which direction? Write this turn
as $$G_w$$ with $$0 \le w < 360$$.

:::solution

The minute hand goes once round, $$360^\circ$$, in $$60$$ minutes, so in $$25$$ minutes it turns
$$\frac{25}{60} \cdot 360 = 150$$ degrees. Clock hands go clockwise, so this is $$G_{-150}$$
(:ref{#rotation}). Since $$-150 = -360 + 210$$, it equals $$G_{210}$$ (:ref{#rotation-whole-turns}):
a turn by $$210^\circ$$ anticlockwise.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-slides-turns-undo}

Which turn about $$O$$ undoes $$G_{70}$$, so that doing $$G_{70}$$ and then it leaves every point
where it was? Give the answer as $$G_w$$ with $$0 \le w < 360$$.

:::solution

Turning by $$70^\circ$$ and then by $$-70^\circ$$ has the same effect as turning by $$0^\circ$$
(rule 2 of :ref{#rotation-rules}), which is the identity (rule 3). So $$G_{-70}$$ undoes $$G_{70}$$.
Since $$-70 = -360 + 290$$, this is $$G_{290}$$ (:ref{#rotation-whole-turns}).

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-slides-turns-quarter-distance}

A point $$P$$ is $$5$$ from $$O$$, and $$P' = G_{90}(P)$$ for the turn about $$O$$. Find
$$d(P, P')$$. What would it be for $$G_{180}$$?

:::solution

**For $$G_{90}$$.** By the definition of a turn (:ref{#rotation}), $$d(O, P') = 5$$ and the angle
$$\angle POP'$$ measures $$90^\circ$$, a right angle. So $$POP'$$ is a right triangle with legs
$$5$$ and $$5$$, and by :ref{#distance-and-angles/pythagoras},

```math
d(P, P') = \sqrt{5^2 + 5^2} = \sqrt{50} = 5\sqrt{2} \approx 7.07
```

**For $$G_{180}$$.** A half turn is the reflection through $$O$$ (:ref{#half-turn}), so $$O$$ is the
midpoint of $$PP'$$. Then $$d(P, P') = 5 + 5 = 10$$ (:ref{#distance-and-angles/segment-rules}).

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-slides-turns-fixed}

Find every fixed point (:ref{#fixed-point}) of each map:

1. A translation by an arrow of length $$3$$
2. The turn $$G_{90}$$ about $$O$$
3. The turn $$G_{360}$$ about $$O$$
4. The reflection through $$O$$

:::solution

1. **None.** Every point moves a distance $$3$$ (:ref{#translation}), so none stays put.
2. **Only $$O$$.** The centre is fixed by definition. A point $$P \ne O$$ is sent to a point $$P'$$
   with the angle from the ray $$OP$$ to the ray $$OP'$$ equal to $$90^\circ$$. If $$P'$$ were
   $$P$$, the two rays would be the same, making a zero angle, not $$90^\circ$$.
3. **Every point.** $$G_{360}$$ is the identity (:ref{#rotation-rules}, rule 3).
4. **Only $$O$$.** Any other point $$P$$ lands on the other side of $$O$$ (:ref{#point-reflection}),
   so it has moved.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-slides-turns-half-twice}

Show that doing the reflection through $$O$$ twice leaves every point where it was. Then explain the
same fact without using turns at all.

:::solution

**With turns.** The reflection through $$O$$ is $$G_{180}$$ (:ref{#half-turn}). Doing it twice is
turning by $$180^\circ$$ and then $$180^\circ$$, which is turning by $$360^\circ$$ (rule 2 of
:ref{#rotation-rules}), and that is the identity (rule 3).

**Without turns.** Let $$P \ne O$$ and $$P'$$ its reflection through $$O$$, so $$O$$ is the midpoint
of $$PP'$$ (:ref{#point-reflection}). Reflecting $$P'$$ through $$O$$ gives the point on the line
$$OP'$$, on the other side of $$O$$ from $$P'$$, at distance $$d(O, P') = d(O, P)$$. The point $$P$$
is such a point, and there is only one, since a ray has one point at each distance from its start
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/segment-rules}). So $$P$$ comes back to $$P$$, and $$O$$ stays put
throughout.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-slides-turns-two-slides}

A slide sends $$P$$ to $$Q$$, and a second slide sends $$Q$$ to $$M$$. Explain why doing the first
and then the second is the slide by the arrow from $$P$$ to $$M$$.

:::solution

By rule 2 of :ref{#translation-rules}, the first slide followed by the second is some translation.
It sends $$P$$ to $$Q$$ and then $$Q$$ to $$M$$, so it sends $$P$$ to $$M$$. By rule 1, exactly one
translation sends $$P$$ to $$M$$: the translation by the arrow from $$P$$ to $$M$$. So the two
slides together are that one.

:::
