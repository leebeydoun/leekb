---
title: Every isometry is a slide, turn or flip
description:
  Why every rigid move of the plane is built from a slide, a turn and at most one flip, and why it
  can always be undone.
---

Suppose someone moves your sheet of tracing paper while you aren't looking. They can slide it, turn
it, flip it over, and repeat any of these as often as they like. However complicated their moves
were, you can always put the sheet back with at most three of your own: one slide, one turn and
possibly one flip.

This page proves that. It means slides, turns and flips are all the rigid moves there are: no
isometry of the plane does anything stranger. It also means every isometry can be undone.

## The idea: putting the sheet back

Here is how you would put the sheet back by hand. Pick a point on it and **slide** the sheet until
that point is home. Then hold that point down with a pin and **turn** the sheet about it until a
second point is home too. Now two points are home, and the sheet can only be either exactly right or
the mirror image of right. If a third point is on the wrong side, lift the sheet and **flip** it
over the line through the two pinned points.

A result from an earlier page explains why three points are enough. An isometry that leaves three
points not on one line where they are moves nothing at all (:ref{#three-fixed-points}). So once
three such points are home, the whole sheet is home. Try it below with different kinds of move.

<PutItBack />

The rest of the page turns this story into proofs. It runs backwards, from the most pins to the
fewest: first an isometry that fixes two points, then one that fixes one point, then any isometry.
Each step uses the one before it.

## Two pins: stay or flip

Pin a sheet at two points $$P$$ and $$Q$$. It can't slide, since $$P$$ is held, and it can't turn,
since turning about $$P$$ would move $$Q$$. The only thing left is to lift it and lay it back face
down, flipped over the line through the two pins. So a sheet pinned at two points is either back
exactly as it was, or flipped in the line through the pins.

:::theorem[Isometries that fix two points]{#fix-two-points}

Let $$F$$ be an isometry (:ref{#isometry}) and $$P$$, $$Q$$ two different points with $$F(P) = P$$
and $$F(Q) = Q$$. Let $$L$$ be the line through $$P$$ and $$Q$$. Then either $$F$$ is the identity
$$I$$, or $$F$$ is the reflection in $$L$$.

:::

```plane
point P -2 0 sw
point Q 2 0 se
point M 0.6 2 n
point M' 0.6 -2 s
line P Q label="L"
segment P M color=blue dashed
segment Q M color=orange dashed
segment P M' color=blue dashed
segment Q M' color=orange dashed
caption "M' is as far from P as M is, and as far from Q as M is, so L is the perpendicular bisector of MM'"
```

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_plane_isometry#Three_mirrors_suffice"}

**Step 1: watch a third point.** Pick a point $$M$$ that is not on $$L$$, and let $$M' = F(M)$$.
Since $$F$$ keeps distances and leaves $$P$$ and $$Q$$ where they are,

```math
d(P, M') = d(P, M) \qquad \text{and} \qquad d(Q, M') = d(Q, M)
```

**Step 2: if $$M$$ stays put, nothing moves.** If $$M' = M$$, then $$F$$ fixes $$P$$, $$Q$$ and
$$M$$, three points not on one line. By :ref{#three-fixed-points}, $$F = I$$.

**Step 3: otherwise $$M'$$ is the mirror image of $$M$$.** Suppose $$M' \ne M$$. Step 1 says $$P$$
is the same distance from $$M$$ as from $$M'$$, since both equal $$d(P, M)$$, so $$P$$ lies on the
perpendicular bisector of $$MM'$$ (:ref{#distance-and-angles/perpendicular-bisector-rule}). The same
goes for $$Q$$. The perpendicular bisector is a line through the two different points $$P$$ and
$$Q$$, and only one line does that (:ref{#distance-and-angles/line-rules}), so the perpendicular
bisector of $$MM'$$ is $$L$$. That is exactly what it means for $$M'$$ to be the reflection of $$M$$
in $$L$$ (:ref{#reflection}). Write $$R$$ for the reflection in $$L$$, so $$R(M) = M'$$ and
$$R(M') = M$$.

**Step 4: flip back and compare.** Consider first $$F$$, then $$R$$, the map $$R \circ F$$. It is an
isometry (:ref{#composite-isometry}). It fixes $$P$$ and $$Q$$, since $$F$$ leaves them where they
are and they lie on the mirror. It also fixes $$M$$: $$F$$ sends $$M$$ to $$M'$$, and $$R$$ sends
$$M'$$ back to $$M$$. So by :ref{#three-fixed-points}, $$R \circ F = I$$.

**Step 5: so $$F$$ is the flip.** Flipping twice in $$L$$ moves nothing (:ref{#reflection-twice}),
so

```math
F = I \circ F = (R \circ R) \circ F = R \circ (R \circ F) = R \circ I = R
```

The regrouping in the middle is allowed by :ref{#composite-associative}.

:::

## One pin: a turn, perhaps after a flip

Now pin the sheet at one point $$O$$. It can turn about the pin, and it can be flipped over a line
through the pin, but it can't slide. To put it back, turn it until a second point is home, and then
you have two pins, which the previous section has already handled.

:::theorem[Isometries that fix one point]{#fix-one-point}

Let $$F$$ be an isometry with $$F(O) = O$$. Then either $$F$$ is a turn about $$O$$, or $$F$$ is
"first a flip in a line through $$O$$, then a turn about $$O$$".

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_plane_isometry#Three_mirrors_suffice"}

**Step 1: pick a second point.** Take any point $$P \ne O$$ and let $$P' = F(P)$$. Since $$F$$ keeps
distances and fixes $$O$$, $$d(O, P') = d(O, P)$$: the point $$P'$$ is on the circle about $$O$$
through $$P$$.

**Step 2: turn it home.** By :ref{#rotation-rules}, there is a turn $$G$$ about $$O$$ with
$$G(P) = P'$$. (If $$P' = P$$, this is the turn by $$0^\circ$$, which moves nothing.) Its inverse
$$G^{-1}$$ is the turn back by the same angle (:ref{#inverse-map}), and it sends $$P'$$ to $$P$$.

**Step 3: now two points are home.** Consider first $$F$$, then turn back: the map
$$H = G^{-1} \circ F$$. It is an isometry, since turns keep distances (:ref{#basic-isometries}) and
so do composites (:ref{#composite-isometry}). It fixes $$O$$, since both $$F$$ and the turn leave
$$O$$ where it is. It fixes $$P$$, since $$F$$ sends $$P$$ to $$P'$$ and $$G^{-1}$$ sends $$P'$$
back to $$P$$. By :ref{#fix-two-points}, $$H = I$$ or $$H = R$$, the reflection in the line $$OP$$.

**Step 4: put the turn back on.** Doing $$G$$ after $$H$$ gives back $$F$$:

```math
G \circ H = G \circ (G^{-1} \circ F) = (G \circ G^{-1}) \circ F = I \circ F = F
```

So if $$H = I$$, then $$F = G \circ I = G$$, a turn. If $$H = R$$, then $$F = G \circ R$$: first the
flip in the line $$OP$$, which passes through $$O$$, then the turn $$G$$ about $$O$$.

:::

The second case can be said more neatly. On the [Combining moves](./combining-moves.mdx) page, two
mirrors crossing at $$O$$ make a turn about $$O$$ by twice the angle between them
(:ref{#crossing-mirrors}). Running that backwards turns "a flip, then a turn" into a single flip.

:::example[A flip then a turn is a flip]

Let $$L$$ be a line through $$O$$, let $$R_L$$ be the flip in $$L$$, and let $$G_x$$ be the turn
about $$O$$ by $$x^\circ$$. Let $$K$$ be the line through $$O$$ that makes an angle of
$$\frac{x}{2}$$ degrees with $$L$$, measured anticlockwise from $$L$$. By :ref{#crossing-mirrors},
first flipping in $$L$$, then in $$K$$ is the turn by $$2 \cdot \frac{x}{2} = x$$ degrees:

```math
R_K \circ R_L = G_x
```

Now flip in $$L$$, then turn:

```math
G_x \circ R_L = (R_K \circ R_L) \circ R_L = R_K \circ (R_L \circ R_L) = R_K \circ I = R_K
```

So "first flip in $$L$$, then turn by $$x^\circ$$" is a single flip, in the line $$K$$. For
instance, a flip in a horizontal line through $$O$$ followed by a quarter turn about $$O$$ is the
flip in the line through $$O$$ at $$45^\circ$$. With :ref{#fix-one-point}, this says: an isometry
that fixes a point is a turn about it or a flip in a line through it.

:::

## No pins: slide first

Finally, take any isometry at all. Nothing need stay where it is, so begin as you would by hand:
slide the sheet until one chosen point is home. That leaves an isometry with one fixed point, which
the previous section has handled.

:::theorem[Every isometry is built from slides, turns and flips]{#classification}

Let $$F$$ be an isometry and $$O$$ any point. Then there are a slide $$T$$, a turn $$G$$ about $$O$$
and a flip $$R$$ in a line through $$O$$ such that either

```math
F = T \circ G \qquad \text{or} \qquad F = T \circ G \circ R
```

In words: $$F$$ is a turn about $$O$$ and then a slide, or a flip, then a turn about $$O$$, then a
slide.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_plane_isometry#Three_mirrors_suffice"}

**Step 1: slide one point home.** Let $$O' = F(O)$$. By :ref{#translation-rules}, there is a slide
$$T$$ with $$T(O) = O'$$. Its inverse $$T^{-1}$$ is the slide by the reverse arrow
(:ref{#inverse-map}), which sends $$O'$$ back to $$O$$.

**Step 2: now one point is home.** Consider first $$F$$, then slide back: the map
$$H = T^{-1} \circ F$$. It is an isometry, since slides keep distances (:ref{#basic-isometries}) and
so do composites (:ref{#composite-isometry}). It fixes $$O$$, since $$F$$ sends $$O$$ to $$O'$$ and
$$T^{-1}$$ sends $$O'$$ back to $$O$$.

**Step 3: use the one-pin result.** By :ref{#fix-one-point}, $$H = G$$ for a turn $$G$$ about $$O$$,
or $$H = G \circ R$$ for such a turn and a flip $$R$$ in a line through $$O$$.

**Step 4: put the slide back on.** Doing $$T$$ after $$H$$ gives back $$F$$:

```math
T \circ H = T \circ (T^{-1} \circ F) = (T \circ T^{-1}) \circ F = I \circ F = F
```

So $$F = T \circ G$$ or $$F = T \circ G \circ R$$.

:::

:::example[Footprints]

A line of footprints in sand repeats one move: a left foot, flipped in the line down the middle of
the path and slid forward half a stride, gives the next right foot. That move is a glide reflection
(:ref{#glide-reflection}): first flip in $$L$$, then slide along $$L$$. In the form of
:ref{#classification}, with $$O$$ on $$L$$, it is $$T \circ G \circ R$$ where $$R$$ is the flip in
$$L$$, $$G$$ is the turn by $$0^\circ$$, and $$T$$ is the slide along $$L$$.

:::

Putting the sheet back by hand undid the move in the reverse order: slide back, turn back, flip
back. Since every isometry is built from pieces that can each be undone, every isometry can be
undone.

:::corollary[Every isometry can be undone]{#isometry-inverse}

Every isometry $$F$$ has an inverse $$F^{-1}$$, and $$F^{-1}$$ is an isometry.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_plane_isometry#Group_structure"}

By :ref{#classification}, $$F = T \circ G$$ or $$F = T \circ G \circ R$$, with $$T$$ a slide, $$G$$
a turn and $$R$$ a flip. Each piece has an inverse: the reverse slide, the turn back, and the flip
itself (:ref{#inverse-map}). By :ref{#inverse-composite}, undoing in reverse order,

```math
(T \circ G)^{-1} = G^{-1} \circ T^{-1} \qquad \text{and} \qquad (T \circ G \circ R)^{-1} = R \circ G^{-1} \circ T^{-1}
```

In words: slide back, then turn back, then flip back if there was a flip. This inverse is made of
slides, turns and flips, which keep distances (:ref{#basic-isometries}), so it is an isometry
(:ref{#composite-isometry}).

:::

Now the condition "if $$F$$ has an inverse" in :ref{#three-points-decide} always holds, so it can be
dropped: two isometries that agree on three points not on one line are the same isometry.

## The four kinds of isometry

The theorem says how to build any isometry, but different recipes can give the same move. A cleaner
way to sort isometries is by what they do. Each one either keeps the sheet face up or turns it face
down, and each one either fixes some points or fixes none. That gives four kinds, which the table
sorts by these two questions.

:::remark[The four kinds of isometry]{#four-kinds}

Every isometry other than the identity is exactly one of these:

| Kind                              | Fixed points | Sheet turned face down? |
| --------------------------------- | ------------ | ----------------------- |
| a slide (:ref{#translation})      | none         | no                      |
| a turn (:ref{#rotation})          | exactly one  | no                      |
| a flip (:ref{#reflection})        | a whole line | yes                     |
| a glide (:ref{#glide-reflection}) | none         | yes                     |

The identity fixes every point and leaves the sheet face up. This is taken as given here; the proof
combines the results on this page with the mirror results on the
[Combining moves](./combining-moves.mdx) page.

::see-also{src="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_plane_isometry#Classification"}

:::

The table gives a practical test. To recognise a rigid move, ask whether it fixes any point and
whether it turns shapes over (a left shoe into a right shoe). For instance, a move that fixes
nothing and turns shapes over can only be a glide, like the step from one footprint to the next.

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on the classification of isometries, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-classification-two-pins}

An isometry $$F$$ fixes two different points $$P$$ and $$Q$$.

1. Suppose $$F$$ also fixes a point $$M$$ that is not on the line $$PQ$$. What is $$F$$?
2. Suppose instead that $$F$$ moves some point. What is $$F$$, and where does it send a point $$X$$
   of the line $$PQ$$?

:::solution

1. $$F$$ fixes three points not on one line, so $$F = I$$ (:ref{#three-fixed-points}).
2. By :ref{#fix-two-points}, $$F$$ is $$I$$ or the reflection in the line $$PQ$$. It moves a point,
   so it isn't $$I$$; it is the reflection in $$PQ$$. A point $$X$$ of the mirror stays where it is
   (:ref{#reflection}), so $$F(X) = X$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-classification-kind}

For each description, say which of the four kinds of isometry it could be (:ref{#four-kinds}).

1. It fixes exactly one point.
2. It fixes no point and keeps the sheet face up.
3. It fixes every point of a line, and moves some other point.
4. It fixes no point and turns the sheet face down.

:::solution

1. A turn, not by a whole number of full turns.
2. A slide, not by the zero arrow.
3. A flip in that line. A turn fixes only its centre, and slides and glides fix nothing, so none of
   those fixes a whole line.
4. A glide reflection, with a slide that isn't zero.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-classification-undo-recipe}

An isometry is $$F = T \circ G \circ R$$, where $$R$$ is the flip in a line $$L$$ through $$O$$,
$$G$$ is the turn about $$O$$ by $$40^\circ$$, and $$T$$ is the slide $$6$$ cm to the east. Write
$$F^{-1}$$ as a composite of a slide, a turn and a flip, and describe it in words.

:::solution

By :ref{#inverse-composite}, $$F^{-1} = R^{-1} \circ G^{-1} \circ T^{-1}$$. Each piece has a known
inverse (:ref{#inverse-map}): $$T^{-1}$$ is the slide $$6$$ cm to the west, $$G^{-1}$$ is the turn
about $$O$$ by $$-40^\circ$$, and $$R^{-1} = R$$. So

```math
F^{-1} = R \circ G_{-40} \circ T^{-1}
```

In words: first slide $$6$$ cm west, then turn $$40^\circ$$ clockwise about $$O$$, then flip in
$$L$$. This is the original recipe run backwards.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-classification-quarter-flip}

Let $$L$$ be a horizontal line through $$O$$, $$R_L$$ the flip in $$L$$, and $$G$$ the quarter turn
anticlockwise about $$O$$. Using :ref{#crossing-mirrors}, show that first $$R_L$$, then $$G$$ is a
single flip, and name its mirror.

:::solution

Let $$K$$ be the line through $$O$$ at $$45^\circ$$ anticlockwise from $$L$$. The mirrors $$L$$ and
$$K$$ cross at $$O$$ at an angle of $$45^\circ$$, so first flipping in $$L$$, then in $$K$$ is the
turn about $$O$$ by $$2 \cdot 45 = 90$$ degrees (:ref{#crossing-mirrors}): $$R_K \circ R_L = G$$.
Then, using :ref{#reflection-twice} and :ref{#composite-associative},

```math
G \circ R_L = (R_K \circ R_L) \circ R_L = R_K \circ (R_L \circ R_L) = R_K
```

So first $$R_L$$, then $$G$$ is the flip in $$K$$, the line through $$O$$ rising at $$45^\circ$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-classification-perpendicular-flips}

Let $$L$$ and $$K$$ be perpendicular lines crossing at $$O$$. What is first the flip in $$L$$, then
the flip in $$K$$? Check your answer against :ref{#four-kinds} by counting fixed points.

:::solution

The mirrors cross at $$O$$ at $$90^\circ$$, so by :ref{#crossing-mirrors} the composite is the turn
about $$O$$ by $$2 \cdot 90 = 180$$ degrees: the half turn about $$O$$.

Check: $$O$$ is on both mirrors, so it stays where it is. Any other point $$P$$ ends up on the other
side of $$O$$, at the same distance, so it moves. Exactly one fixed point and the sheet ends up face
up (two flips turn it over twice), so it is a turn, as the table says.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-classification-one-pin-fixed}

An isometry $$F$$ fixes a point $$O$$ and also a second point $$P \ne O$$. A student says: "by
:ref{#fix-one-point}, $$F$$ is a turn about $$O$$". Explain what is missing, and what $$F$$ can
actually be.

:::solution

:ref{#fix-one-point} says $$F$$ is a turn about $$O$$, or a flip in a line through $$O$$ followed by
a turn. The student forgot the second case. Since $$F$$ fixes two points, :ref{#fix-two-points} says
more: $$F$$ is the identity, which is the turn by $$0^\circ$$, or the flip in the line $$OP$$. A
turn by any other angle moves $$P$$, so these are the only two possibilities.

:::
