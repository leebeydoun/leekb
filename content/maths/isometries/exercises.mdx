These exercises draw on the whole chapter, and most of them need ideas from more than one guide.
Some ask for a route, a move or a count, and some ask for a short proof. Try each one before opening
its solution, and say which rule or result justifies each step, as the guides do.

In several exercises, a position is given as "$$a$$ east and $$b$$ north" of a starting point. Two
points that differ by $$a$$ east and $$b$$ north are the corners of a right triangle with legs $$a$$
and $$b$$, so they are $$\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$$ apart (:ref{#distance-and-angles/pythagoras}).

## Routes and mirrors

::::exercise{#ex-isometries-pump-station}

Two villages, $$P$$ and $$Q$$, lie on the same side of a straight canal $$L$$. Village $$P$$ is
$$3$$ km from the canal and village $$Q$$ is $$5$$ km from it, and their nearest points on the canal
are $$6$$ km apart. A pumping station $$M$$ goes on the canal bank, with one pipe to each village.

1. How short can the two pipes be in total?
2. Show that the best place for the station is $$2.25$$ km along the canal from the point nearest
   $$P$$.
3. How much longer are the pipes if the station sits at the point of the canal nearest $$P$$?

:::solution

1. Let $$P'$$ be the reflection of $$P$$ in the canal. By :ref{#shortest-route}, the total is
   smallest where the segment $$P'Q$$ crosses the canal, and the smallest total is $$d(P', Q)$$. The
   image $$P'$$ is $$3$$ km on the far side, so $$P'$$ and $$Q$$ are $$3 + 5 = 8$$ km apart measured
   square to the canal, and $$6$$ km apart along it. So the smallest total is
   $$\sqrt{6^2 + 8^2} = \sqrt{100} = 10$$ km.
2. Put $$M$$ on the canal $$2.25$$ km along from the point nearest $$P$$. Then $$M$$ is
   $$6 - 2.25 = 3.75$$ km along from the point nearest $$Q$$, and the two pipes have lengths

   ```math
   d(P, M) = \sqrt{2.25^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{14.0625} = 3.75, \qquad d(M, Q) = \sqrt{3.75^2 + 5^2} = \sqrt{39.0625} = 6.25
   ```

   These add up to $$10$$ km, the smallest total from part 1. Since $$d(P', M) = d(P, M)$$
   (:ref{#mirror-distances}), this says $$d(P', M) + d(M, Q) = d(P', Q)$$, so $$M$$ lies on the
   segment $$P'Q$$ (:ref{#distance-and-angles/segment-rules}, rule 1). So $$M$$ is the crossing
   point of the theorem.

   The number $$2.25$$ comes from the picture: the segment from $$P'$$ to $$Q$$ climbs $$8$$ km
   square to the canal while it moves $$6$$ km along. It reaches the canal after climbing $$3$$ of
   those $$8$$ km, so after $$\frac{3}{8} \cdot 6 = 2.25$$ km along. The check above is what proves
   it.

3. The first pipe is $$3$$ km, and the second is $$\sqrt{6^2 + 5^2} = \sqrt{61} \approx 7.81$$ km.
   The total is about $$10.81$$ km, so about $$0.81$$ km longer than the best.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-isometries-two-walls}

Two straight walls meet at a corner $$O$$ at right angles: wall $$L$$ runs east from $$O$$, and wall
$$K$$ runs north. A dog at $$P$$, $$4$$ east and $$2$$ north of $$O$$, must touch wall $$L$$, then
wall $$K$$, then run to its owner at $$Q$$, $$1$$ east and $$3$$ north of $$O$$.

1. Let $$P'$$ be the flip of $$P$$ in $$L$$, and $$Q'$$ the flip of $$Q$$ in $$K$$. Show that every
   route of this kind is at least $$d(P', Q')$$ long.
2. Find $$d(P', Q')$$, and find a route that long.
3. How long is the route that touches both walls at the corner $$O$$?

:::solution

1. Let the dog touch $$L$$ at $$X$$ and $$K$$ at $$Y$$. Since $$X$$ is on the mirror $$L$$,
   $$d(P, X) = d(P', X)$$, and since $$Y$$ is on $$K$$, $$d(Y, Q) = d(Y, Q')$$
   (:ref{#mirror-distances}). Using the triangle inequality
   (:ref{#distance-and-angles/distance-rules}) twice,

   ```math
   d(P, X) + d(X, Y) + d(Y, Q) = d(P', X) + d(X, Y) + d(Y, Q') \ge d(P', Y) + d(Y, Q') \ge d(P', Q')
   ```

2. The flip in $$L$$ keeps how far east a point is and swaps north for south, so $$P'$$ is $$4$$
   east and $$2$$ south of $$O$$. The flip in $$K$$ swaps east for west, so $$Q'$$ is $$1$$ west and
   $$3$$ north. They differ by $$5$$ west and $$5$$ north, so
   $$d(P', Q') = \sqrt{50} = 5\sqrt{2} \approx 7.07$$.

   Try $$X$$ at $$2$$ east of $$O$$ and $$Y$$ at $$2$$ north of $$O$$. The route has legs

   ```math
   d(P, X) = \sqrt{2^2 + 2^2} = 2\sqrt{2}, \qquad d(X, Y) = \sqrt{2^2 + 2^2} = 2\sqrt{2}, \qquad d(Y, Q) = \sqrt{1^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{2}
   ```

   which add up to $$5\sqrt{2}$$. So this route is as short as any route can be.

3. The legs are $$d(P, O) = \sqrt{4^2 + 2^2} = \sqrt{20} \approx 4.47$$ and
   $$d(O, Q) = \sqrt{1^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{10} \approx 3.16$$, about $$7.64$$ in total. That is about
   $$0.57$$ longer than the best route.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-isometries-three-mirrors}

Three parallel mirrors $$L$$, $$K$$ and $$M$$ stand in a hall of mirrors. Measured along a line
square to them, $$L$$ is at $$0$$, $$K$$ at $$3$$ and $$M$$ at $$10$$. Show that "first flip in
$$L$$, then in $$K$$, then in $$M$$" is a single flip, and find its mirror.

:::solution

By :ref{#parallel-mirrors}, $$R_K \circ R_L$$ is the slide by $$2 \cdot 3 = 6$$, square to the
mirrors, in the direction from $$L$$ towards $$K$$.

Now let $$N$$ be the mirror parallel to the others at $$7$$, so $$N$$ is $$3$$ before $$M$$. By the
same theorem, $$R_M \circ R_N$$ is also the slide by $$6$$ in that direction. Two slides that move
every point the same distance in the same direction send every point to the same place, so they are
equal maps (:ref{#map-equality}): $$R_K \circ R_L = R_M \circ R_N$$. Then, using
:ref{#composite-associative} and :ref{#reflection-twice},

```math
R_M \circ (R_K \circ R_L) = R_M \circ (R_M \circ R_N) = (R_M \circ R_M) \circ R_N = R_N
```

So the three flips together are the flip in the mirror at $$7$$. Check a point at $$0$$: the flip in
$$L$$ leaves it at $$0$$, the flip in $$K$$ sends it to $$6$$, and the flip in $$M$$ sends it to
$$14$$. The flip in the mirror at $$7$$ also sends $$0$$ to $$14$$.

:::

## Combining, undoing and recognising moves

::::exercise{#ex-isometries-what-kind}

Say which single move each composite is, and check your answer against :ref{#four-kinds}.

1. First the flip in a line $$L$$, then the flip in a line $$K$$, where the lines cross at $$O$$ and
   the angle turning anticlockwise from $$L$$ to $$K$$ is $$25^\circ$$.
2. First the turn by $$90^\circ$$ about $$O$$, then the turn by $$270^\circ$$ about $$O$$.
3. First the flip in a level line $$L$$, then the slide $$4$$ units straight up.
4. First the half turn about a point $$O$$ of a line $$L$$, then the flip in $$L$$.

:::solution

1. By :ref{#crossing-mirrors}, it is the turn about $$O$$ by $$2 \cdot 25 = 50$$ degrees
   anticlockwise. It fixes exactly one point, $$O$$, and two flips leave the sheet face up, as the
   table says for a turn.
2. Turns add (:ref{#rotation-rules}), so it is the turn by $$360^\circ$$, which is the identity
   $$I$$. Every point is fixed, so it is none of the four kinds: the table covers every isometry
   other than the identity.
3. Let $$K$$ be the level line $$2$$ units above $$L$$. By :ref{#parallel-mirrors},
   $$R_K \circ R_L$$ is the slide by $$2 \cdot 2 = 4$$ units straight up, so it is the given slide
   $$T$$. Then

   ```math
   T \circ R_L = (R_K \circ R_L) \circ R_L = R_K \circ (R_L \circ R_L) = R_K
   ```

   by :ref{#composite-associative} and :ref{#reflection-twice}. It is the flip in $$K$$. Check: a
   point of $$L$$ stays put under $$R_L$$ and then rises $$4$$, and $$R_K$$ also sends it from $$2$$
   below $$K$$ to $$2$$ above.

4. Let $$K$$ be the line through $$O$$ perpendicular to $$L$$. The angle turning from $$K$$ to $$L$$
   is $$90^\circ$$ or $$270^\circ$$, depending on the direction you measure, and twice either one is
   a half turn, since $$540^\circ$$ is a whole turn plus $$180^\circ$$
   (:ref{#rotation-whole-turns}). So $$R_L \circ R_K$$ is the half turn $$G_{180}$$ about $$O$$
   (:ref{#crossing-mirrors}), and

   ```math
   R_L \circ G_{180} = R_L \circ (R_L \circ R_K) = (R_L \circ R_L) \circ R_K = R_K
   ```

   It is the flip in the line through $$O$$ perpendicular to $$L$$. A flip fixes a whole line and
   turns the sheet face down; here the half turn keeps it face up and the flip in $$L$$ turns it
   over.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-isometries-turned-mirror}

A drawing program has three buttons for a picture on the screen: turn a quarter turn anticlockwise
about the centre $$O$$, turn a quarter turn clockwise about $$O$$, and flip in the level line $$L$$
through $$O$$. There is no button to flip in the upright line $$V$$ through $$O$$. Show that
pressing "clockwise", then "flip", then "anticlockwise" does exactly that flip.

:::solution

Write $$G$$ for the quarter turn anticlockwise, so the clockwise button is $$G^{-1} = G_{-90}$$. The
three presses make the map $$F = G \circ R_L \circ G^{-1}$$. It is an isometry, since each step is
(:ref{#basic-isometries}) and so are composites (:ref{#composite-isometry}).

Follow three points that don't lie on one line:

- **$$O$$**: every step leaves $$O$$ where it is, so $$F(O) = O$$, and $$R_V(O) = O$$ too.
- **$$P$$, $$1$$ right of $$O$$**: the clockwise turn sends it to $$1$$ below $$O$$, the flip sends
  that to $$1$$ above $$O$$, and the anticlockwise turn sends that to $$1$$ left of $$O$$. The flip
  in $$V$$ also sends $$P$$ to $$1$$ left of $$O$$.
- **$$Q$$, $$1$$ above $$O$$**: the clockwise turn sends it to $$1$$ right of $$O$$, which is on
  $$L$$, so the flip leaves it there, and the anticlockwise turn sends it back to $$1$$ above $$O$$.
  The point $$Q$$ is on $$V$$, so $$R_V(Q) = Q$$ too.

So $$F$$ and $$R_V$$ are isometries that agree at three points not on one line. Every isometry has
an inverse (:ref{#isometry-inverse}), so by :ref{#three-points-decide}, $$F = R_V$$.

The idea behind it: turn the picture so that the mirror you want lies along the mirror you have,
flip, then turn the picture back.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-isometries-swap-two-points}

An isometry $$F$$ swaps two different points: $$F(P) = Q$$ and $$F(Q) = P$$. Let $$O$$ be the
midpoint of $$PQ$$. Show that $$F$$ is either the half turn about $$O$$ or the flip in the
perpendicular bisector of $$PQ$$, and that both of these do swap $$P$$ and $$Q$$.

:::solution

**$$F$$ fixes $$O$$.** Let $$D = d(P, Q)$$ and $$O' = F(O)$$. Distances are kept, so

```math
d(Q, O') = d(P, O) = \tfrac{D}{2}, \qquad d(O', P) = d(O, Q) = \tfrac{D}{2}
```

These add up to $$D = d(Q, P)$$, so $$O'$$ lies on the segment $$QP$$
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/segment-rules}, rule 1). It is equally far from $$P$$ and $$Q$$, so it is
the midpoint (:ref{#distance-and-angles/equidistant-on-line}). So $$O' = O$$.

**Two possibilities.** By :ref{#fix-one-point}, $$F$$ is a turn $$G_x$$ about $$O$$, or
$$F = G_x \circ R_N$$ for a flip $$R_N$$ in a line $$N$$ through $$O$$. In the second case, if
$$x = 0$$ then $$F = R_N$$. Otherwise let $$K$$ be the line through $$O$$ at $$\frac{x}{2}$$ degrees
anticlockwise from $$N$$. Then $$G_x = R_K \circ R_N$$ (:ref{#crossing-mirrors}), so
$$F = R_K \circ R_N \circ R_N = R_K$$ (:ref{#reflection-twice}). Either way, $$F$$ is a turn about
$$O$$ or a flip in a line through $$O$$.

**If $$F$$ is a turn.** The point $$Q$$ is on the line $$OP$$, on the other side of $$O$$ from
$$P$$, at the same distance, so the reflection through $$O$$ sends $$P$$ to $$Q$$
(:ref{#point-reflection}). That is the half turn $$G_{180}$$ (:ref{#half-turn}). By
:ref{#rotation-rules}, rule 1, only one turn by an angle from $$0^\circ$$ up to $$360^\circ$$ sends
$$P$$ to $$Q$$, so $$F = G_{180}$$.

**If $$F$$ is a flip** in a line $$K$$. Since $$F(P) = Q \ne P$$, the point $$P$$ is not on $$K$$,
so $$K$$ is the perpendicular bisector of $$PQ$$ (:ref{#reflection}).

**Both swap.** The half turn sends $$P$$ to $$Q$$, and sends $$Q$$ to $$G_{180}(G_{180}(P))$$, which
is $$G_{360}(P) = P$$. The flip in the bisector sends $$P$$ to $$Q$$ by its definition, and sends
$$Q$$ to $$R(R(P)) = P$$ by :ref{#reflection-twice}.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-isometries-own-inverse}

Some moves are their own undo button: doing them twice puts everything back. Find every isometry
$$F$$ with $$F \circ F = I$$.

:::solution

The answer: the identity, every flip in a line, and every half turn. Go through the kinds in
:ref{#four-kinds}:

- **The identity**: $$I \circ I = I$$.
- **A flip**: $$R \circ R = I$$ by :ref{#reflection-twice}.
- **A slide** $$T$$ by a non-zero distance $$s$$: doing it twice moves every point $$2s$$ in the
  same direction, so some point moves and $$T \circ T \ne I$$.
- **A turn** $$G_x$$ about $$O$$ with $$0 < x < 360$$: by :ref{#rotation-rules},
  $$G_x \circ G_x = G_{2x}$$, and $$0 < 2x < 720$$. If $$2x < 360$$, or $$2x > 360$$ so that
  $$G_{2x} = G_{2x - 360}$$ with $$0 < 2x - 360 < 360$$ (:ref{#rotation-whole-turns}), this is a
  turn by something other than a whole number of full turns, which moves every point other than
  $$O$$. So $$G_x \circ G_x = I$$ exactly when $$2x = 360$$, that is, for the half turn.
- **A glide** $$T \circ R$$, with $$R$$ the flip in $$L$$ and $$T$$ a non-zero slide by $$s$$ along
  $$L$$: describe a point as "$$a$$ along $$L$$ and $$b$$ above it". Both $$T \circ R$$ and
  $$R \circ T$$ send it to $$a + s$$ along and $$b$$ below, so $$T \circ R = R \circ T$$
  (:ref{#map-equality}). Then

  ```math
  (T \circ R) \circ (T \circ R) = T \circ (R \circ T) \circ R = T \circ (T \circ R) \circ R = (T \circ T) \circ (R \circ R) = T \circ T
  ```

  using :ref{#composite-associative} to regroup. That is a slide by $$2s$$, not the identity.

:::

## Congruence and symmetry

::::exercise{#ex-isometries-across-the-pond}

A surveyor needs the distance between two posts $$P$$ and $$M$$ on opposite sides of a pond. The
surveyor stands at a point $$Q$$ on dry land and walks in a straight line from $$P$$ through $$Q$$,
stopping at $$P'$$ with $$d(Q, P') = d(Q, P)$$. The surveyor does the same from $$M$$ through $$Q$$
to $$M'$$. Then the surveyor measures $$d(P', M') = 85$$ m. Explain, using one isometry, why the
pond is $$85$$ m across from $$P$$ to $$M$$.

:::solution

The point $$P'$$ is on the line $$PQ$$, on the other side of $$Q$$ from $$P$$, at the same distance.
So the reflection through $$Q$$ sends $$P$$ to $$P'$$ (:ref{#point-reflection}), and in the same way
it sends $$M$$ to $$M'$$. That reflection is the half turn about $$Q$$ (:ref{#half-turn}), and turns
keep distances (:ref{#basic-isometries}). So

```math
d(P, M) = d(P', M') = 85 \text{ m}
```

The half turn carries the whole triangle $$PQM$$ across the pond onto the triangle $$P'QM'$$ on dry
land, which is why the measurement can be made there.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-isometries-isosceles-mirror}

The triangle $$\triangle PQM$$ has $$d(Q, P) = d(Q, M)$$, like the end of a roof with equal slopes.
Let $$K$$ be the perpendicular bisector of $$PM$$
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/perpendicular-bisector}).

1. Show that the flip in $$K$$ is a symmetry of the triangle, meaning its three sides.
2. Use part 1 to show again that the angles at $$P$$ and $$M$$ are equal (:ref{#isosceles}).

:::solution

1. Let $$R$$ be the flip in $$K$$. The point $$Q$$ is as far from $$P$$ as from $$M$$, so it lies on
   $$K$$ (:ref{#distance-and-angles/perpendicular-bisector-rule}), and $$R(Q) = Q$$. The line $$K$$
   is the perpendicular bisector of $$PM$$, so $$R(P) = M$$ (:ref{#reflection}), and
   $$R(M) = R(R(P)) = P$$ (:ref{#reflection-twice}). Segments go onto segments
   (:ref{#isometry-segments}), so $$R$$ sends the side $$QP$$ onto $$QM$$, the side $$QM$$ onto
   $$QP$$, and the side $$PM$$ onto $$MP$$. The image of the three sides is the three sides again
   (:ref{#image}), so $$R$$ is a symmetry (:ref{#symmetry}).
2. The angle at $$P$$ is between the rays $$PQ$$ and $$PM$$. The flip sends $$P$$ to $$M$$, $$Q$$ to
   $$Q$$ and $$M$$ to $$P$$, so it sends this angle to the angle at $$M$$ between the rays $$MQ$$
   and $$MP$$. Angles are kept (:ref{#isometry-angles}), so $$\angle QPM = \angle QMP$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-isometries-hexagon}

The head of a bolt is a regular hexagon with centre $$O$$ and corners $$A$$, $$B$$, $$C$$, $$D$$,
$$E$$, $$F$$ in order anticlockwise. Let $$L$$ be the mirror line through $$A$$ and $$D$$, and $$K$$
the mirror line through the midpoints of the sides $$AB$$ and $$DE$$.

1. Name the six turn symmetries of the hexagon.
2. The angle turning anticlockwise from $$L$$ to $$K$$ is $$30^\circ$$. What is first the flip in
   $$L$$, then the flip in $$K$$?
3. Which order of the same two flips turns the hexagon the other way?

:::solution

1. Here $$n = 6$$, so the turn $$G$$ by $$\frac{360}{6} = 60$$ degrees about $$O$$ is a symmetry
   (:ref{#regular-turns}). Symmetries combine into symmetries (:ref{#symmetry-combine}), so its
   powers are too: the turns by $$0^\circ$$, $$60^\circ$$, $$120^\circ$$, $$180^\circ$$,
   $$240^\circ$$ and $$300^\circ$$. The next power, $$G^6$$, is the turn by $$360^\circ$$, which is
   $$G^0 = I$$ again.
2. By :ref{#crossing-mirrors}, $$R_K \circ R_L$$ is the turn about $$O$$ by $$2 \cdot 30 = 60$$
   degrees anticlockwise, the turn $$G$$ that moves each corner on to the next: $$A$$ to $$B$$,
   $$B$$ to $$C$$, and so on.
3. The other order, $$R_L \circ R_K$$, undoes it: by :ref{#inverse-composite} and since each flip is
   its own inverse,

   ```math
   (R_K \circ R_L)^{-1} = R_L^{-1} \circ R_K^{-1} = R_L \circ R_K
   ```

   So first flipping in $$K$$, then in $$L$$, is $$G^{-1}$$, the turn by $$60^\circ$$ clockwise,
   which is also $$G^5$$ (:ref{#inverse-by-repeating}).

:::

::::exercise{#ex-isometries-two-centres}

A tiled border runs along a wall without end. It looks the same after a half turn about a point
$$O$$ on its middle line, and also after a half turn about a second point $$Q$$ on that line, $$20$$
cm further along. Show that the border also looks the same after a slide, and find how far.

:::solution

Write $$H_O$$ and $$H_Q$$ for the two half turns, and $$N$$ for the middle line through $$O$$ and
$$Q$$. Symmetries combine into symmetries (:ref{#symmetry-combine}), so $$H_Q \circ H_O$$ is a
symmetry of the border.

Let $$A$$ and $$B$$ be the lines perpendicular to $$N$$ through $$O$$ and through $$Q$$. Each
crosses $$N$$ at $$90^\circ$$, and two flips in lines crossing at $$90^\circ$$ make a half turn
about the crossing point (:ref{#crossing-mirrors}; whichever way you measure, twice the angle is
$$180^\circ$$ or $$540^\circ$$, the same turn). So

```math
H_O = R_N \circ R_A, \qquad H_Q = R_B \circ R_N
```

Then, using :ref{#composite-associative} and :ref{#reflection-twice},

```math
H_Q \circ H_O = R_B \circ (R_N \circ R_N) \circ R_A = R_B \circ R_A
```

The lines $$A$$ and $$B$$ are both perpendicular to $$N$$, so they are parallel
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/perpendiculars-parallel}), and the segment $$OQ$$ meets both square on,
so they are $$20$$ cm apart (:ref{#distance-and-angles/parallel-distance}). By
:ref{#parallel-mirrors}, $$R_B \circ R_A$$ is the slide by $$2 \cdot 20 = 40$$ cm along the border,
from $$O$$ towards $$Q$$.

Check with the point $$O$$: the half turn about $$O$$ leaves it in place, and the half turn about
$$Q$$ sends it to the point on the other side of $$Q$$, $$20$$ cm beyond. That is $$40$$ cm from
where $$O$$ started.

:::
