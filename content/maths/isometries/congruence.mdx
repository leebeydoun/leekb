  When two shapes are the same shape and size, and the tests that decide it for triangles.
---

A spare key cut from the same pattern opens the same lock, and any bolt from a box fits the same
nut. Factories depend on this: two parts made to the same measurements are interchangeable, because
one could be laid exactly on top of the other. This page makes "the same shape and size" precise
using the moves of the earlier guides. It then gives tests that decide it for triangles without
moving anything, and explains why a frame made of triangles is rigid while a square frame folds
over.

## Same shape and size

Trace a shape onto tracing paper. If you can slide, turn or flip the sheet so that the tracing lands
exactly on a second shape, covering all of it and nothing more, the two shapes are the same shape
and size. Flipping is allowed, since lifting the sheet and turning it over is one of the moves. So a
shape and its mirror image count as the same, like the two halves of a butterfly.

Every move of the sheet keeps distances, and :ref{#classification} shows that every isometry is such
a move. So the precise version says "an isometry carries one shape exactly onto the other".

:::definition[Congruent]{#congruent}

A shape $$S$$ is **congruent** to a shape $$S'$$ if there is an isometry $$F$$ (:ref{#isometry})
whose image of $$S$$ is exactly $$S'$$ (:ref{#image}):

```math
F(S) = S'
```

You write $$S \cong S'$$.

:::

The word "exactly" matters: every point of $$S$$ must land somewhere in $$S'$$, and every point of
$$S'$$ must be landed on. A short segment slid onto part of a longer one lands inside it, but it
doesn't cover it, so that slide doesn't show they are congruent.

```plane
point P 0 0 sw
point Q 3 0 se
point M 1 2 n
point P' 7 0 se
point Q' 7 3 ne
point M' 5 1 w
polygon P Q M color=blue
polygon P' Q' M' color=orange
segment P Q
segment Q M
segment M P
segment P' Q'
segment Q' M'
segment M' P'
caption "Turned a quarter turn and slid across, the blue triangle lands exactly on the orange one"
```

Congruence behaves like "equals" does for numbers. Every shape is congruent to itself, you can say
it in either direction, and two congruences in a row make a third.

:::proposition[Congruence behaves like equality]{#congruence-relation}

For any shapes $$S$$, $$S'$$ and $$S''$$:

1. **Itself**: $$S \cong S$$
2. **Either way round**: if $$S \cong S'$$, then $$S' \cong S$$
3. **In a chain**: if $$S \cong S'$$ and $$S' \cong S''$$, then $$S \cong S''$$

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congruence_(geometry)"}

**Part 1.** The identity $$I$$ moves nothing, so it keeps every distance and $$I(S) = S$$.

**Part 2.** Suppose $$F$$ is an isometry with $$F(S) = S'$$. By :ref{#isometry-inverse}, $$F$$ has
an inverse $$F^{-1}$$ (:ref{#inverse-map}), and the inverse is an isometry too. Every point of
$$S'$$ is $$F(X)$$ for some $$X$$ in $$S$$, and $$F^{-1}$$ sends it back to $$X$$. So $$F^{-1}$$
sends every point of $$S'$$ into $$S$$, and every point $$X$$ of $$S$$ is reached, from $$F(X)$$.
That says $$F^{-1}(S') = S$$, so $$S' \cong S$$.

**Part 3.** Suppose $$F(S) = S'$$ and $$G(S') = S''$$ for isometries $$F$$ and $$G$$. First $$F$$,
then $$G$$, is an isometry (:ref{#composite-isometry}). It carries $$S$$ onto $$S'$$ and then $$S'$$
onto $$S''$$, so $$(G \circ F)(S) = S''$$.

:::

## Circles and segments

Two coins of the same size are congruent: slide one until its centre sits on the other's centre, and
the rims match all the way round. Two sticks of the same length are congruent too: slide one end
onto the other stick's end, then turn until the sticks line up. Both proofs below are these two
pictures, written out.

:::theorem[Circles of the same radius are congruent]{#circles-congruent}

Two circles with the same radius $$r$$ are congruent.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congruence_(geometry)#Congruent_conic_sections"}

Let the circles have centres $$O$$ and $$O'$$ (:ref{#distance-and-angles/circle}). Let $$T$$ be the
slide that takes $$O$$ to $$O'$$ (:ref{#translation-rules}). A slide keeps distances
(:ref{#basic-isometries}). Two sets are equal when each is inside the other
(:ref{#basic-proofs/set-equality}), so there are two things to show.

**Every point of the first circle lands on the second.** If $$d(O, P) = r$$, then
$$d(O', T(P)) = d(T(O), T(P)) = d(O, P) = r$$.

**Every point of the second circle is landed on.** Let $$d(O', Q) = r$$. The slide back, $$T^{-1}$$,
takes $$O'$$ to $$O$$, so $$P = T^{-1}(Q)$$ satisfies $$d(O, P) = d(O', Q) = r$$. So $$P$$ is on the
first circle, and $$T(P) = Q$$.

So $$T$$ carries the first circle exactly onto the second.

:::

:::theorem[Segments of the same length are congruent]{#segments-congruent}

Let $$PQ$$ and $$MN$$ be segments with $$d(P, Q) = d(M, N)$$. Then there is an isometry $$F$$ with
$$F(M) = P$$ and $$F(N) = Q$$, and it carries the segment $$MN$$ exactly onto $$PQ$$. So the two
segments are congruent.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_plane_isometry"}

**Slide.** Let $$T$$ be the slide that takes $$M$$ to $$P$$ (:ref{#translation-rules}), and write
$$N' = T(N)$$. Slides keep distances, so $$d(P, N') = d(M, N) = d(P, Q)$$.

**Turn.** So $$N'$$ and $$Q$$ are the same distance from $$P$$, and by :ref{#rotation-rules} there
is a turn $$G$$ about $$P$$ that takes $$N'$$ to $$Q$$. It leaves its centre $$P$$ where it is.

**Together.** First $$T$$, then $$G$$, is an isometry $$F = G \circ T$$ (:ref{#composite-isometry}),
with $$F(M) = G(P) = P$$ and $$F(N) = G(N') = Q$$. By :ref{#isometry-segments} it carries the
segment $$MN$$ exactly onto the segment between $$F(M)$$ and $$F(N)$$, which is $$PQ$$.

:::

## Three sides fix a triangle

Bolt four wooden bars into a square and push its top corner: the frame folds over into a leaning
shape, although every bar keeps its length. Bolt one more bar across a diagonal and the frame stays
square, however hard you push. The brace splits the square into two triangles, and a triangle can't
change shape while its three sides keep their lengths.

<FrameBracing caption="A square of four bars can fold over; a diagonal brace makes two triangles, and the frame holds its shape" />

The interactive shows why. Two sides joined by a hinge can make a triangle at every opening, and the
gap between their free ends changes as the hinge opens. Once a third bar of fixed length closes the
gap, only one opening fits.

<TriangleFromSides />

This is why bridges, cranes, roof trusses and bicycle frames are built from triangles. Each
triangle's shape is fixed by its three sides, so the whole frame is.

<TrussBridge caption="A truss bridge: every panel is a triangle, so no panel can change shape under a load" />

In terms of moves, "a triangle is fixed by its sides" says this: two triangles whose sides match are
congruent. The proof moves one triangle onto the other in three steps, like laying a cut-out on a
drawing. Slide a corner home, turn so that a side lies along its partner, and flip the cut-out over
if the third corner has come out on the wrong side.

:::theorem[Side, side, side]{#sss}

Let $$\triangle PQM$$ and $$\triangle P'Q'M'$$ be triangles whose matching sides have the same
lengths:

```math
d(P, Q) = d(P', Q'), \qquad d(Q, M) = d(Q', M'), \qquad d(M, P) = d(M', P')
```

Then there is an isometry $$F$$ with $$F(P) = P'$$, $$F(Q) = Q'$$ and $$F(M) = M'$$. It carries each
side onto the matching side, so the two triangles are congruent.

:::

:::proof{source="https://www.cut-the-knot.org/pythagoras/SSS.shtml"}

**Slide and turn.** The sides $$PQ$$ and $$P'Q'$$ have the same length, so by
:ref{#segments-congruent} there is an isometry $$F_1$$, a slide followed by a turn, with
$$F_1(P) = P'$$ and $$F_1(Q) = Q'$$. Write $$X = F_1(M)$$ for where the third corner lands.
Isometries keep distances, so

```math
d(P', X) = d(P, M) = d(P', M'), \qquad d(Q', X) = d(Q, M) = d(Q', M')
```

**Check the third corner.** If $$X = M'$$, then $$F = F_1$$ does the job. Otherwise $$X$$ has come
out as in the figure below, the same distance from $$P'$$ as $$M'$$ and the same distance from
$$Q'$$.

**Flip.** The point $$P'$$ is the same distance from $$X$$ as from $$M'$$, so it lies on the
perpendicular bisector of $$XM'$$ (:ref{#distance-and-angles/perpendicular-bisector-rule}). So does
$$Q'$$. Only one line passes through $$P'$$ and $$Q'$$ (:ref{#distance-and-angles/line-rules}), so
the line $$P'Q'$$ _is_ the perpendicular bisector of $$XM'$$. By :ref{#reflection}, the flip $$R$$
in the line $$P'Q'$$ takes $$X$$ to $$M'$$, and it leaves $$P'$$ and $$Q'$$, which lie on the
mirror, where they are. So first $$F_1$$, then $$R$$, is an isometry $$F = R \circ F_1$$ with

```math
F(P) = P', \qquad F(Q) = Q', \qquad F(M) = R(X) = M'
```

**The sides.** By :ref{#isometry-segments}, $$F$$ carries each side of $$\triangle PQM$$ exactly
onto the segment between the images of its ends, which is the matching side of $$\triangle P'Q'M'$$.
So $$F$$ carries the triangle onto the triangle.

:::

```plane
point P' 0 0 w
point Q' 4 0 e
point M' 1.5 2 n
point X 1.5 -2 s
line P' Q' color=muted extend=0.8
segment P' Q'
segment Q' M'
segment M' P'
segment P' X color=orange dashed
segment Q' X color=orange dashed
segment X M' color=purple dashed
ticks P' M' 1
ticks P' X 1 color=orange
ticks Q' M' 2
ticks Q' X 2 color=orange
caption "If the third corner lands at X instead of M', the line P'Q' is the mirror that swaps them"
```

Once two triangles are congruent, everything you can measure on them matches, since the isometry
keeps distances (:ref{#isometry}), angles (:ref{#isometry-angles}) and area (:ref{#isometry-area}).
So matching sides force matching angles, which is the surprising half of the test: you only measured
lengths.

:::example[Matching the corners]

A triangle has sides of $$5$$, $$6$$ and $$7$$ cm, and so does a second triangle drawn somewhere
else. By :ref{#sss} they are congruent. The corners match up through the sides: the corner between
the $$5$$ and $$6$$ cm sides in one goes to the corner between the $$5$$ and $$6$$ cm sides in the
other. So those two corners have the same angle, and so do the other two pairs, without measuring a
single angle.

:::

## Two sides and the angle between them

Go back to the hinge. Two sides of fixed lengths, with the hinge opened to a fixed angle, also leave
no freedom: the free ends are pinned, and so is the third side. This gives a second test, which only
needs one angle.

:::theorem[Side, angle, side]{#sas}

Let $$\triangle PQM$$ and $$\triangle P'Q'M'$$ be triangles with

```math
d(Q, P) = d(Q', P'), \qquad d(Q, M) = d(Q', M'), \qquad \angle PQM = \angle P'Q'M'
```

Then there is an isometry $$F$$ with $$F(P) = P'$$, $$F(Q) = Q'$$ and $$F(M) = M'$$, so the
triangles are congruent.

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Triangle_Side-Angle-Side_Congruence"}

Write $$b = d(Q, M)$$ and $$\theta$$ for the angle at $$Q$$, which is less than a straight angle
since the corners aren't on one line.

**Slide and turn.** By :ref{#segments-congruent} there is an isometry $$F_1$$ with $$F_1(Q) = Q'$$
and $$F_1(P) = P'$$. Write $$X = F_1(M)$$. Then $$d(Q', X) = b$$, and since isometries keep angles
(:ref{#isometry-angles}), $$\angle P'Q'X = \theta$$.

**Only two places.** Picture the hinge at $$Q'$$: one arm lies along the ray $$Q'P'$$, and the other
arm, of length $$b$$, opens to the angle $$\theta$$. It can open to that angle on either side of the
ray, so its free end has two possible places.

To see that there are no others, let $$Z$$ be the point of the ray $$Q'P'$$ at distance $$b$$ from
$$Q'$$, and let $$Y$$ be any point at distance $$b$$ from $$Q'$$ with $$\angle P'Q'Y = \theta$$. By
:ref{#rotation-rules} exactly one turn about $$Q'$$, by some $$x^\circ$$ with $$0 \le x < 360$$,
takes $$Z$$ to $$Y$$, and by :ref{#rotation} the angle turning anticlockwise from the ray $$Q'Z$$ to
the ray $$Q'Y$$ is $$x^\circ$$. The two angles between these rays add up to a full turn, and
$$\angle P'Q'Y$$ is the one that is at most a straight angle
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/angle-name}). So $$x = \theta$$ or $$x = 360 - \theta$$: the point $$Y$$
is $$Z$$ turned by $$\theta$$ anticlockwise or by $$\theta$$ clockwise. Both $$X$$ and $$M'$$ are
such points.

**Flip if needed.** If $$X = M'$$, take $$F = F_1$$. Otherwise let $$R$$ be the flip in the line
$$Q'P'$$. It leaves $$Q'$$ and $$P'$$ where they are, so $$R(X)$$ is still at distance $$b$$ from
$$Q'$$, and by :ref{#isometry-angles} the angle between the rays $$Q'P'$$ and $$Q'R(X)$$ is still
$$\theta$$. So $$R(X)$$ is one of the two places. It isn't $$X$$ itself: a flip only leaves the
points of its mirror in place, and $$X$$ is not on the line $$Q'P'$$, since $$\theta$$ is neither
zero nor a straight angle. So $$X$$ and $$R(X)$$ are the two places. The point $$M'$$ is one of them
and isn't $$X$$, so $$R(X) = M'$$, and $$F = R \circ F_1$$ does the job.

:::

The angle has to be the one **between** the two given sides. Two sides and an angle somewhere else
don't fix a triangle. In the figure, the side $$QM$$ swings like a gate between two positions where
it meets the base line, giving two different triangles with the same two sides and the same angle at
$$P$$.

```plane
point P 0 0 sw
point Q 3.46 2 n
point M 1.96 0 s
point N 4.96 0 s
circle Q 2.5 color=muted dashed
ray P N color=muted extend=1
segment P Q
segment Q M color=blue
segment Q N color=orange
angle N P Q color=green r=0.9
caption "Two sides and an angle not between them: QM and QN have the same length, so both triangles fit"
```

## Right triangles, revisited

In [Right triangles](../distance-and-angles/right-triangles.mdx) one fact was taken on trust: two
right triangles with the same legs match completely, in their hypotenuse, their angles and their
area. It was pictured as cutting two triangles from card and laying one on the other. That picture
is exactly a congruence, and now it follows from the tests.

:::corollary[Right triangles with the same legs are congruent]{#right-triangles-congruent}

Let $$\triangle PQM$$ and $$\triangle P'Q'M'$$ be right triangles with their right angles at $$Q$$
and $$Q'$$, and legs of matching lengths: $$d(Q, P) = d(Q', P')$$ and $$d(Q, M) = d(Q', M')$$. Then
the triangles are congruent, and:

1. **Hypotenuse**: $$d(P, M) = d(P', M')$$
2. **Angles**: $$\angle QPM = \angle Q'P'M'$$ and $$\angle QMP = \angle Q'M'P'$$
3. **Area**: they have the same area

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congruence_(geometry)#Determining_congruence"}

Both angles at $$Q$$ and $$Q'$$ are right angles, so they are equal, and they lie between the
matching legs. By :ref{#sas} there is an isometry $$F$$ with $$F(P) = P'$$, $$F(Q) = Q'$$ and
$$F(M) = M'$$, carrying one triangle onto the other.

1. $$F$$ keeps distances, so $$d(P', M') = d(F(P), F(M)) = d(P, M)$$.
2. $$F$$ keeps angles (:ref{#isometry-angles}), and it carries the angle at $$P$$ onto the angle at
   $$P'$$, and the angle at $$M$$ onto the angle at $$M'$$.
3. $$F$$ keeps area (:ref{#isometry-area}).

:::

So the three parts of :ref{#distance-and-angles/right-triangle-rule} are no longer separate facts
about triangles. They are what happens when one triangle is moved onto another, and moves keep
lengths, angles and area. The earlier guides still lean on the rule, so it stays on the list of
things taken as given there, but it is now explained by a single picture: laying one triangle on top
of the other.

## Isosceles triangles

Cut a triangle with two equal sides out of paper, and fold it along the line from the corner where
those sides meet to the middle of the opposite side. The two halves fall exactly on top of each
other. So the two angles at the ends of the base, which land on each other, must be equal.

A triangle with two sides of equal length is called **isosceles**. The corner where the equal sides
meet is its apex, and the side opposite it is its base.

:::proposition[Base angles of an isosceles triangle]{#isosceles}

Let $$\triangle PQM$$ be a triangle with $$d(Q, P) = d(Q, M)$$. Then the angles at the ends of the
base are equal:

```math
\angle QPM = \angle QMP
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pons_asinorum"}

Let $$L$$ be the perpendicular bisector of the base $$PM$$
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/perpendicular-bisector}). The apex $$Q$$ is the same distance from $$P$$
as from $$M$$, so it lies on $$L$$ (:ref{#distance-and-angles/perpendicular-bisector-rule}).

Let $$R$$ be the flip in $$L$$, which is the fold. Since $$L$$ is the perpendicular bisector of
$$PM$$, the flip swaps $$P$$ and $$M$$ (:ref{#reflection}), and it leaves $$Q$$, on the mirror,
where it is.

The angle $$\angle QPM$$ has its corner at $$P$$ and its sides along the rays $$PQ$$ and $$PM$$. By
:ref{#isometry-angles}, the flip sends it to the angle at $$R(P) = M$$ between the rays
$$R(P)R(Q) = MQ$$ and $$R(P)R(M) = MP$$, which is $$\angle QMP$$, and the size doesn't change. So
$$\angle QPM = \angle QMP$$.

:::

```plane
point P 0 0 sw
point M 4 0 se
point Q 2 3.2 n
point O 2 0 s
line Q O color=purple dashed extend=0.6
segment P M
segment P Q
segment M Q
ticks P Q 1
ticks M Q 1
angle M P Q color=blue
angle Q M P color=blue
caption "Folding along the dashed line swaps P and M and keeps Q, so the two base angles match"
```

:::example[Finding the base angles]

An isosceles triangle has an angle of $$40^\circ$$ at its apex. Its two base angles are equal, say
$$x^\circ$$ each, and the three angles add up to $$180^\circ$$
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/triangle-angle-sum}). So $$40 + 2x = 180$$, and each base angle is
$$70^\circ$$.

:::

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on congruence, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-congruence-match-corners}

Triangle $$\triangle ABC$$ has $$d(A, B) = 5$$, $$d(B, C) = 6$$ and $$d(C, A) = 7$$. Triangle
$$\triangle XYZ$$ has $$d(X, Y) = 7$$, $$d(Y, Z) = 5$$ and $$d(Z, X) = 6$$. Show that they are
congruent, say which corner of $$\triangle XYZ$$ matches each of $$A$$, $$B$$ and $$C$$, and say
which angle of $$\triangle XYZ$$ equals $$\angle ABC$$.

:::solution

A corner is where two sides meet, so it matches the corner where the sides of the same lengths meet:

- $$B$$ is where the sides of lengths $$5$$ and $$6$$ meet; in $$\triangle XYZ$$ those are $$YZ$$
  and $$ZX$$, which meet at $$Z$$
- $$A$$ is where the sides of lengths $$5$$ and $$7$$ meet; those are $$YZ$$ and $$XY$$, which meet
  at $$Y$$
- $$C$$ is where the sides of lengths $$6$$ and $$7$$ meet; those are $$ZX$$ and $$XY$$, which meet
  at $$X$$

With $$A, B, C$$ matched to $$Y, Z, X$$, the sides agree: $$d(A, B) = d(Y, Z) = 5$$,
$$d(B, C) = d(Z, X) = 6$$ and $$d(C, A) = d(X, Y) = 7$$. By :ref{#sss} there is an isometry taking
$$A$$, $$B$$, $$C$$ to $$Y$$, $$Z$$, $$X$$, so the triangles are congruent. It keeps angles
(:ref{#isometry-angles}), so $$\angle ABC = \angle YZX$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-congruence-discs}

Prove that two discs (:ref{#distance-and-angles/circle}) with the same radius are congruent.

:::solution

Follow the proof of :ref{#circles-congruent}, with "at most $$r$$" in place of "exactly $$r$$". Let
the discs have centres $$O$$ and $$O'$$ and radius $$r$$, and let $$T$$ be the slide taking $$O$$ to
$$O'$$ (:ref{#translation-rules}).

If $$d(O, P) \le r$$, then $$d(O', T(P)) = d(T(O), T(P)) = d(O, P) \le r$$, so $$T(P)$$ is in the
second disc. If $$d(O', Q) \le r$$, then $$P = T^{-1}(Q)$$ has $$d(O, P) = d(O', Q) \le r$$, so it
is in the first disc and $$T(P) = Q$$. So $$T$$ carries the first disc exactly onto the second.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-congruence-lines}

Prove that any two lines are congruent.

:::solution

Let the lines be $$L$$ and $$K$$. Pick points $$P$$ and $$Q$$ on $$L$$ with $$d(P, Q) = 1$$, and
points $$P'$$ and $$Q'$$ on $$K$$ with $$d(P', Q') = 1$$. By :ref{#segments-congruent} there is an
isometry $$F$$ with $$F(P) = P'$$ and $$F(Q) = Q'$$.

By :ref{#isometry-lines}, $$F$$ carries the line through $$P$$ and $$Q$$, which is $$L$$, exactly
onto the line through $$P'$$ and $$Q'$$, which is $$K$$. So $$L \cong K$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-congruence-isosceles-angles}

1. An isosceles triangle has an apex angle of $$100^\circ$$. Find its base angles.
2. An isosceles triangle has base angles of $$35^\circ$$ each. Find its apex angle.
3. Can an isosceles triangle have base angles of $$95^\circ$$?

:::solution

The base angles are equal (:ref{#isosceles}), and the three angles add up to $$180^\circ$$
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/triangle-angle-sum}).

1. $$100 + 2x = 180$$, so each base angle is $$40^\circ$$.
2. The apex angle is $$180 - 2 \cdot 35 = 110$$ degrees.
3. No. Two angles of $$95^\circ$$ already add up to $$190^\circ$$, more than the $$180^\circ$$ the
   whole triangle has.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-congruence-equilateral}

A triangle whose three sides all have the same length is called equilateral. Prove that each of its
angles is $$60^\circ$$.

:::solution

Let $$\triangle PQM$$ have $$d(P, Q) = d(Q, M) = d(M, P)$$. Since $$d(Q, P) = d(Q, M)$$, the
triangle is isosceles with apex $$Q$$, so $$\angle QPM = \angle QMP$$ (:ref{#isosceles}). Since
$$d(P, Q) = d(P, M)$$, it is also isosceles with apex $$P$$, so $$\angle PQM = \angle PMQ$$.

So all three angles are equal, say $$x^\circ$$ each. They add up to $$180^\circ$$
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/triangle-angle-sum}), so $$3x = 180$$ and $$x = 60$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-congruence-kite}

A kite $$PQRM$$ has $$d(P, Q) = d(P, M)$$ and $$d(R, Q) = d(R, M)$$, as in the figure. Prove that
the diagonal $$PR$$ splits the angle at $$P$$ into two equal halves: $$\angle QPR = \angle RPM$$.

```plane
point P 0 0 w
point Q 2 1.5 n
point R 5 0 e
point M 2 -1.5 s
segment P Q
segment Q R
segment R M
segment M P
segment P R color=purple dashed
ticks P Q 1
ticks P M 1
ticks Q R 2
ticks M R 2
caption "A kite: two pairs of equal sides next to each other"
```

:::solution

Compare $$\triangle PQR$$ with $$\triangle PMR$$, matching $$P$$, $$Q$$, $$R$$ with $$P$$, $$M$$,
$$R$$. The sides match: $$d(P, Q) = d(P, M)$$ and $$d(Q, R) = d(M, R)$$ are given, and the side
$$PR$$ is shared. By :ref{#sss} there is an isometry $$F$$ with $$F(P) = P$$, $$F(Q) = M$$ and
$$F(R) = R$$.

It carries the angle $$\angle QPR$$, between the rays $$PQ$$ and $$PR$$, onto the angle between the
rays $$PM$$ and $$PR$$. Isometries keep angles (:ref{#isometry-angles}), so
$$\angle QPR = \angle RPM$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-congruence-side-side-angle}

A base line runs from a point $$P$$. A point $$Q$$ is $$4$$ from $$P$$, with an angle of
$$30^\circ$$ between $$PQ$$ and the base line. The foot $$H$$ of the perpendicular from $$Q$$ to the
base line has $$d(Q, H) = 2$$ and $$d(P, H) = 2\sqrt{3}$$. Find the two points $$M$$ on the base
line with $$d(Q, M) = 2.5$$, and explain why this shows that two sides and an angle that is not
between them don't fix a triangle.

:::solution

A point $$M$$ on the base line at distance $$t$$ from $$H$$ has $$d(Q, M)^2 = 2^2 + t^2$$ by
:ref{#distance-and-angles/pythagoras}. Setting this equal to $$2.5^2 = 6.25$$ gives $$t^2 = 2.25$$,
so $$t = 1.5$$.

So $$M$$ is $$1.5$$ either side of $$H$$, at a distance $$2\sqrt{3} - 1.5 \approx 1.96$$ or
$$2\sqrt{3} + 1.5 \approx 4.96$$ from $$P$$. Both triangles $$\triangle PQM$$ have $$d(P, Q) = 4$$,
$$d(Q, M) = 2.5$$ and an angle of $$30^\circ$$ at $$P$$. But their third sides, about $$1.96$$ and
$$4.96$$, are different, and isometries keep distances, so the triangles are not congruent. The
$$30^\circ$$ angle is at $$P$$, not between the two given sides, which meet at $$Q$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-congruence-four-bars}

A square frame $$ABCD$$ has four bars of length $$1$$. Pushed over, it becomes a shape $$ABCD$$
whose four bars still have length $$1$$, but whose angle at $$A$$ is now $$60^\circ$$. Show that the
diagonal $$BD$$ of the pushed shape can't have the same length as the square's diagonal. Explain why
this means that four bars don't fix the shape, while a brace along $$BD$$ would.

:::solution

**The square.** Its diagonal $$BD$$ is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs $$1$$ and $$1$$,
so by :ref{#distance-and-angles/pythagoras} it has length $$\sqrt{2}$$.

**The pushed shape.** Suppose its diagonal $$BD$$ also had length $$\sqrt{2}$$. Then its triangle
$$\triangle ABD$$ would have sides $$1$$, $$1$$ and $$\sqrt{2}$$, the same as the square's triangle
$$\triangle ABD$$. By :ref{#sss} the two triangles would be congruent, by an isometry taking the
square's corners $$A$$, $$B$$, $$D$$ to the pushed shape's corners $$A$$, $$B$$, $$D$$. Isometries
keep angles (:ref{#isometry-angles}), so the angle at $$A$$ would be $$90^\circ$$ in both. But it is
$$60^\circ$$ in the pushed shape, so the diagonals differ (:ref{#basic-proofs/contradiction}).

So the same four bars make shapes with different diagonals, and four side lengths don't fix the
shape: the frame can fold. A brace along $$BD$$ fixes the third side of both triangles
$$\triangle ABD$$ and $$\triangle CBD$$, and by :ref{#sss} three sides fix a triangle, so the braced
frame can't change shape.
