  that follow from that one picture.

Imagine riding a Ferris wheel, with your seat starting level with the hub, out to the right. As the
wheel turns, the seat climbs and moves inwards. A quarter of the way round you are straight above
the hub, and half way round you are level with it again, out to the left. At every moment two
numbers say where you are: how far across from the hub, and how far up.

For a wheel of radius $$1$$, those two numbers are called the **cosine** and the **sine** of the
angle the wheel has turned. Cosine is how far across, and sine is how far up. In this guide we'll
define both from a turning point, and work out what that picture tells us about them.

## The point on the unit circle

Draw the Ferris wheel on a grid, with the hub at the origin $$O$$ and a radius of $$1$$. The seat
then travels round the **unit circle**, the circle of radius $$1$$ centred at $$O$$. It starts at
$$E_1 = (1, 0)$$, and turning the wheel by an angle $$x$$ carries it to a new point of the circle.
The two coordinates of that point are the two numbers we want.

In this chapter $$G_x$$ is the turn about $$O$$ by $$x$$ radians, anticlockwise when $$x$$ is
positive and clockwise when $$x$$ is negative (:ref{#radians-turn-notation}). So the seat, turned by
$$x$$, is at $$G_x(E_1)$$.

:::definition[Cosine and sine]{#sine-cosine}

Let $$x$$ be a real number. Turn the point $$E_1 = (1, 0)$$ about $$O$$ by $$x$$ radians. The point
where it lands has coordinates written $$(\cos x, \sin x)$$:

```math
(\cos x, \sin x) = G_x(E_1)
```

The first coordinate, $$\cos x$$, is the **cosine** of $$x$$, read "cos $$x$$". The second,
$$\sin x$$, is the **sine** of $$x$$, read "sine $$x$$".

:::

So cosine and sine are names for the two coordinates of one point, nothing more mysterious.
$$\cos x$$ says how far across the point is: to the right of the $$y$$-axis when it is positive, to
the left when it is negative. $$\sin x$$ says how far up it is: above the $$x$$-axis when it is
positive, below when it is negative.


Each real number $$x$$ gives one point, so it has exactly one cosine and one sine. That makes cosine
and sine functions on all real numbers (:ref{#functions/function}). The brackets round the input are
usually left out when it is a single letter or number, as in $$\sin x$$ and $$\cos 2$$. When the
input is longer, keep them: $$\sin(x + 1)$$ is the sine of the number $$x + 1$$, which is a
different thing from $$\sin x + 1$$.

Move the slider below to turn the point, and watch the blue and orange segments change. The diagram
starts at $$x = 2.5$$, about $$143.2^\circ$$, where the point is left of the $$y$$-axis and above
the $$x$$-axis. So its cosine is negative and its sine is positive.

The definition says where the point is. It doesn't give a recipe for working out $$\cos x$$ from the
number $$x$$. As in the Isometries chapter, the size of an angle and the turn by it come from the
picture, with the [rules for rotations](../isometries/slides-and-turns.mdx) taken as given, and this
chapter builds on those rules rather than computing angles from formulas. For most $$x$$ we read the
values off a calculator, which gives $$\cos 1 \approx 0.5403$$ and $$\sin 1 \approx 0.8415$$. The
guide [Special angles](./special-angles.mdx) finds exact values for a few angles.

## Sine and cosine at the quarter turns

At four places on the wheel you can read the answer without a calculator: the seat far right, at the
top, far left and at the bottom. These are the quarter turns, and the moves that reach them already
have formulas. A quarter turn sends $$(a, b)$$ to $$(-b, a)$$, and a half turn sends it to
$$(-a, -b)$$.

:::proposition[Sine and cosine at the quarter turns]{#quarter-values}

| $$x$$      | $$0$$       | $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$ | $$\pi$$       | $$\frac{3\pi}{2}$$ | $$2\pi$$      |
| ---------- | ----------- | ----------------- | ------------- | ------------------ | ------------- |
| in degrees | $$0^\circ$$ | $$90^\circ$$      | $$180^\circ$$ | $$270^\circ$$      | $$360^\circ$$ |
| the point  | $$E_1$$     | $$E_2$$           | $$-E_1$$      | $$-E_2$$           | $$E_1$$       |
| $$\cos x$$ | $$1$$       | $$0$$             | $$-1$$        | $$0$$              | $$1$$         |
| $$\sin x$$ | $$0$$       | $$1$$             | $$0$$         | $$-1$$             | $$0$$         |

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine_and_cosine#Unit_circle_definition"}

Each column finds $$G_x(E_1)$$ for one value of $$x$$, using the parts of :ref{#radian-turns}.

- **For** $$x = 0$$: $$G_0$$ is the identity (part 2), so $$G_0(E_1) = E_1 = (1, 0)$$.
- **For** $$x = \frac{\pi}{2}$$ ($$90^\circ$$): $$G_{\pi/2}(a, b) = (-b, a)$$ (part 4), so
  $$G_{\pi/2}(1, 0) = (0, 1) = E_2$$.
- **For** $$x = \pi$$ ($$180^\circ$$): $$G_\pi(a, b) = (-a, -b)$$, the half turn about $$O$$ (part
  5). So $$G_\pi(E_1) = (-1, 0) = -E_1$$.
- **For** $$x = \frac{3\pi}{2}$$ ($$270^\circ$$): turning by $$\pi$$ and then by $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$
  is turning by $$\frac{3\pi}{2}$$ (part 1). So
  $$G_{3\pi/2}(E_1) = G_{\pi/2}(-1, 0) = (0, -1) = -E_2$$.
- **For** $$x = 2\pi$$ ($$360^\circ$$): $$G_{2\pi}$$ is the identity (part 2), so
  $$G_{2\pi}(E_1) = E_1 = (1, 0)$$.

In each case $$\cos x$$ is the first coordinate of the point and $$\sin x$$ the second.

:::

The columns for $$0$$ and $$2\pi$$ ($$360^\circ$$) agree, because a full turn brings the seat back
to where it started. Try the buttons $$0$$, $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$ ($$90^\circ$$) and $$\pi$$
($$180^\circ$$) in the unit circle diagram in
[The point on the unit circle](#the-point-on-the-unit-circle): at each of them one segment vanishes
and the other has length $$1$$.

## Every point of the unit circle

A turn keeps every point the same distance from the centre, so the seat never leaves the wheel. And
every point of the wheel is a place the seat reaches, at exactly one moment during the first full
turn. So the points of the unit circle and the angles from $$0$$ up to a full turn match up one to
one.

:::proposition[Every point of the unit circle]{#unit-circle-point}

1. **On the circle**: for every number $$x$$, the point $$(\cos x, \sin x)$$ is on the unit circle
2. **Every point once**: for every point $$U$$ of the unit circle, there is exactly one number $$x$$
   with $$0 \le x < 2\pi$$ such that $$U = (\cos x, \sin x)$$

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_functions#Unit-circle_definitions"}

**On the circle.** By part 3 of :ref{#radian-turns}, the turn $$G_x$$ equals a turn $$G_w$$ with
$$0 \le w < 2\pi$$. By the definition of a turn (:ref{#isometries/rotation}), $$G_w$$ keeps the
distance from $$O$$:

```math
d\big(O, G_x(E_1)\big) = d(O, E_1) = 1
```

So $$(\cos x, \sin x) = G_x(E_1)$$ is on the circle with centre $$O$$ and radius $$1$$
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/circle}).

**Every point once.** Let $$U$$ be a point of the unit circle, so $$d(O, U) = 1 = d(O, E_1)$$, and
$$E_1 \ne O$$. By rule 1 of :ref{#isometries/rotation-rules}, exactly one turn about $$O$$ by
$$y^\circ$$ with $$0 \le y < 360$$ sends $$E_1$$ to $$U$$. That angle has $$x = \frac{\pi y}{180}$$
radians (:ref{#radian}), and $$G_x$$ is that turn (:ref{#radians-turn-notation}). Also
$$0 \le y < 360$$ exactly when $$0 \le x < 2\pi$$. So exactly one $$x$$ with $$0 \le x < 2\pi$$ has
$$G_x(E_1) = U$$, which says $$U = (\cos x, \sin x)$$.

:::

For example, $$(0.6, 0.8)$$ is on the unit circle, since $$0.6^2 + 0.8^2 = 0.36 + 0.64 = 1$$. So it
is $$(\cos x, \sin x)$$ for exactly one $$x$$ from $$0$$ up to $$2\pi$$ ($$360^\circ$$). That $$x$$
is about $$0.93$$ radians, or $$53.1^\circ$$. Finding it takes the inverse buttons on a calculator,
which [Sine and cosine in right triangles](./right-triangles.mdx) explains.

Outside that range there are more answers. Adding a full turn to $$x$$ lands the point in the same
place, as [Whole turns change nothing](#whole-turns-change-nothing) shows.

## Sine squared plus cosine squared

Drop a line from the point straight down to the $$x$$-axis. Together with the radius, it makes a
right triangle whose hypotenuse is the radius, of length $$1$$. Its other two sides are as long as
$$\cos x$$ and $$\sin x$$, apart from their signs. So by the Pythagoras theorem the squares of the
cosine and the sine add up to $$1$$, for every angle.

```plane
scale 3.2
point X1 -0.3 0 hidden
point X2 1.4 0 hidden
point Y1 0 -0.3 hidden
point Y2 0 1.2 hidden
point O 0 0 sw
point P 0.6 0.8 ne
point F 0.6 0 s
segment X1 X2 color=muted
segment Y1 Y2 color=muted
segment O P label="1"
segment O F color=blue label="cos x" side=right
segment F P color=orange label="sin x" side=right
right O F P
angle F O P color=purple label="x" r=0.25
caption "The radius from O to P = (cos x, sin x) is the hypotenuse, of length 1. The other sides are cos x across and sin x up."
```

The square of $$\sin x$$ is written $$\sin^2 x$$, read "sine squared $$x$$". It means
$$(\sin x)^2$$: work out $$\sin x$$ first, and then square it. Putting the $$2$$ up next to $$\sin$$
saves a pair of brackets, and $$\cos^2 x$$ means $$(\cos x)^2$$ in the same way.

:::theorem[Sine squared plus cosine squared]{#pythagorean-identity}

For every number $$x$$,

```math
\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x = 1
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_trigonometric_identity#Proof_using_the_unit_circle"}

Let $$P = (\cos x, \sin x)$$. Part 1 of :ref{#unit-circle-point} says $$P$$ is on the unit circle,
so $$d(O, P) = 1$$. Work out the same distance with the distance formula
(:ref{#coordinates/distance-formula}), from $$O = (0, 0)$$ to $$P$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
1 &= d(O, P) &&\qquad \text{$P$ is on the unit circle} \\
&= \sqrt{(\cos x - 0)^2 + (\sin x - 0)^2} &&\qquad \text{the distance formula} \\
&= \sqrt{\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x} &&\qquad \text{subtracting $0$ changes nothing} \\
1 &= \cos^2 x + \sin^2 x &&\qquad \text{squaring both sides}
\end{aligned}
```

The last step squares a square root, which gives back the number inside, since
$$\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x$$ is not negative.

:::

Check it with a calculator at $$x = 1$$ (about $$57.3^\circ$$): $$\cos 1 \approx 0.5403$$ and
$$\sin 1 \approx 0.8415$$, and $$0.5403^2 + 0.8415^2 \approx 0.2919 + 0.7081 = 1.0000$$. At
$$x = \pi$$ ($$180^\circ$$) it says $$(-1)^2 + 0^2 = 1$$. The proof doesn't use the triangle at all,
so it works when the point is on an axis and there is no triangle to draw.

One warning about the notation. The raised number in $$\sin^{-1} x$$ is not a power: it names an
angle whose sine is $$x$$, which the guide
[Sine and cosine in right triangles](./right-triangles.mdx) defines (:ref{#inverse-sin-cos}). So
$$\sin^{-1} x$$ is not $$\frac{1}{\sin x}$$. When you want $$\frac{1}{\sin x}$$, write it as a
fraction or as $$(\sin x)^{-1}$$.

:::example[From the sine to the cosine]

Suppose $$\sin x = \frac{3}{5}$$. By :ref{#pythagorean-identity},

```math
\cos^2 x = 1 - \sin^2 x = 1 - \frac{9}{25} = \frac{16}{25}
```

So $$\cos x = \frac{4}{5}$$ or $$\cos x = -\frac{4}{5}$$. The identity can't decide between them,
because there are two points of the unit circle at height $$\frac{3}{5}$$, one on each side of the
$$y$$-axis. If you also know that the point is in the second quadrant, left of the $$y$$-axis, then
$$\cos x = -\frac{4}{5}$$. To two decimal places, that point is the starting position of the unit
circle diagram, at $$x = 2.5$$ ($$143.2^\circ$$), where $$\cos x \approx -0.80$$ and
$$\sin x \approx 0.60$$.

:::

```plane
scale 3.2
point X1 -1.4 0 hidden
point X2 1.4 0 hidden
point Y1 0 -1.4 hidden
point Y2 0 1.4 hidden
point O 0 0 sw
point A 0.8 0.6 ne
point B -0.8 0.6 nw
point L -1.3 0.6 hidden
point R 1.3 0.6 hidden
segment X1 X2 color=muted
segment Y1 Y2 color=muted
circle O 1 color=muted
segment L R color=orange dashed
segment O A color=blue
segment O B color=blue
text 0.8 0.85 "(4/5, 3/5)"
text -0.8 0.85 "(−4/5, 3/5)"
caption "The two points of the unit circle at height 3/5 are (4/5, 3/5) and (−4/5, 3/5): the sine alone leaves two choices for the cosine"
```

Since a square is never negative, the identity also keeps both numbers between $$-1$$ and $$1$$.
That matches the picture: no point of the unit circle is further than $$1$$ across or $$1$$ up from
$$O$$.

:::corollary[Sine and cosine lie between minus one and one]{#sine-and-cosine-bounds}

For every number $$x$$,

```math
-1 \le \cos x \le 1 \qquad -1 \le \sin x \le 1
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Real_Sine_Function_is_Bounded"}

By :ref{#pythagorean-identity}, $$\sin^2 x = 1 - \cos^2 x$$. Since $$\cos^2 x \ge 0$$, this gives
$$\sin^2 x \le 1$$. A number bigger than $$1$$ has a square bigger than $$1$$, and so does a number
smaller than $$-1$$. So $$-1 \le \sin x \le 1$$. Swapping the roles of $$\sin x$$ and $$\cos x$$
gives the same for $$\cos x$$.

:::

## Whole turns change nothing

After one full turn of the wheel, the seat is back where it started. So turning by $$x$$ and turning
by $$x$$ plus a full turn put the seat in the same place, and the cosine and sine come out the same.
Turning a full turn backwards, or several full turns, makes no difference either.

:::proposition[Sine and cosine repeat]{#periodic}

For every number $$x$$ and every integer $$n$$,

```math
\cos(x + 2\pi n) = \cos x \qquad \sin(x + 2\pi n) = \sin x
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Sine_and_Cosine_are_Periodic_on_Reals"}

By part 3 of :ref{#radian-turns}, $$G_{x + 2\pi n} = G_x$$. So the two turns send $$E_1$$ to the
same point:

```math
\big(\cos(x + 2\pi n), \sin(x + 2\pi n)\big) = G_{x + 2\pi n}(E_1) = G_x(E_1) = (\cos x, \sin x)
```

Two points are equal exactly when their coordinates are equal, so the cosines agree and the sines
agree.

:::

For example, $$\cos(2.5 + 2\pi) = \cos 2.5 \approx -0.80$$. A negative angle can be moved into the
first full turn in the same way. The turn by $$-\frac{\pi}{2}$$ ($$-90^\circ$$) is a quarter turn
clockwise, and with $$n = 1$$,

```math
\sin\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = \sin\left(-\frac{\pi}{2} + 2\pi\right) = \sin\frac{3\pi}{2} = -1
```

using :ref{#quarter-values}. A quarter turn clockwise lands the seat at the bottom, as three quarter
turns anticlockwise do.

## Signs in each quadrant

The cosine is positive when the point is to the right of the $$y$$-axis, and the sine is positive
when it is above the $$x$$-axis. So the quadrant the point lands in decides both signs. A memory aid
in words: cosine is across, so it is positive on the right half of the circle; sine is up, so it is
positive on the top half.

```plane
scale 3.2
point X1 -1.4 0 hidden
point X2 1.4 0 hidden
point Y1 0 -1.4 hidden
point Y2 0 1.4 hidden
point O 0 0 sw
segment X1 X2 color=muted
segment Y1 Y2 color=muted
circle O 1 color=muted
text 0.55 0.55 "(+, +)" color=green
text -0.55 0.55 "(−, +)" color=blue
text -0.55 -0.55 "(−, −)" color=orange
text 0.55 -0.55 "(+, −)" color=purple
caption "The signs of (cos x, sin x) in each quadrant: both positive top right, then anticlockwise (−, +), (−, −) and (+, −)"
```

:::proposition[Signs in each quadrant]{#quadrant-signs}

1. **First quadrant**: for $$0 < x < \frac{\pi}{2}$$ ($$0^\circ$$ to $$90^\circ$$), $$\cos x > 0$$
   and $$\sin x > 0$$
2. **Second quadrant**: for $$\frac{\pi}{2} < x < \pi$$ ($$90^\circ$$ to $$180^\circ$$),
   $$\cos x < 0$$ and $$\sin x > 0$$
3. **Third quadrant**: for $$\pi < x < \frac{3\pi}{2}$$ ($$180^\circ$$ to $$270^\circ$$),
   $$\cos x < 0$$ and $$\sin x < 0$$
4. **Fourth quadrant**: for $$\frac{3\pi}{2} < x < 2\pi$$ ($$270^\circ$$ to $$360^\circ$$),
   $$\cos x > 0$$ and $$\sin x < 0$$

The same holds for $$x + 2\pi n$$ in place of $$x$$, for every integer $$n$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://www.mathsisfun.com/geometry/unit-circle.html"}

**First quadrant.** Let $$0 < t < \frac{\pi}{2}$$, that is, between $$0^\circ$$ and $$90^\circ$$.
Turning the positive $$x$$-axis anticlockwise by less than a right angle carries it into the quarter
of the plane between the positive $$x$$-axis and the positive $$y$$-axis, short of the $$y$$-axis.
We take this from the picture. So $$G_t(E_1) = (a, b)$$ with $$a > 0$$ and $$b > 0$$: it is in the
first quadrant (:ref{#coordinates/quadrants}).

**The other quadrants.** Write $$x$$ as a quarter turn, a half turn or three quarter turns, plus
such a $$t$$. Turns add (part 1 of :ref{#radian-turns}), so the point for $$x$$ is the point
$$(a, b)$$ for $$t$$, turned further. Parts 4 and 5 give the formulas for the extra turns:

- **Second**: if $$x = \frac{\pi}{2} + t$$, the point is $$G_{\pi/2}(a, b) = (-b, a)$$, with
  $$-b < 0$$ and $$a > 0$$
- **Third**: if $$x = \pi + t$$, the point is $$G_\pi(a, b) = (-a, -b)$$, with both coordinates
  negative
- **Fourth**: if $$x = \frac{3\pi}{2} + t$$, the point is $$G_{\pi/2}(-a, -b) = (b, -a)$$, with
  $$b > 0$$ and $$-a < 0$$

Each range for $$x$$ in the statement is exactly the values $$\frac{\pi}{2} + t$$, $$\pi + t$$ or
$$\frac{3\pi}{2} + t$$ with $$0 < t < \frac{\pi}{2}$$. The last sentence of the statement follows
from :ref{#periodic}, since $$x + 2\pi n$$ gives the same point as $$x$$.

:::

The proof found more than signs: it shows the point for $$x + \frac{\pi}{2}$$ is the point for $$x$$
given a quarter turn. The guide [Identities you can see](./symmetries.mdx) turns facts like that
into formulas.

:::example[Which quadrant]

Take $$x = 4$$, about $$229.2^\circ$$. Since $$\pi \approx 3.14$$ and
$$\frac{3\pi}{2} \approx 4.71$$, the number $$4$$ is between $$\pi$$ and $$\frac{3\pi}{2}$$. So the
point is in the third quadrant, and both coordinates are negative. A calculator agrees:
$$\cos 4 \approx -0.65$$ and $$\sin 4 \approx -0.76$$.

:::

## Points further from the centre

A real Ferris wheel is bigger than $$1$$ unit. Picture a wheel of radius $$20$$ m beside the unit
circle, both turned by the same angle: the big seat is $$20$$ times as far across and $$20$$ times
as far up as the small one. So turning and then scaling by $$20$$ gives the same point as scaling
and then turning.

:::proposition[Turning keeps rays]{#turn-scales}

For every number $$x$$, every point $$P$$ and every number $$c > 0$$,

```math
G_x(cP) = c\,G_x(P)
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_(mathematics)#Two_dimensions"}

By part 3 of :ref{#radian-turns}, $$G_x$$ equals a turn $$G_w$$ with $$0 \le w < 2\pi$$, so we may
take $$0 \le x < 2\pi$$. If $$P = O$$, both sides are $$O$$, since a turn about $$O$$ keeps $$O$$
where it is and $$cO = O$$. So let $$P \ne O$$, and write $$Q = G_x(P)$$ and $$M = G_x(cP)$$.

**The distance.** By :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/distance-norm} and
:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/norm-scale}, $$d(O, cP) = |cP| = c\,|P|$$. A turn keeps the distance
from $$O$$ (:ref{#isometries/rotation}), so $$|M| = c\,|P|$$, which is not $$0$$.

**Scaling back.** Let $$N = \frac{1}{c}M$$. By :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/norm-scale},

```math
|N| = \frac{1}{c}\,|M| = \frac{1}{c} \cdot c\,|P| = |P|
```

**The rays.** The point $$cP$$ is a positive multiple of $$P$$, so the ray from $$O$$ through $$cP$$
is the ray from $$O$$ through $$P$$ (:ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/rays-equal}, with the rays read
as in :ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/ray-agrees}). In the same way, $$N$$ is a positive multiple of
$$M$$, so the ray from $$O$$ through $$N$$ is the ray from $$O$$ through $$M$$.

**The angle.** By the definition of a turn, the angle turning anticlockwise from the ray $$O(cP)$$
to the ray $$OM$$ measures $$x$$ radians. By the step before, these are the rays $$OP$$ and $$ON$$.
So the angle turning anticlockwise from $$OP$$ to $$ON$$ measures $$x$$ radians.

**The conclusion.** So $$N$$ has both properties that the definition of a turn asks of $$G_x(P)$$:
it is as far from $$O$$ as $$P$$, and the angle from $$OP$$ to $$ON$$ is $$x$$. The definition
speaks of the one point with these two properties, so $$N = Q$$. Then $$M = cN = c\,Q$$, by
:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/scale-twice}, which is $$G_x(cP) = c\,G_x(P)$$.

:::

The name says what the proof found: the turn carries the ray from $$O$$ through $$P$$ onto the ray
from $$O$$ through $$G_x(P)$$, and keeps each point's distance along it. With $$P = E_1$$, the point
$$(r, 0) = rE_1$$ at distance $$r$$ along the positive $$x$$-axis turns to $$r$$ times the point on
the unit circle.

:::corollary[Points at any distance from the centre]{#circle-point-radius}

Let $$r > 0$$ and let $$x$$ be a number. Then

```math
G_x(r, 0) = (r\cos x, r\sin x)
```

So if a point $$X$$ is at distance $$r$$ from $$O$$, and the angle turning anticlockwise from the
ray $$OE_1$$ to the ray $$OX$$ measures $$x$$ radians, with $$0 \le x < 2\pi$$, then
$$X = (r\cos x, r\sin x)$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_coordinate_system#Converting_between_polar_and_Cartesian_coordinates"}

**The formula.** Since $$(r, 0) = rE_1$$ (:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/scalar-multiple}), and
$$r > 0$$, :ref{#turn-scales} gives

```math
G_x(rE_1) = r\,G_x(E_1) = r(\cos x, \sin x) = (r\cos x, r\sin x)
```

**The point X.** The point $$rE_1$$ is at distance $$r$$ from $$O$$, and the ray from $$O$$ through
it is the ray $$OE_1$$, since it is a positive multiple of $$E_1$$
(:ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/rays-equal}). So $$X$$ is as far from $$O$$ as $$rE_1$$, and the
angle turning anticlockwise from the ray through $$rE_1$$ to the ray $$OX$$ measures $$x$$. Those
are the two properties of $$G_x(rE_1)$$ in the definition of a turn (:ref{#isometries/rotation}), so
$$X = G_x(rE_1) = (r\cos x, r\sin x)$$.

:::

:::example[Where the seat is]

Put the hub of a Ferris wheel of radius $$20$$ m at $$O$$, with distances in metres, and start a
seat at $$(20, 0)$$, level with the hub on the right. After a quarter turn, $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$
($$90^\circ$$), the seat is at

```math
\left(20\cos\frac{\pi}{2}, 20\sin\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = (20 \cdot 0, 20 \cdot 1) = (0, 20)
```

straight above the hub, $$20$$ m up. After a turn of $$2$$ radians (about $$114.6^\circ$$), a
calculator gives $$\cos 2 \approx -0.4161$$ and $$\sin 2 \approx 0.9093$$, so the seat is at about

```math
(20 \cdot (-0.4161), 20 \cdot 0.9093) \approx (-8.32, 18.19)
```

That is $$8.32$$ m to the left of the hub and $$18.19$$ m above it. In
[Special angles](./special-angles.mdx) we'll find the seat after a turn of $$\frac{\pi}{6}$$
($$30^\circ$$) exactly, without a calculator.

## Radian mode and degree mode

A calculator can measure angles in degrees or in radians, and it doesn't ask which you mean. It uses
whichever mode it is set to. So the same keys can give two different answers, and only one of them
is the one you wanted.

Type $$\sin 30$$ meaning $$30^\circ$$. In degree mode you get $$0.5$$. In radian mode you get about
$$-0.988$$, the sine of $$30$$ radians. That is about $$1718.9^\circ$$, four full turns and then
$$278.9^\circ$$ more, which lands in the fourth quadrant, where the sine is negative. Neither answer
is a mistake by the calculator; it answered a different question.

In this chapter, $$\sin x$$ always means $$x$$ in radians. When an angle is given in degrees, write
the degree sign: $$\sin 30^\circ$$ means the sine of the angle of $$30^\circ$$, which is
$$\sin\frac{\pi}{6}$$ (:ref{#radian}). Before a calculation, test the mode with an answer you know:
in radian mode $$\sin\frac{\pi}{2}$$ gives $$1$$, and in degree mode $$\sin 90$$ gives $$1$$.

::::exercise{#ex-sine-and-cosine-read-off}

For a certain $$x$$, the turn $$G_x$$ sends $$E_1$$ to $$(-0.28, 0.96)$$. Write down $$\cos x$$ and
$$\sin x$$, check that $$\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x = 1$$, and say which quadrant the point is in.

:::solution

By :ref{#sine-cosine}, $$\cos x$$ and $$\sin x$$ are the coordinates of $$G_x(E_1)$$, so
$$\cos x = -0.28$$ and $$\sin x = 0.96$$. Then

```math
(-0.28)^2 + 0.96^2 = 0.0784 + 0.9216 = 1
```

as :ref{#pythagorean-identity} says it must be. The point is left of the $$y$$-axis and above the
$$x$$-axis, so it is in the second quadrant.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-sine-and-cosine-whole-turns}

Use the quarter turns and whole turns to find $$\cos x$$ and $$\sin x$$ for each angle:

1. $$x = \frac{5\pi}{2}$$ ($$450^\circ$$)
2. $$x = -\pi$$ ($$-180^\circ$$)
3. $$x = 3\pi$$ ($$540^\circ$$)
4. $$x = -\frac{\pi}{2}$$ ($$-90^\circ$$)

:::solution

In each case add or take away whole turns of $$2\pi$$ ($$360^\circ$$) to reach the table in
:ref{#quarter-values}, using :ref{#periodic}.

1. $$\frac{5\pi}{2} = \frac{\pi}{2} + 2\pi$$, so the point is $$E_2$$: $$\cos x = 0$$ and
   $$\sin x = 1$$.
2. $$-\pi = \pi - 2\pi$$, so the point is $$-E_1$$: $$\cos x = -1$$ and $$\sin x = 0$$.
3. $$3\pi = \pi + 2\pi$$, so again the point is $$-E_1$$: $$\cos x = -1$$ and $$\sin x = 0$$.
4. $$-\frac{\pi}{2} = \frac{3\pi}{2} - 2\pi$$, so the point is $$-E_2$$: $$\cos x = 0$$ and
   $$\sin x = -1$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-sine-and-cosine-signs}

Without a calculator, give the signs of $$\cos x$$ and $$\sin x$$ for $$x = 2$$, $$x = -1$$ and
$$x = 7$$ (radians). Use $$\pi \approx 3.14$$. Then check with a calculator in radian mode.

:::solution

Use :ref{#quadrant-signs}, with $$\frac{\pi}{2} \approx 1.57$$, $$\pi \approx 3.14$$,
$$\frac{3\pi}{2} \approx 4.71$$ and $$2\pi \approx 6.28$$:

- **For** $$x = 2$$: $$1.57 < 2 < 3.14$$, so second quadrant: $$\cos 2 < 0$$ and $$\sin 2 > 0$$. A
  calculator gives $$\cos 2 \approx -0.42$$ and $$\sin 2 \approx 0.91$$.
- **For** $$x = -1$$: add $$2\pi$$ to get about $$5.28$$, between $$4.71$$ and $$6.28$$, so fourth
  quadrant: $$\cos(-1) > 0$$ and $$\sin(-1) < 0$$. A calculator gives about $$0.54$$ and $$-0.84$$.
- **For** $$x = 7$$: take away $$2\pi$$ to get about $$0.72$$, between $$0$$ and $$1.57$$, so first
  quadrant: both positive. A calculator gives $$\cos 7 \approx 0.75$$ and $$\sin 7 \approx 0.66$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-sine-and-cosine-third-quadrant}

Suppose $$\cos x = -\frac{5}{13}$$, and $$\pi < x < \frac{3\pi}{2}$$. Find $$\sin x$$.

:::solution

By :ref{#pythagorean-identity},

```math
\sin^2 x = 1 - \cos^2 x = 1 - \frac{25}{169} = \frac{144}{169}
```

So $$\sin x = \frac{12}{13}$$ or $$\sin x = -\frac{12}{13}$$. Since $$\pi < x < \frac{3\pi}{2}$$,
the point is in the third quadrant, where the sine is negative (:ref{#quadrant-signs}). So
$$\sin x = -\frac{12}{13}$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-sine-and-cosine-not-a-point}

A classmate says that for some $$x$$, $$\cos x = 0.7$$ and $$\sin x = 0.7$$. Show that this is
impossible, and say what it means about the point $$(0.7, 0.7)$$.

:::solution

If it were true, :ref{#pythagorean-identity} would give $$0.7^2 + 0.7^2 = 1$$. But
$$0.49 + 0.49 = 0.98$$, which is not $$1$$. So no such $$x$$ exists.

In the picture, the point $$(0.7, 0.7)$$ is at distance $$\sqrt{0.98} \approx 0.99$$ from $$O$$, a
little inside the unit circle. Every point $$(\cos x, \sin x)$$ is on the circle
(:ref{#unit-circle-point}), so $$(0.7, 0.7)$$ is not one of them, however close it is.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-sine-and-cosine-roundabout}

A seat on a roundabout in a playground is $$3$$ m from the centre. With the centre at $$O$$ and
distances in metres, the seat starts at $$(3, 0)$$, and the roundabout turns anticlockwise by
$$1.2$$ radians. Find where the seat ends up, to two decimal places, and check that it is still
$$3$$ m from the centre.

:::solution

By :ref{#circle-point-radius}, the seat ends at $$(3\cos 1.2, 3\sin 1.2)$$. A calculator in radian
mode gives $$\cos 1.2 \approx 0.3624$$ and $$\sin 1.2 \approx 0.9320$$, so the seat is at about
$$(1.09, 2.80)$$.

Its distance from $$O$$ is

```math
\sqrt{(3\cos 1.2)^2 + (3\sin 1.2)^2} = \sqrt{9\,(\cos^2 1.2 + \sin^2 1.2)} = \sqrt{9} = 3
```

using :ref{#pythagorean-identity}. With the rounded numbers, $$1.09^2 + 2.80^2 = 1.1881 + 7.84$$,
which is about $$9.03$$, close to $$9$$ as it should be.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-sine-and-cosine-sum-of-squares}

Show that for every number $$x$$,

```math
(\cos x + \sin x)^2 + (\cos x - \sin x)^2 = 2
```

Then check it at $$x = \frac{\pi}{2}$$ ($$90^\circ$$).

:::solution

Multiply out each square, one step at a time:

```math
\begin{aligned}
(\cos x + \sin x)^2 &= \cos^2 x + 2\cos x\sin x + \sin^2 x &&\qquad \text{square of a sum} \\
(\cos x - \sin x)^2 &= \cos^2 x - 2\cos x\sin x + \sin^2 x &&\qquad \text{square of a difference}
\end{aligned}
```

Adding them, the middle terms cancel, and :ref{#pythagorean-identity} finishes it:

```math
\begin{aligned}
(\cos x + \sin x)^2 + (\cos x - \sin x)^2 &= 2\cos^2 x + 2\sin^2 x &&\qquad \text{adding} \\
&= 2\,(\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x) &&\qquad \text{taking out the } 2 \\
&= 2 &&\qquad \text{sine squared plus cosine squared}
\end{aligned}
```

At $$x = \frac{\pi}{2}$$, $$\cos x = 0$$ and $$\sin x = 1$$ (:ref{#quarter-values}), and the left
side is $$1^2 + (-1)^2 = 2$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-sine-and-cosine-zero-cosine}

Show that $$\cos x = 0$$ exactly when $$\sin x = 1$$ or $$\sin x = -1$$.

:::solution

By :ref{#pythagorean-identity}, $$\cos^2 x = 1 - \sin^2 x$$.

If $$\cos x = 0$$, then $$0 = 1 - \sin^2 x$$, so $$\sin^2 x = 1$$. The only numbers whose square is
$$1$$ are $$1$$ and $$-1$$, so $$\sin x = 1$$ or $$\sin x = -1$$.

Conversely, if $$\sin x = 1$$ or $$\sin x = -1$$, then $$\sin^2 x = 1$$, so $$\cos^2 x = 0$$, and
the only number whose square is $$0$$ is $$0$$. So $$\cos x = 0$$.

In the picture, the points of the unit circle with first coordinate $$0$$ are the top and the
bottom, $$E_2$$ and $$-E_2$$.

:::
