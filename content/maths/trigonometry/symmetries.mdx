Imagine a clock on the wall opposite a bathroom mirror. In the mirror, the hour hand that points at
2 seems to point at 10, and while the real second hand sweeps clockwise, the one in the mirror
sweeps anticlockwise. Nothing about the clock has changed except the way round it goes. A mirror
reverses every turn.

That one picture explains a whole family of **trigonometric identities**: equations involving sine
and cosine that are true for every value of $$x$$, like $$\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x = 1$$
(:ref{#pythagorean-identity}). Each identity in this guide comes from moving the point on the unit
circle with a flip or a turn whose formula we already know, and seeing where it lands. In this guide
we'll prove that a flip reverses a turn, and then watch five identities happen, one for each move.

## One idea behind every identity

The point on the unit circle for an angle $$x$$ is where $$E_1 = (1, 0)$$ lands after a turn by
$$x$$ about the origin, and its coordinates are $$(\cos x, \sin x)$$ (:ref{#sine-cosine}): cosine is
how far across, sine is how far up. Now move that point with a flip or a turn about the origin. It
stays on the unit circle, so it lands on the point for some other angle. The formula for the move
gives its coordinates one way, the new angle gives them another way, and the two answers must agree.

Here is the idea with numbers. Take $$x = \frac{\pi}{3}$$ ($$60^\circ$$), whose point is
$$P = \left(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)$$ (:ref{#sin-cos-30-60}). The half turn about the
origin sends each point to its negative (:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/half-turn-formula}), so it
sends $$P$$ to $$\left(-\frac{1}{2}, -\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)$$. But by part 5 of
:ref{#radian-turns} the half turn is also the turn by $$\pi$$ ($$180^\circ$$), so it turns the point
for $$\frac{\pi}{3}$$ into the point for $$\frac{\pi}{3} + \pi = \frac{4\pi}{3}$$ ($$240^\circ$$).
Comparing the two answers gives $$\cos\frac{4\pi}{3} = -\frac{1}{2}$$ and
$$\sin\frac{4\pi}{3} = -\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$.

Nothing in that argument depended on $$x$$ being $$\frac{\pi}{3}$$, and the same goes for the other
moves we have formulas for. The table lists all five. In the formula column, $$a$$ and $$b$$ stand
for the coordinates of any point, since the letter $$x$$ is busy being the angle.

| Move                   | Formula                     | $$(\cos x, \sin x)$$ goes to | The point for         | Identity                                                                    |
| ---------------------- | --------------------------- | ---------------------------- | --------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Flip in the $$x$$-axis | $$(a, b) \mapsto (a, -b)$$  | $$(\cos x, -\sin x)$$        | $$-x$$                | $$\cos(-x) = \cos x$$, $$\sin(-x) = -\sin x$$                               |
| Half turn              | $$(a, b) \mapsto (-a, -b)$$ | $$(-\cos x, -\sin x)$$       | $$x + \pi$$           | $$\cos(x + \pi) = -\cos x$$, $$\sin(x + \pi) = -\sin x$$                    |
| Quarter turn           | $$(a, b) \mapsto (-b, a)$$  | $$(-\sin x, \cos x)$$        | $$x + \frac{\pi}{2}$$ | $$\cos(x + \frac{\pi}{2}) = -\sin x$$, $$\sin(x + \frac{\pi}{2}) = \cos x$$ |
| Flip in the $$y$$-axis | $$(a, b) \mapsto (-a, b)$$  | $$(-\cos x, \sin x)$$        | $$\pi - x$$           | $$\cos(\pi - x) = -\cos x$$, $$\sin(\pi - x) = \sin x$$                     |
| Flip in $$y = x$$      | $$(a, b) \mapsto (b, a)$$   | $$(\sin x, \cos x)$$         | $$\frac{\pi}{2} - x$$ | $$\cos(\frac{\pi}{2} - x) = \sin x$$, $$\sin(\frac{\pi}{2} - x) = \cos x$$  |

Try each move below. Slide $$x$$ round the circle and watch where the image $$P'$$ lands, and check
the identity in the readout with the numbers.

The two turns in the table follow from the rule that turns add. The three flips need one more fact,
the one the mirror and the clock show: a flip reverses the direction of a turn.

## A flip reverses a turn

Turn a point anticlockwise and then look at it in a mirror lying along the $$x$$-axis. The mirror
shows the flipped point turned the same amount clockwise, as the clock on the bathroom wall does. So
"turn by $$x$$, then flip" should land where "flip, then turn by $$x$$ the other way" lands.

Here it is with numbers, for a quarter turn. The quarter turn anticlockwise sends $$(a, b)$$ to
$$(-b, a)$$ (:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/quarter-turn}), and the quarter turn clockwise sends
$$(a, b)$$ to $$(b, -a)$$ (:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/combining-moves-flips}, part 3). Start at
$$(3, 1)$$:

- **Turn, then flip**: the quarter turn sends $$(3, 1)$$ to $$(-1, 3)$$, and the flip
  $$R_1(a, b) = (a, -b)$$ sends that to $$(-1, -3)$$
- **Flip, then turn back**: the flip sends $$(3, 1)$$ to $$(3, -1)$$, and the clockwise quarter turn
  sends that to $$(-1, -3)$$

Both orders end at the same point. The lemma says this happens for every angle and every point. As
everywhere in this chapter, $$G_x$$ is the turn about $$O$$ by $$x$$ radians, anticlockwise when
$$x > 0$$ and clockwise when $$x < 0$$ (:ref{#radians-turn-notation}).

:::lemma[A flip reverses turns]{#flip-reverses-turn}

Let $$R_1(a, b) = (a, -b)$$ be the flip in the $$x$$-axis. For every number $$x$$,

```math
R_1 \circ G_x = G_{-x} \circ R_1
```

So turning by $$x$$ and then flipping has the same effect as flipping and then turning by $$x$$ the
other way.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotations_and_reflections_in_two_dimensions#Mathematical_expression"}

The idea is that a turn is two flips. By :ref{#isometries/crossing-mirrors}, flipping in the
$$x$$-axis and then in a second mirror is a turn, and flipping in the same two mirrors in the other
order is the same size of turn the other way. So we write $$G_x$$ as two flips, one of them $$R_1$$,
and then regroup.

The crossing-mirrors theorem talks about the angle you turn through, anticlockwise, from one line
through $$O$$ to another. Such an angle is less than a half turn, since a half turn brings a line
through $$O$$ back onto itself (:ref{#isometries/half-turn}). So we first make sure every angle we
use lies between $$0$$ and $$\pi$$ ($$180^\circ$$).

**Step 1: an angle less than a full turn.** Write $$x = w + 2\pi n$$, with $$n$$ an integer and
$$0 \le w < 2\pi$$ (part 3 of :ref{#radian-turns}). Then $$-x = -w + 2\pi(-n)$$, so by the same
part,

```math
G_x = G_w \qquad G_{-x} = G_{-w}
```

So it is enough to prove $$R_1 \circ G_w = G_{-w} \circ R_1$$. If $$w = 0$$, both sides are $$R_1$$,
since $$G_0$$ is the identity. From now on $$0 < w < 2\pi$$, and we write $$\theta = \frac{w}{2}$$,
so that $$0 < \theta < \pi$$.

**Step 2: the second mirror.** Let $$Q = G_\theta(E_1)$$, which is on the unit circle, so
$$Q \ne O$$. Let $$K$$ be the line through $$O$$ and $$Q$$. The turn $$G_\theta$$ keeps $$O$$ where
it is and sends $$E_1$$ to $$Q$$, and it sends lines to lines (:ref{#isometries/basic-isometries},
:ref{#isometries/isometry-lines}). So it carries the $$x$$-axis, the line through $$O$$ and $$E_1$$,
onto $$K$$: turning anticlockwise by $$\theta$$ takes the $$x$$-axis to $$K$$. By
:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/axis-reflections}, $$R_1$$ is the flip in the $$x$$-axis. Write
$$R_K$$ for the flip in $$K$$.

**Step 3: flip in the x-axis, then in K.** By :ref{#isometries/crossing-mirrors}, with $$L$$ the
$$x$$-axis, $$R_K \circ R_1$$ is the turn about $$O$$ by twice $$\theta$$. That theorem counts in
degrees, but doubling an angle doubles it in either unit: $$\theta$$ radians is
$$\frac{180\theta}{\pi}$$ degrees, twice that is $$\frac{360\theta}{\pi}$$ degrees, and that is
$$2\theta$$ radians (:ref{#radians-turn-notation}). So

```math
R_K \circ R_1 = G_{2\theta} = G_w
```

**Step 4: flip in K, then in the x-axis.** Now turn on from $$K$$ by $$\pi - \theta$$. By parts 1
and 5 of :ref{#radian-turns}, turns add and $$G_\pi$$ is the half turn, which sends each point to
its negative:

```math
G_{\pi - \theta}(Q) = G_{\pi - \theta}\big(G_\theta(E_1)\big) = G_\pi(E_1) = -E_1 = (-1, 0)
```

So the turn by $$\pi - \theta$$ keeps $$O$$ and sends $$Q$$ to a point of the $$x$$-axis. As in step
2, it carries $$K$$ onto the line through $$O$$ and $$(-1, 0)$$, which is the $$x$$-axis. Since
$$0 < \theta < \pi$$, the angle $$\pi - \theta$$ is between $$0$$ and $$\pi$$ too. By
:ref{#isometries/crossing-mirrors} again, now with $$K$$ as the first mirror and the $$x$$-axis as
the second, $$R_1 \circ R_K$$ is the turn by $$2(\pi - \theta) = 2\pi - w$$. By part 3 of
:ref{#radian-turns}, turns that differ by $$2\pi$$ are equal, so

```math
R_1 \circ R_K = G_{2\pi - w} = G_{-w}
```

**Step 5: regroup.** Using step 3, then :ref{#isometries/composite-associative} to move the
brackets, then step 4:

```math
\begin{aligned}
R_1 \circ G_w &= R_1 \circ (R_K \circ R_1) && \text{step 3} \\
&= (R_1 \circ R_K) \circ R_1 && \text{grouping doesn't matter} \\
&= G_{-w} \circ R_1 && \text{step 4}
\end{aligned}
```

By step 1, that is $$R_1 \circ G_x = G_{-x} \circ R_1$$.

:::

The proof leans on :ref{#isometries/crossing-mirrors}, whose proof reads the angles off the picture.
That is the footing of this whole chapter: angle sizes come from the picture, and trigonometry is
built on top of them.

The lemma can also be written without $$R_1$$ on the right. Flip first and then apply either side of
the lemma. On the right the two flips cancel, since $$R_1 \circ R_1 = I$$
(:ref{#isometries/reflection-twice}), so $$R_1 \circ G_x \circ R_1 = G_{-x}$$. In words: flip, turn
and flip back, and you have turned the other way, as the clock in the bathroom mirror shows.

## Negative angles

Turning $$E_1$$ clockwise by an angle lands at the mirror image, in the $$x$$-axis, of where turning
anticlockwise by the same angle lands. The two points are equally far across. One is as far below
the axis as the other is above it.

```plane
scale 3.2
point X1 -1.3 0 hidden
point X2 1.3 0 hidden
point Y1 0 -1.3 hidden
point Y2 0 1.3 hidden
point O 0 0 nw
point E 1 0 hidden
point P 0.866 0.5 ne
point P' 0.866 -0.5 se
segment Y1 Y2 color=muted
circle O 1 color=muted
line X1 X2 color=purple
segment P P' dashed
angle E O P color=blue arrow r=0.3 label="x"
angle P' O E color=orange r=0.45 label="x"
caption "The point P for x = π/6 (30°) and its mirror image P′ in the x-axis. P′ is the point for −x, where E₁ lands after turning π/6 clockwise."
```

So the cosines of $$x$$ and $$-x$$ are equal, and their sines are negatives of each other. That
makes cosine an even function and sine an odd one (:ref{#functions/even-odd-function}).

:::proposition[Negative angles]{#negative-angle}

For every number $$x$$,

```math
\cos(-x) = \cos x \qquad \sin(-x) = -\sin x
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_trigonometric_identities#Reflections"}

The flip leaves $$E_1$$ where it is, since $$R_1(1, 0) = (1, -0) = (1, 0)$$. By
:ref{#flip-reverses-turn}, applied to $$E_1$$,

```math
R_1\big(G_x(E_1)\big) = G_{-x}\big(R_1(E_1)\big) = G_{-x}(E_1)
```

Work out each side with :ref{#sine-cosine}. The left side is
$$R_1(\cos x, \sin x) = (\cos x, -\sin x)$$, and the right side is $$(\cos(-x), \sin(-x))$$. Two
points are equal exactly when their coordinates are, so $$\cos(-x) = \cos x$$ and
$$\sin(-x) = -\sin x$$.

:::

As a check, put $$x = \frac{\pi}{6}$$ ($$30^\circ$$), where
$$\cos x = \frac{\sqrt3}{2} \approx 0.866$$ and $$\sin x = \frac{1}{2}$$ (:ref{#sin-cos-30-60}). A
calculator in radian mode gives $$\cos(-0.5236) \approx 0.866$$ and $$\sin(-0.5236) \approx -0.5$$,
as the proposition says.

The minus sign comes out of the sine but disappears from the cosine. It's worth remembering which is
which by the picture: flipping in the $$x$$-axis changes how far up, never how far across.

## A half turn more

Adding $$\pi$$ ($$180^\circ$$) to the angle means turning on by a further half turn. A half turn
about the origin sends every point straight through the origin to the opposite side, at the same
distance. So both coordinates change sign.

```plane
scale 3.2
point X1 -1.3 0 hidden
point X2 1.3 0 hidden
point Y1 0 -1.3 hidden
point Y2 0 1.3 hidden
point O 0 0 nw
point E 1 0 hidden
point P 0.866 0.5 ne
point P' -0.866 -0.5 sw
segment X1 X2 color=muted
segment Y1 Y2 color=muted
circle O 1 color=muted
segment P P' color=purple dashed
angle E O P color=blue arrow r=0.3 label="x"
angle P O P' color=purple arrow r=0.55 label="π"
caption "The half turn sends the point P for x = π/6 (30°) through O to P′, the point for x + π = 7π/6 (210°)."
```

:::proposition[A half turn more]{#half-turn-identities}

For every number $$x$$,

```math
\cos(x + \pi) = -\cos x \qquad \sin(x + \pi) = -\sin x
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_trigonometric_identities#Shifts_and_periodicity"}

By parts 1 and 5 of :ref{#radian-turns}, turning by $$x$$ and then by $$\pi$$ is turning by
$$x + \pi$$, and $$G_\pi$$ is the half turn about $$O$$, which sends each point $$P$$ to $$-P$$. So,
with :ref{#sine-cosine},

```math
\begin{aligned}
(\cos(x + \pi), \sin(x + \pi)) &= G_{x + \pi}(E_1) && \text{the point for } x + \pi \\
&= G_\pi\big(G_x(E_1)\big) && \text{turns add} \\
&= -(\cos x, \sin x) && \text{the half turn} \\
&= (-\cos x, -\sin x)
\end{aligned}
```

Comparing coordinates gives both identities.

:::

As a check, put $$x = \frac{\pi}{6}$$ ($$30^\circ$$), so $$x + \pi = \frac{7\pi}{6}$$
($$210^\circ$$). A calculator in radian mode gives $$\cos 3.6652 \approx -0.866$$ and
$$\sin 3.6652 \approx -0.5$$, which are $$-\cos\frac{\pi}{6}$$ and $$-\sin\frac{\pi}{6}$$.

## A quarter turn more

Adding $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$ ($$90^\circ$$) to the angle means turning on by a quarter turn
anticlockwise. The quarter turn swings the positive $$x$$-axis up onto the positive $$y$$-axis, and
the positive $$y$$-axis round to the left. So what was across becomes up, and what was up becomes
across, backwards.

```plane
scale 3.2
point X1 -1.3 0 hidden
point X2 1.3 0 hidden
point Y1 0 -1.3 hidden
point Y2 0 1.3 hidden
point O 0 0 sw
point E 1 0 hidden
point P 0.866 0.5 ne
point P' -0.5 0.866 nw
segment X1 X2 color=muted
segment Y1 Y2 color=muted
circle O 1 color=muted
segment O P color=blue
segment O P' color=purple
right P O P'
angle E O P color=blue arrow r=0.3 label="x"
caption "The quarter turn sends the point P = (0.87, 0.5) for x = π/6 (30°) to P′ = (−0.5, 0.87), the point for x + π/2 = 2π/3 (120°)."
```

:::proposition[A quarter turn more]{#quarter-turn-identities}

For every number $$x$$,

```math
\cos\left(x + \frac{\pi}{2}\right) = -\sin x \qquad \sin\left(x + \frac{\pi}{2}\right) = \cos x
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_trigonometric_identities#Shifts_and_periodicity"}

By parts 1 and 4 of :ref{#radian-turns}, turning by $$x$$ and then by $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$ is turning
by $$x + \frac{\pi}{2}$$, and $$G_{\pi/2}$$ is the quarter turn $$(a, b) \mapsto (-b, a)$$. So

```math
\begin{aligned}
\left(\cos\left(x + \tfrac{\pi}{2}\right), \sin\left(x + \tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)\right) &= G_{\pi/2}\big(G_x(E_1)\big) && \text{turns add} \\
&= G_{\pi/2}(\cos x, \sin x) && \text{the point for } x \\
&= (-\sin x, \cos x) && \text{the quarter turn}
\end{aligned}
```

Comparing coordinates gives both identities.

:::

As a check, put $$x = \frac{\pi}{6}$$ ($$30^\circ$$), so $$x + \frac{\pi}{2} = \frac{2\pi}{3}$$
($$120^\circ$$). A calculator in radian mode gives $$\cos 2.0944 \approx -0.5$$ and
$$\sin 2.0944 \approx 0.866$$, which are $$-\sin\frac{\pi}{6}$$ and $$\cos\frac{\pi}{6}$$.

It's tempting to read $$\cos\left(x + \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$$ as $$\cos x + \cos\frac{\pi}{2}$$, and
that is a slip almost everyone makes at some point. Cosine doesn't share out over a sum. At
$$x = 0$$ the left side is $$\cos\frac{\pi}{2} = 0$$, but
$$\cos 0 + \cos\frac{\pi}{2} = 1 + 0 = 1$$. The guide [Addition formulas](./addition-formulas.mdx)
finds what the cosine of a sum is (:ref{#addition-formulas}).

## Angles that add to a straight angle

The angles $$x$$ and $$\pi - x$$ add up to $$\pi$$ ($$180^\circ$$), a straight angle. Their points
are mirror images in the $$y$$-axis: at the same height, and equally far across on opposite sides.
So they have the same sine, and cosines of opposite sign.

```plane
scale 3.2
point X1 -1.3 0 hidden
point X2 1.3 0 hidden
point Y1 0 -1.3 hidden
point Y2 0 1.3 hidden
point O 0 0 s
point E 1 0 hidden
point W -1 0 hidden
point P 0.866 0.5 ne
point P' -0.866 0.5 nw
segment X1 X2 color=muted
circle O 1 color=muted
line Y1 Y2 color=purple
segment P P' dashed
angle E O P color=blue arrow r=0.3 label="x"
angle P' O W color=orange r=0.3 label="x"
caption "The flip in the y-axis sends the point P for x = π/6 (30°) to P′, the point for π − x = 5π/6 (150°), at the same height."
```

:::proposition[Angles that add to a straight angle]{#supplement-identities}

For every number $$x$$,

```math
\sin(\pi - x) = \sin x \qquad \cos(\pi - x) = -\cos x
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_trigonometric_identities#Reflections"}

Write $$\pi - x$$ as $$(-x) + \pi$$. By :ref{#half-turn-identities} with $$-x$$ in place of $$x$$,
and then :ref{#negative-angle},

```math
\begin{aligned}
\sin(\pi - x) &= \sin\big((-x) + \pi\big) = -\sin(-x) = -(-\sin x) = \sin x \\
\cos(\pi - x) &= \cos\big((-x) + \pi\big) = -\cos(-x) = -\cos x
\end{aligned}
```

:::

The proof flips the point in the $$x$$-axis and then turns it a half turn. That pair of moves is the
flip in the $$y$$-axis: $$(a, b)$$ goes to $$(a, -b)$$ and then to $$(-a, b)$$, as part 1 of
:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/combining-moves-flips} found. So the algebra and the picture agree.

As a check, put $$x = \frac{\pi}{6}$$ ($$30^\circ$$), so $$\pi - x = \frac{5\pi}{6}$$
($$150^\circ$$). A calculator in radian mode gives $$\sin 2.618 \approx 0.5$$ and
$$\cos 2.618 \approx -0.866$$. This is why an equation such as $$\sin x = \frac{1}{2}$$ has two
answers in a full turn, $$\frac{\pi}{6}$$ and $$\frac{5\pi}{6}$$, as :ref{#same-sine} showed with
the picture.

## Angles that add to a right angle

The angles $$x$$ and $$\frac{\pi}{2} - x$$ add up to $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$ ($$90^\circ$$), a right
angle. Their points are mirror images in the diagonal line $$y = x$$, and that flip swaps the two
coordinates. So the cosine of each angle is the sine of the other.

```plane
scale 3.2
point X1 -1.3 0 hidden
point X2 1.3 0 hidden
point Y1 0 -1.3 hidden
point Y2 0 1.3 hidden
point D1 -1.2 -1.2 hidden
point D2 1.2 1.2 hidden
point O 0 0 se
point E 1 0 hidden
point N 0 1 hidden
point P 0.866 0.5 e
point P' 0.5 0.866 n
segment X1 X2 color=muted
segment Y1 Y2 color=muted
circle O 1 color=muted
line D1 D2 color=purple
segment P P' dashed
angle E O P color=blue arrow r=0.3 label="x"
angle P' O N color=orange r=0.3 label="x"
caption "The flip in y = x sends the point P = (0.87, 0.5) for x = π/6 (30°) to P′ = (0.5, 0.87), the point for π/2 − x = π/3 (60°)."
```

:::proposition[Angles that add to a right angle]{#complement-identities}

For every number $$x$$,

```math
\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - x\right) = \sin x \qquad \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - x\right) = \cos x
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_trigonometric_identities#Reflections"}

Write $$\frac{\pi}{2} - x$$ as $$(-x) + \frac{\pi}{2}$$. By :ref{#quarter-turn-identities} with
$$-x$$ in place of $$x$$, and then :ref{#negative-angle},

```math
\begin{aligned}
\cos\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2} - x\right) &= \cos\left((-x) + \tfrac{\pi}{2}\right) = -\sin(-x) = -(-\sin x) = \sin x \\
\sin\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2} - x\right) &= \sin\left((-x) + \tfrac{\pi}{2}\right) = \cos(-x) = \cos x
\end{aligned}
```

:::

Here too the algebra follows the picture. The proof flips in the $$x$$-axis and then turns a quarter
turn, and $$(a, b)$$ goes to $$(a, -b)$$ and then to $$(b, a)$$: that pair of moves is the flip
$$S$$ in $$y = x$$, as part 3 of :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/combining-moves-flips} found.

As a check, put $$x = \frac{\pi}{6}$$ ($$30^\circ$$), so $$\frac{\pi}{2} - x = \frac{\pi}{3}$$
($$60^\circ$$). By :ref{#sin-cos-30-60}, $$\cos\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{1}{2} = \sin\frac{\pi}{6}$$ and
$$\sin\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2} = \cos\frac{\pi}{6}$$. In a right triangle the two angles
other than the right angle add up to $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$, and :ref{#other-angle} found these
identities there, for $$0 < x < \frac{\pi}{2}$$. The proposition shows they hold for every $$x$$.

The name cosine comes from this identity. Two angles that add up to a right angle are called
**complementary**, and the cosine of an angle is the sine of its complement: the "co" is short for
"complement".

## Round the whole circle

The flips in the two axes and the half turn carry the first quadrant onto each of the other three.
So every point of the unit circle that isn't on an axis is a mirror image of a point in the first
quadrant, and its sine and cosine are those of an angle between $$0$$ and $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$
($$90^\circ$$), with signs changed to suit its quadrant.

```plane
scale 3.2
point X1 -1.3 0 hidden
point X2 1.3 0 hidden
point Y1 0 -1.3 hidden
point Y2 0 1.3 hidden
point O 0 0 hidden
point E 1 0 hidden
point W -1 0 hidden
point A 0.866 0.5 ne
point B -0.866 0.5 nw
point C -0.866 -0.5 sw
point D 0.866 -0.5 se
segment X1 X2 color=muted
segment Y1 Y2 color=muted
circle O 1 color=muted
segment A B dashed
segment B C dashed
segment C D dashed
segment D A dashed
angle E O A color=blue r=0.35 label="π/6"
angle B O W color=orange r=0.35 label="π/6"
angle W O C color=green r=0.35 label="π/6"
angle D O E color=purple r=0.35 label="π/6"
caption "The points for π/6, 5π/6, 7π/6 and 11π/6 (30°, 150°, 210° and 330°). Each makes an angle of π/6 with the x-axis, so they share the numbers ½ and √3/2, with different signs."
```

The angle between the point's ray and the $$x$$-axis does the work, and it has a name.

:::definition[Reference angle]{#symmetries-reference-angle}

Let $$0 \le x < 2\pi$$, with the point for $$x$$ off both axes. The **reference angle** $$r$$ of
$$x$$ is the angle between $$0$$ and $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$ that the ray from $$O$$ through the point
makes with the $$x$$-axis:

- **First quadrant**, $$0 < x < \frac{\pi}{2}$$: $$r = x$$
- **Second quadrant**, $$\frac{\pi}{2} < x < \pi$$: $$r = \pi - x$$
- **Third quadrant**, $$\pi < x < \frac{3\pi}{2}$$: $$r = x - \pi$$
- **Fourth quadrant**, $$\frac{3\pi}{2} < x < 2\pi$$: $$r = 2\pi - x$$

For any other $$x$$ whose point is off the axes, first add or subtract a multiple of $$2\pi$$ to
land between $$0$$ and $$2\pi$$, which changes neither sine nor cosine (:ref{#periodic}).

:::

In each case $$r$$ is between $$0$$ and $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$. For example, in the second quadrant,
$$\frac{\pi}{2} < x < \pi$$ gives $$0 < \pi - x < \frac{\pi}{2}$$.

:::proposition[Sine and cosine from the reference angle]{#symmetries-reference-values}

Let $$x$$ be as in :ref{#symmetries-reference-angle}, with reference angle $$r$$. Then
$$\cos x = \pm\cos r$$ and $$\sin x = \pm\sin r$$, with these signs:

- **First quadrant**: $$\cos x = \cos r$$ and $$\sin x = \sin r$$
- **Second quadrant**: $$\cos x = -\cos r$$ and $$\sin x = \sin r$$
- **Third quadrant**: $$\cos x = -\cos r$$ and $$\sin x = -\sin r$$
- **Fourth quadrant**: $$\cos x = \cos r$$ and $$\sin x = -\sin r$$

:::

:::proof{source="https://www.mathsisfun.com/algebra/trig-four-quadrants.html"}

In the first quadrant $$x = r$$. In the second, $$x = \pi - r$$, and :ref{#supplement-identities}
gives $$\cos x = -\cos r$$ and $$\sin x = \sin r$$. In the third, $$x = r + \pi$$, and
:ref{#half-turn-identities} gives $$\cos x = -\cos r$$ and $$\sin x = -\sin r$$.

In the fourth, $$x = -r + 2\pi$$. By :ref{#periodic} and then :ref{#negative-angle},

```math
\cos x = \cos(-r) = \cos r \qquad \sin x = \sin(-r) = -\sin r
```

:::

The signs are the ones :ref{#quadrant-signs} found from where the point lies: the reference angle
gives the size of each number, and the quadrant gives its sign. Together with the special angles
from [Special angles](./special-angles.mdx), that finds sine and cosine of every multiple of
$$\frac{\pi}{6}$$ or $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$ in three steps:

1. Find the quadrant the point lies in.
2. Find the reference angle, which is $$\frac{\pi}{6}$$, $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$ or $$\frac{\pi}{3}$$.
3. Take its sine or cosine, and give it the sign that the quadrant asks for.

:::example[Three values from the reference angle]

**$$\sin\frac{5\pi}{6}$$.** The angle $$\frac{5\pi}{6}$$ ($$150^\circ$$) is between
$$\frac{\pi}{2}$$ and $$\pi$$, so the point is in the second quadrant. The reference angle is
$$\pi - \frac{5\pi}{6} = \frac{\pi}{6}$$, and sine is positive there. So
$$\sin\frac{5\pi}{6} = \sin\frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{1}{2}$$.

**$$\cos\frac{4\pi}{3}$$.** The angle $$\frac{4\pi}{3}$$ ($$240^\circ$$) is between $$\pi$$ and
$$\frac{3\pi}{2}$$, so the point is in the third quadrant. The reference angle is
$$\frac{4\pi}{3} - \pi = \frac{\pi}{3}$$, and cosine is negative there. So
$$\cos\frac{4\pi}{3} = -\cos\frac{\pi}{3} = -\frac{1}{2}$$, as we found with the half turn at the
start of the guide.

**$$\sin\left(-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)$$.** Here :ref{#negative-angle} does it in one step:
$$\sin\left(-\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = -\sin\frac{\pi}{4} = -\frac{\sqrt2}{2}$$ (:ref{#sin-cos-45}).
The three steps agree: $$-\frac{\pi}{4}$$ ($$-45^\circ$$) plus $$2\pi$$ is $$\frac{7\pi}{4}$$, in
the fourth quadrant, with reference angle $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$ and sine negative.

:::

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on identities you can see, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-symmetries-negative}

Take $$x = \frac{\pi}{3}$$ ($$60^\circ$$). Use :ref{#negative-angle} and :ref{#half-turn-identities}
to find the exact values of $$\cos\left(-\frac{\pi}{3}\right)$$,
$$\sin\left(-\frac{\pi}{3}\right)$$, $$\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{3} + \pi\right)$$ and
$$\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{3} + \pi\right)$$.

:::solution

By :ref{#sin-cos-30-60}, $$\cos\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{1}{2}$$ and
$$\sin\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$. Flipping in the $$x$$-axis keeps the cosine and changes
the sign of the sine:

```math
\cos\left(-\tfrac{\pi}{3}\right) = \cos\tfrac{\pi}{3} = \tfrac{1}{2} \qquad \sin\left(-\tfrac{\pi}{3}\right) = -\sin\tfrac{\pi}{3} = -\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}
```

The half turn changes the sign of both:

```math
\cos\left(\tfrac{\pi}{3} + \pi\right) = -\tfrac{1}{2} \qquad \sin\left(\tfrac{\pi}{3} + \pi\right) = -\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}
```

::::exercise{#ex-symmetries-five-moves}

An angle $$x$$ has $$\cos x = \frac{5}{13}$$ and $$\sin x = \frac{12}{13}$$. Without finding $$x$$,
write down the cosine and sine of each angle:

1. $$-x$$
2. $$x + \pi$$
3. $$x + \frac{\pi}{2}$$
4. $$\pi - x$$
5. $$\frac{\pi}{2} - x$$

:::solution

Each answer is the image of $$P = \left(\frac{5}{13}, \frac{12}{13}\right)$$ under one of the five
moves in the table at the start of the guide:

1. The flip in the $$x$$-axis: $$\cos(-x) = \frac{5}{13}$$, $$\sin(-x) = -\frac{12}{13}$$
2. The half turn: $$\cos(x + \pi) = -\frac{5}{13}$$, $$\sin(x + \pi) = -\frac{12}{13}$$
3. The quarter turn: $$\cos\left(x + \frac{\pi}{2}\right) = -\frac{12}{13}$$,
   $$\sin\left(x + \frac{\pi}{2}\right) = \frac{5}{13}$$
4. The flip in the $$y$$-axis: $$\cos(\pi - x) = -\frac{5}{13}$$, $$\sin(\pi - x) = \frac{12}{13}$$
5. The flip in $$y = x$$: $$\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - x\right) = \frac{12}{13}$$,
   $$\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - x\right) = \frac{5}{13}$$

As a check, each pair still has squares adding up to $$\frac{25 + 144}{169} = 1$$, as every point of
the unit circle must (:ref{#pythagorean-identity}).

:::

::::exercise{#ex-symmetries-exact-values}

Use reference angles to find the exact values:

1. $$\sin\frac{7\pi}{6}$$
2. $$\cos\frac{3\pi}{4}$$
3. $$\sin\frac{5\pi}{3}$$
4. $$\cos\left(-\frac{5\pi}{4}\right)$$

:::solution

By :ref{#symmetries-reference-values}:

1. $$\frac{7\pi}{6}$$ ($$210^\circ$$) is in the third quadrant, with reference angle
   $$\frac{7\pi}{6} - \pi = \frac{\pi}{6}$$. Sine is negative there, so
   $$\sin\frac{7\pi}{6} = -\frac{1}{2}$$.
2. $$\frac{3\pi}{4}$$ ($$135^\circ$$) is in the second quadrant, with reference angle
   $$\pi - \frac{3\pi}{4} = \frac{\pi}{4}$$. Cosine is negative there, so
   $$\cos\frac{3\pi}{4} = -\frac{\sqrt2}{2}$$.
3. $$\frac{5\pi}{3}$$ ($$300^\circ$$) is in the fourth quadrant, with reference angle
   $$2\pi - \frac{5\pi}{3} = \frac{\pi}{3}$$. Sine is negative there, so
   $$\sin\frac{5\pi}{3} = -\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$.
4. By :ref{#negative-angle}, $$\cos\left(-\frac{5\pi}{4}\right) = \cos\frac{5\pi}{4}$$. The angle
   $$\frac{5\pi}{4}$$ ($$225^\circ$$) is in the third quadrant, with reference angle
   $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$, and cosine is negative there. So
   $$\cos\left(-\frac{5\pi}{4}\right) = -\frac{\sqrt2}{2}$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-symmetries-two-answers}

Find every $$x$$ with $$0 \le x < 2\pi$$ and $$\sin x = -\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$. Explain why there are
no others in that range.

:::solution

Since $$\sin\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$, the reference angle is $$\frac{\pi}{3}$$, and sine
is negative in the third and fourth quadrants. By :ref{#symmetries-reference-values}, that gives

```math
x = \pi + \tfrac{\pi}{3} = \tfrac{4\pi}{3} \qquad x = 2\pi - \tfrac{\pi}{3} = \tfrac{5\pi}{3}
```

that is, $$240^\circ$$ and $$300^\circ$$, and indeed
$$\sin\frac{4\pi}{3} = \sin\frac{5\pi}{3} = -\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$.

There are no others. A point $$(a, b)$$ of the unit circle with $$b = -\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$ has
$$a^2 = 1 - \frac{3}{4} = \frac{1}{4}$$ (:ref{#coordinates/circle-equation}), so $$a = \frac{1}{2}$$
or $$a = -\frac{1}{2}$$. That makes two points, and by :ref{#unit-circle-point} each is the point
for exactly one $$x$$ with $$0 \le x < 2\pi$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-symmetries-minus-pi}

Show that $$\cos(x - \pi) = -\cos x$$ and $$\sin(x - \pi) = -\sin x$$ for every $$x$$, in two ways:

1. with :ref{#half-turn-identities}, putting $$x - \pi$$ in place of $$x$$
2. with :ref{#periodic}

Say in words which move of the point on the unit circle these identities describe.

:::solution

1. By :ref{#half-turn-identities} with $$x - \pi$$ in place of $$x$$,
   $$\cos x = \cos\big((x - \pi) + \pi\big) = -\cos(x - \pi)$$. Multiplying both sides by $$-1$$
   gives $$\cos(x - \pi) = -\cos x$$. The same steps with sine give $$\sin(x - \pi) = -\sin x$$.
2. Since $$x - \pi = (x + \pi) - 2\pi$$, :ref{#periodic} gives
   $$\cos(x - \pi) = \cos(x + \pi) = -\cos x$$, and the same for sine.

Subtracting $$\pi$$ turns the point a half turn clockwise, and that lands in the same place as a
half turn anticlockwise: straight through the origin to the opposite side.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-symmetries-three-quarters}

Prove that $$\cos\left(\frac{3\pi}{2} - x\right) = -\sin x$$ and
$$\sin\left(\frac{3\pi}{2} - x\right) = -\cos x$$ for every $$x$$. Check both with
$$x = \frac{\pi}{6}$$ ($$30^\circ$$).

:::solution

Write $$\frac{3\pi}{2} - x$$ as $$\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - x\right) + \pi$$. By
:ref{#half-turn-identities} with $$\frac{\pi}{2} - x$$ in place of $$x$$, and then
:ref{#complement-identities},

```math
\begin{aligned}
\cos\left(\tfrac{3\pi}{2} - x\right) &= -\cos\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2} - x\right) = -\sin x \\
\sin\left(\tfrac{3\pi}{2} - x\right) &= -\sin\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2} - x\right) = -\cos x
\end{aligned}
```

For $$x = \frac{\pi}{6}$$, $$\frac{3\pi}{2} - \frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{4\pi}{3}$$. From the table,
$$\cos\frac{4\pi}{3} = -\frac{1}{2} = -\sin\frac{\pi}{6}$$ and
$$\sin\frac{4\pi}{3} = -\frac{\sqrt3}{2} = -\cos\frac{\pi}{6}$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-symmetries-other-mirror}

Let $$R_2(a, b) = (-a, b)$$ be the flip in the $$y$$-axis.

1. Show that $$R_2 = G_\pi \circ R_1$$.
2. Use part 1 and :ref{#flip-reverses-turn} to show that $$R_2 \circ G_x = G_{-x} \circ R_2$$ for
   every $$x$$.
3. Apply part 2 to $$E_1$$ to give a second proof of :ref{#supplement-identities}.

:::solution

1. $$R_1$$ sends $$(a, b)$$ to $$(a, -b)$$, and the half turn $$G_\pi$$ sends that to its negative,
   $$(-a, b)$$ (part 5 of :ref{#radian-turns}). That is $$R_2(a, b)$$.
2. Turns add in either order, since $$\pi + (-x) = -x + \pi$$, so
   $$G_\pi \circ G_{-x} = G_{-x} \circ G_\pi$$ (:ref{#radian-turns}). Then, regrouping freely
   (:ref{#isometries/composite-associative}),

   ```math
   \begin{aligned}
   R_2 \circ G_x &= G_\pi \circ R_1 \circ G_x && \text{part 1} \\
   &= G_\pi \circ G_{-x} \circ R_1 && \text{a flip reverses turns} \\
   &= G_{-x} \circ G_\pi \circ R_1 && \text{turns add in either order} \\
   &= G_{-x} \circ R_2 && \text{part 1}
   \end{aligned}
   ```

3. $$R_2(E_1) = (-1, 0) = G_\pi(E_1)$$. So by part 2 and :ref{#radian-turns},

   ```math
   R_2\big(G_x(E_1)\big) = G_{-x}\big(G_\pi(E_1)\big) = G_{\pi - x}(E_1)
   ```

   The left side is $$R_2(\cos x, \sin x) = (-\cos x, \sin x)$$ and the right side is
   $$(\cos(\pi - x), \sin(\pi - x))$$. Comparing coordinates gives $$\cos(\pi - x) = -\cos x$$ and
   $$\sin(\pi - x) = \sin x$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-symmetries-slip}

A friend says that $$\sin(x + \pi) = \sin x$$, because adding $$\pi$$ flips the point over. Test the
claim with $$x = \frac{\pi}{6}$$ ($$30^\circ$$), say which move adding $$\pi$$ does make, and
correct the claim. Which angle does have the same sine as $$x$$?

:::solution

At $$x = \frac{\pi}{6}$$ the right side is $$\frac{1}{2}$$, but
$$\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{6} + \pi\right) = \sin\frac{7\pi}{6} = -\frac{1}{2}$$. The two sides differ,
so the claim is false.

Adding $$\pi$$ turns the point a half turn about the origin, which sends it straight through to the
opposite side, so its height changes sign: $$\sin(x + \pi) = -\sin x$$
(:ref{#half-turn-identities}). The move that keeps the height is the flip in the $$y$$-axis, which
sends the point for $$x$$ to the point for $$\pi - x$$. So $$\sin(\pi - x) = \sin x$$
(:ref{#supplement-identities}), and at $$x = \frac{\pi}{6}$$ that is
$$\sin\frac{5\pi}{6} = \frac{1}{2}$$.

:::
