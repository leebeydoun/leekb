description:
  Sine and cosine as the two coordinates of a point turning round a circle, and everything that
  grows from that one picture.
---

Imagine riding a Ferris wheel. As it turns, two numbers keep changing: how high your seat is above
the centre of the wheel, and how far it sits to the left or right. After a whole turn both numbers
come back to where they started, and the next turn repeats them exactly. For a wheel of radius $$1$$
those two numbers have names: the distance across is the **cosine** of the angle turned, and the
height is the **sine**. The study of these numbers and what they tell us about angles and triangles
is called **trigonometry**. In this chapter we'll build it up from that one picture.

The picture to keep in mind is the point $$(1, 0)$$ turned about the origin by an angle $$x$$. It
lands at the point $$(\cos x, \sin x)$$, so cosine is how far across and sine is how far up. Every
definition in the chapter comes back to this point, and so does almost every proof. When a formula
looks hard to remember, the point on the circle is usually the surest way to rebuild it.

Trigonometry has a reputation for long lists of identities to learn by heart. This chapter takes a
different road. Most identities turn out to be a move you can watch: flip the point in an axis, or
turn it a quarter of the way round, and read off where it lands. The few that aren't come from two
addition formulas, and a whole guide shows how to rebuild everything else from them, and how to
check any formula with a number before trusting it.

## What this chapter builds on

Angles keep the meaning they had in [Distance and angles](../distance-and-angles/introduction.mdx),
where an angle is measured by the fraction of a disc its slice takes up. Turns keep the meaning they
had in [Isometries](../isometries/introduction.mdx), with the rules there taken as given. The
chapter adds one thing taken from the picture, the rule that an arc is the same fraction of its
circle as its slice is of the disc (:ref{#arc-rule}). Everything else is proved, from those facts
and from the coordinates, steps and dot products of [Coordinates](../coordinates/introduction.mdx),
[Adding and scaling points](../adding-and-scaling-points/introduction.mdx) and
[Segments, rays and lines](../segments-rays-and-lines/introduction.mdx). Sine and cosine are
functions in the sense of [Functions](../functions/introduction.mdx), so their graphs, their
symmetries and their inverses use the ideas of that chapter.

Along the way the chapter keeps some promises that earlier chapters made:

- **Turns by any angle**: [Moves as formulas](../adding-and-scaling-points/moves-as-formulas.mdx)
  wrote quarter turns and half turns as formulas, and left the others for trigonometry. Here every
  turn gets its formula (:ref{#rotation-formula}).
- **Zooms keep angles**: [Scaling lengths](../area-and-scaling/scaling-lengths.mdx) took as given
  that a zoom keeps the size of every angle. Here it is proved (:ref{#zoom-angles}), so all three
  rules for zooms are now theorems.
- **Pinning down pi**: the table of polygons squeezing the circle in
  [The circumference](../area-and-scaling/circumference.mdx) comes from the side of a regular
  polygon, $$2r\sin\frac{\pi}{n}$$ (:ref{#polygon-side}).
- **Perpendicular slopes**: slopes of perpendicular lines multiply to $$-1$$
  (:ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/perpendicular-slopes}), and here we see why from the tangent of an
  angle a quarter turn on (:ref{#tangent-perpendicular-slopes}).

## Guides in this chapter

The chapter has thirteen guides:

1. [Radians](./radians.mdx): measuring a turn by the distance travelled round a circle of radius
   $$1$$, arcs and slices, and turns by any number
2. [Sine and cosine](./sine-and-cosine.mdx): the two coordinates of the turning point, their signs
   in each quadrant, and $$\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x = 1$$
3. [Special angles](./special-angles.mdx): exact values for $$30^\circ$$, $$45^\circ$$ and
   $$60^\circ$$ from half a square and half an equilateral triangle
4. [Sine and cosine in right triangles](./right-triangles.mdx): opposite, adjacent and hypotenuse,
   solving triangles, and finding an angle from a ratio
5. [Identities you can see](./symmetries.mdx): negative angles, half turns, quarter turns and mirror
   images, each one a move of the point on the circle
6. [The graphs of sine and cosine](./graphs.mdx): the wave a turning wheel draws, how often it
   repeats, and what stretching and squeezing do to it
7. [The tangent](./tangent.mdx): the slope of the ray, heights and distances, and the tangent's
   three cousins
8. [Addition formulas](./addition-formulas.mdx): the sine and cosine of $$a + b$$, proved by
   measuring one chord two ways, and the double and half angles
9. [Working with identities](./working-with-identities.mdx): rebuilding formulas instead of
   memorising them, proving and checking identities, and solving equations
10. [Polar coordinates and rotations](./rotations.mdx): describing a point by distance and
    direction, the formula for any turn, and matrices
11. [Angles in any triangle](./triangles.mdx): the dot product and the angle, the laws of cosines
    and sines, and why zooms keep angles
12. [Test yourself](./test-yourself.mdx): a multiple choice quiz on the ideas in the chapter
13. [Exercises](./exercises.mdx): exercises on the whole chapter, with worked solutions

**Radians and turns**

| Result                     | Statement                                                                                          |
| -------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| :ref{#radian-arc-sector}   | An angle of $$x$$ radians cuts an arc of length $$rx$$ and a sector of area $$\frac{1}{2}r^2x$$    |
| :ref{#radian-turns}        | $$G_x \circ G_y = G_{x + y}$$, $$G_{2\pi} = I$$, $$G_{\pi/2}(x, y) = (-y, x)$$ and $$G_\pi = H_O$$ |

**Sine and cosine**

| Result                                    | Statement                                                                                                                      |
| ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| :ref{#quarter-values}                     | At $$0$$, $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$, $$\pi$$ and $$\frac{3\pi}{2}$$ the point is $$E_1$$, $$E_2$$, $$-E_1$$ and $$-E_2$$               |
| :ref{#unit-circle-point}                  | Every point of the unit circle is $$(\cos x, \sin x)$$ for exactly one $$x$$ in $$[0, 2\pi)$$                                  |
| :ref{#pythagorean-identity}               | $$\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x = 1$$                                                                                                    |
| :ref{#sine-and-cosine-bounds}             | $$-1 \le \cos x \le 1$$ and $$-1 \le \sin x \le 1$$                                                                            |
| :ref{#periodic}                           | $$\cos(x + 2\pi n) = \cos x$$ and $$\sin(x + 2\pi n) = \sin x$$                                                                |
| :ref{#quadrant-signs}                     | Cosine is positive right of the $$y$$-axis, sine is positive above the $$x$$-axis                                              |
| :ref{#special-angles-angle-to-turn}       | A point of the unit circle above the $$x$$-axis at $$\theta^\circ$$ is $$(\cos x, \sin x)$$ with $$x = \frac{\pi\theta}{180}$$ |
| :ref{#sin-cos-45}                         | $$\cos\frac{\pi}{4} = \sin\frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{\sqrt2}{2}$$                                                                   |
| :ref{#sin-cos-30-60}                      | $$\cos\frac{\pi}{3} = \sin\frac{\pi}{6} = \frac12$$ and $$\sin\frac{\pi}{3} = \cos\frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$           |
| :ref{#working-with-identities-same-point} | Two angles give the same point exactly when they differ by $$2\pi n$$                                                          |

| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |

| Result                              | Statement                                                                                                                               |
| ----------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |

**Addition formulas and identities**

| Result                   | Statement                                                                                                                                   |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| :ref{#cos-difference}    | $$\cos(a - b) = \cos a\cos b + \sin a\sin b$$                                                                                               |
| :ref{#addition-formulas} | $$\cos(a + b) = \cos a\cos b - \sin a\sin b$$ and $$\sin(a + b) = \sin a\cos b + \cos a\sin b$$, with the middle sign swapped for $$a - b$$ |
| :ref{#double-angle}      | $$\sin 2x = 2\sin x\cos x$$ and $$\cos 2x = \cos^2 x - \sin^2 x = 2\cos^2 x - 1 = 1 - 2\sin^2 x$$                                           |
| :ref{#half-angle}        | $$\cos^2 x = \frac{1 + \cos 2x}{2}$$ and $$\sin^2 x = \frac{1 - \cos 2x}{2}$$                                                               |
| :ref{#tan-addition}      | $$\tan(a + b) = \frac{\tan a + \tan b}{1 - \tan a\tan b}$$                                                                                  |
| :ref{#sine-solutions}    | $$\sin x = c$$ exactly when $$x = x_0 + 2\pi n$$ or $$x = \pi - x_0 + 2\pi n$$, where $$x_0 = \sin^{-1} c$$                                 |
| :ref{#cosine-solutions}  | $$\cos x = c$$ exactly when $$x = \pm x_0 + 2\pi n$$, where $$x_0 = \cos^{-1} c$$                                                           |

| ---------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| :ref{#polar-exist}           | Every point has polar coordinates, with $$r = \lvert P \rvert$$                                |
| :ref{#rotations-polar-angle} | $$\tan^{-1}\frac{y}{x}$$ is an angle for $$(x, y)$$ when $$x > 0$$; add $$\pi$$ when $$x < 0$$ |
| :ref{#polar-turn}            | A turn by $$\varphi$$ adds $$\varphi$$ to the angle and keeps the distance                     |

| Result                | Statement                                                                                          |
