Imagine you're mapping a field with a pond in the middle, and you need the distance between two
posts on opposite banks. You can't stretch a tape across the water. But from a third post on dry
ground you can tape the distance to each of the two, and read the angle between the two directions
off a compass. Two sides and the angle between them fix the triangle (:ref{#isometries/sas}), so
they must fix the distance across the pond too.

If the angle were a right angle, the Pythagoras theorem would give that distance. For any other
angle, the cosine of the angle tells you how much to add or take away, and the rule that does it is
called the **law of cosines**. In this guide we'll find it from the dot product, and then use the
sine of an angle to find the area of a triangle and its other sides.

## The dot product measures the angle

The dot product of two steps from the origin is $$0$$ exactly when they make a right angle
(:ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/perpendicular-dot}). So the dot product notices a right angle, and it
notices every other angle too, through its cosine. Divide the dot product by the two lengths, and
what is left is the cosine of the angle between the steps.

Here is a case with numbers. Take $$A = (2, 0)$$, which is $$2$$ along the positive x-axis, and the
point $$B$$ at distance $$3$$ along the ray at angle $$\frac{\pi}{3}$$ ($$60^\circ$$). By
:ref{#circle-point-radius} and :ref{#sin-cos-30-60},

```math
B = \left(3\cos\tfrac{\pi}{3},\ 3\sin\tfrac{\pi}{3}\right) = \left(\tfrac{3}{2},\ \tfrac{3\sqrt3}{2}\right)
```

Then $$A \cdot B = 2 \cdot \frac{3}{2} + 0 \cdot \frac{3\sqrt3}{2} = 3$$. The lengths are
$$|A| = 2$$ and $$|B| = 3$$, and $$2 \cdot 3 \cdot \cos\frac{\pi}{3} = 6 \cdot \frac{1}{2} = 3$$,
the same number.

```plane
scale 1.5
point O 0 0 sw
point A 2 0 se
point B 1.5 2.598 n

segment O B color=orange label="3"
angle A O B color=green label="π/3" r=0.6
caption "The points A = (2, 0) and B at distance 3 along the ray at angle π/3: A · B = 3 = 2 · 3 · cos(π/3)"
```

:::theorem[The dot product and the angle]{#dot-angle}

Let $$A$$ and $$B$$ be points other than $$O$$, and let $$\theta = \angle AOB$$, measured in
radians, so $$0 \le \theta \le \pi$$. Then

```math
A \cdot B = |A|\,|B|\cos\theta
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Cosine_Formula_for_Dot_Product"}

**Writing the points with sine and cosine.** By :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/direction},
$$A = |A|\,U$$ and $$B = |B|\,V$$ for unit points $$U$$ and $$V$$. By :ref{#unit-circle-point},
there are numbers $$\alpha$$ and $$\beta$$, each at least $$0$$ and less than $$2\pi$$, with

```math
U = (\cos\alpha, \sin\alpha) = G_\alpha(E_1) \qquad V = (\cos\beta, \sin\beta) = G_\beta(E_1)
```

The second form of each is the definition of sine and cosine (:ref{#sine-cosine}).

**The dot product.** By parts 1 and 3 of :ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/dot-rules}, the two lengths
come out in front. Then :ref{#cos-difference}, with $$a = \beta$$ and $$b = \alpha$$, turns the
bracket into one cosine:

```math
\begin{aligned}
A \cdot B &= |A|\,|B|\,(U \cdot V) && \text{moving the numbers out} \\
&= |A|\,|B|\,(\cos\beta\cos\alpha + \sin\beta\sin\alpha) && \text{by the definition of } U \cdot V \\
&= |A|\,|B|\cos(\beta - \alpha) && \text{cosine of a difference}
\end{aligned}
```

**The turn from one ray to the other.** The number $$\beta - \alpha$$ lies between $$-2\pi$$ and
$$2\pi$$. Let $$w$$ be $$\beta - \alpha$$ if that is $$0$$ or more, and $$\beta - \alpha + 2\pi$$
otherwise, so that $$0 \le w < 2\pi$$ and $$G_w = G_{\beta - \alpha}$$ (:ref{#radian-turns}). Turns
add (:ref{#radian-turns}), so

```math
G_w(U) = G_{\beta - \alpha}\big(G_\alpha(E_1)\big) = G_\beta(E_1) = V
```

By :ref{#turn-scales}, $$G_w(A) = G_w(|A|\,U) = |A|\,V = \frac{|A|}{|B|}\,B$$. That is a positive
multiple of $$B$$, so it lies on the ray from $$O$$ through $$B$$
(:ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/zoom-on-ray}). So the turn by $$w$$ sends the ray $$OA$$ to the ray
$$OB$$, and by the definition of a turn (:ref{#isometries/rotation}), the angle turning
anticlockwise from the ray $$OA$$ to the ray $$OB$$ measures $$w$$ radians.

**Which of the two angles.** The two rays make two angles, which together fill a full turn, so the
other one measures $$2\pi - w$$. By :ref{#distance-and-angles/angle-name}, $$\angle AOB$$ is the one
that is at most a straight angle, $$\pi$$. So $$\theta = w$$ when $$w \le \pi$$, and
$$\theta = 2\pi - w$$ when $$w > \pi$$.

**The cosine.** In the second case, :ref{#periodic} and :ref{#negative-angle} give

```math
\cos\theta = \cos(2\pi - w) = \cos(-w) = \cos w
```

So in both cases $$\cos\theta = \cos w$$. Since $$w$$ and $$\beta - \alpha$$ differ by $$0$$ or
$$2\pi$$, $$\cos w = \cos(\beta - \alpha)$$ (:ref{#periodic}). So
$$A \cdot B = |A|\,|B|\cos\theta$$.

:::

Two steps of the proof come from the picture rather than from algebra. The first is that a turn by
$$w$$ makes an anticlockwise angle of $$w$$, which is how turns were defined in
[Slides and turns](../isometries/slides-and-turns.mdx), with the sizes of angles taken from sectors
of a disc. The second is that the two angles between two rays fill a full turn, because their
sectors fill the disc. The chapter keeps those foundations as they are, and everything else in the
proof is calculation.

### The angle at any corner

The theorem measures the angle at the origin. To measure the angle at a corner $$P$$, slide $$P$$ to
the origin first: the steps from $$P$$ to the other two points become points, and the slide doesn't
change the angle. Then the theorem applies to those steps.

:::corollary[The angle from the dot product]{#angle-from-dot}

Let $$P$$, $$M$$ and $$N$$ be points with $$M \ne P$$ and $$N \ne P$$. Then

```math
\cos\angle MPN = \frac{(M - P) \cdot (N - P)}{|M - P|\,|N - P|} \qquad \angle MPN = \cos^{-1}\left(\frac{(M - P) \cdot (N - P)}{|M - P|\,|N - P|}\right)
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dot_product#Geometric_definition"}

The slide $$T_{-P}$$ sends each point $$X$$ to $$X - P$$
(:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/slide-formula}), and it is an isometry
(:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/formula-isometries}). It sends $$P$$ to $$O$$, $$M$$ to $$M - P$$
and $$N$$ to $$N - P$$. Isometries keep angles (:ref{#isometries/isometry-angles}), so
$$\angle MPN$$ is the angle at $$O$$ between the rays through $$M - P$$ and $$N - P$$. Neither point
is $$O$$, so :ref{#dot-angle} gives the first formula.

For the second, write $$c$$ for the fraction. The angle $$\angle MPN$$ is between $$0$$ and $$\pi$$
and has cosine $$c$$. By :ref{#inverse-sin-cos}, $$\cos^{-1} c$$ is the only such angle, so
$$\angle MPN = \cos^{-1} c$$.

:::

:::example[The angle between (3, 1) and (1, 2)]

Take $$A = (3, 1)$$ and $$B = (1, 2)$$. Then $$A \cdot B = 3 + 2 = 5$$, $$|A| = \sqrt{10}$$ and
$$|B| = \sqrt{5}$$, so

```math
\cos\angle AOB = \frac{5}{\sqrt{10}\,\sqrt{5}} = \frac{5}{\sqrt{50}} = \frac{5}{5\sqrt2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}
```

By :ref{#sin-cos-45}, $$\cos\frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}$$, and $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$ is between
$$0$$ and $$\pi$$. So $$\angle AOB = \frac{\pi}{4}$$, which is $$45^\circ$$.

:::

The test for right angles is the case $$\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}$$. A cosine is $$0$$ at
$$\frac{\pi}{2}$$ and nowhere else between $$0$$ and $$\pi$$ (:ref{#quadrant-signs}), so for
$$A, B \ne O$$ the angle $$\angle AOB$$ is a right angle exactly when $$A \cdot B = 0$$. That is
part 2 of :ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/perpendicular-dot} again, now as one value of a general
formula.

## The law of cosines

Hinge two sticks, $$a$$ and $$b$$ long, at one end. Opened a little, their free ends are close
together, and opened wide, they are far apart. At a right angle, the gap is $$\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$$, by
the Pythagoras theorem. The law of cosines gives the gap at every angle in between: it corrects
$$a^2 + b^2$$ by an amount that depends on the cosine of the angle.

:::theorem[The law of cosines]{#law-of-cosines}

In a triangle with corners $$A$$, $$B$$ and $$C$$, let $$a = d(B, C)$$, $$b = d(C, A)$$ and
$$c = d(A, B)$$ be the sides opposite the corners, and let $$\gamma = \angle ACB$$ be the angle at
$$C$$. Then

```math
c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos\gamma
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_cosines#Using_vectors"}

Take the steps from $$C$$ to the other two corners, $$X = A - C$$ and $$Y = B - C$$. By
:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/distance-norm}, $$|X| = b$$ and $$|Y| = a$$. The two copies of $$C$$
cancel in $$X - Y = A - B$$, so $$|X - Y| = c$$. The second line below is
:ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/difference-length}, and the last is :ref{#angle-from-dot}, which says
$$X \cdot Y = |X|\,|Y|\cos\gamma$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
c^2 &= |X - Y|^2 && \text{the side } AB \\
&= |X|^2 + |Y|^2 - 2\,X \cdot Y && \text{the length of a difference} \\
&= b^2 + a^2 - 2\,X \cdot Y && \text{the sides } CA \text{ and } CB \\
&= a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos\gamma && \text{the angle from the dot product}
\end{aligned}
```

:::

When $$\gamma = \frac{\pi}{2}$$, the cosine is $$0$$ and the law says $$c^2 = a^2 + b^2$$, which is
the Pythagoras theorem (:ref{#distance-and-angles/pythagoras}). When $$\gamma$$ is wider than a
right angle, $$\cos\gamma$$ is negative (:ref{#quadrant-signs}), so $$-2ab\cos\gamma$$ adds to
$$a^2 + b^2$$ and the third side is longer than Pythagoras would give. When $$\gamma$$ is narrower
than a right angle, the third side is shorter.

:::example[Across the pond]

From the post $$C$$, the post $$A$$ is $$80$$ m away and the post $$B$$ is $$50$$ m away, with an
angle of $$\frac{\pi}{3}$$ ($$60^\circ$$) between the two directions. By :ref{#law-of-cosines} and
:ref{#sin-cos-30-60},

```math
c^2 = 80^2 + 50^2 - 2 \cdot 80 \cdot 50 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} = 6400 + 2500 - 4000 = 4900
```

So the posts are $$\sqrt{4900} = 70$$ m apart, across the water. A right angle at $$C$$ would have
put them $$\sqrt{8900} \approx 94.3$$ m apart.

:::

The law also runs backwards. If you know all three sides, solve for the cosine of an angle:

```math
\cos\gamma = \frac{a^2 + b^2 - c^2}{2ab}
```

For the triangle with sides $$3$$, $$5$$ and $$7$$, the angle facing the side $$7$$ has cosine
$$\frac{9 + 25 - 49}{2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5} = \frac{-15}{30} = -\frac{1}{2}$$. By
:ref{#supplement-identities}, $$\cos\frac{2\pi}{3} = -\cos\frac{\pi}{3} = -\frac{1}{2}$$, and
$$\frac{2\pi}{3}$$ is between $$0$$ and $$\pi$$. So the angle is $$\frac{2\pi}{3}$$, which is
$$120^\circ$$ (:ref{#inverse-sin-cos}).

## Area from two sides and the angle

Lean a ladder of length $$a$$ against a wall at an angle $$\gamma$$ to the ground, and its top is
$$a\sin\gamma$$ up (:ref{#right-triangle-ratios}). A triangle's height works the same way. Stand the
triangle on its side $$CA$$, and the side $$CB$$ is the ladder: the corner $$B$$ is $$a\sin\gamma$$
above the line $$CA$$. When $$\gamma$$ is wider than a right angle, the ladder leans back past
$$C$$, but the height is $$a\sin(\pi - \gamma)$$, which is still $$a\sin\gamma$$
(:ref{#supplement-identities}).

:::theorem[Area from two sides and the angle]{#area-sine}

In a triangle with corners $$A$$, $$B$$ and $$C$$, with $$a = d(B, C)$$, $$b = d(C, A)$$ and
$$\gamma = \angle ACB$$,

```math
\text{area} = \tfrac{1}{2}ab\sin\gamma
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Area_of_Triangle_in_Terms_of_Two_Sides_and_Angle"}

Take the side $$CA$$ as the base, of length $$b$$. The proof works out the height $$h$$, the
distance from $$B$$ to the line $$CA$$ (:ref{#distance-and-angles/height}), with coordinates, so
that wide and narrow angles are handled together.

**The foot of the height.** The line $$CA$$ is the line $$C + t(A - C)$$
(:ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/two-point-line}). Let $$F$$ be its closest point to $$B$$, given by
:ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/closest-point}:

```math
F = C + t_0(A - C) \qquad t_0 = \frac{(B - C) \cdot (A - C)}{|A - C|^2}
```

$$F$$ is the foot of the perpendicular from $$B$$, so $$h = |B - F|$$, as the paragraph after
:ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/closest-point} explains.

**The distance from C to the foot.** By :ref{#angle-from-dot},
$$(B - C) \cdot (A - C) = ab\cos\gamma$$, and $$|A - C| = b$$. So by
:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/norm-scale},

```math
|F - C| = |t_0|\,|A - C| = \frac{|ab\cos\gamma|}{b^2}\,b = a\,|\cos\gamma|
```

**The height.** :ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/closest-point} with $$Y = C$$ gives
$$|B - C|^2 = |B - F|^2 + |F - C|^2$$. So, using :ref{#pythagorean-identity} in the last line,

```math
\begin{aligned}
h^2 &= |B - C|^2 - |F - C|^2 && \text{taking } |F - C|^2 \text{ from both sides} \\
&= a^2 - a^2\cos^2\gamma && \text{the side } CB \text{ and the step above} \\
&= a^2(1 - \cos^2\gamma) && \text{taking out } a^2 \\
&= a^2\sin^2\gamma && \text{since } \cos^2\gamma + \sin^2\gamma = 1
\end{aligned}
```

The corners don't lie on one line, so $$\gamma$$ is neither the zero angle nor a straight angle, and
$$0 < \gamma < \pi$$. Then $$\sin\gamma > 0$$, by :ref{#quadrant-signs} and :ref{#quarter-values}.
Since $$h \ge 0$$ too, $$h = a\sin\gamma$$. By :ref{#distance-and-angles/triangle-area}, the area is
$$\frac{1}{2}bh = \frac{1}{2}ab\sin\gamma$$.

:::


As a check, a right triangle with legs $$3$$ and $$4$$ has $$\gamma = \frac{\pi}{2}$$, and the
formula gives $$\frac{1}{2} \cdot 3 \cdot 4 \cdot 1 = 6$$, which is half the $$3$$ by $$4$$
rectangle. For an equilateral triangle with sides $$2$$, every angle is $$\frac{\pi}{3}$$, and the
area is $$\frac{1}{2} \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot \frac{\sqrt3}{2} = \sqrt3$$. For the posts across the
pond, the triangle has area $$\frac{1}{2} \cdot 80 \cdot 50 \cdot \frac{\sqrt3}{2} \approx 1732$$
square metres.

## The law of sines

The area formula $$\frac{1}{2}ab\sin\gamma$$ works at any corner of a triangle, with the two sides
that meet there. All three versions give the same area, and setting them equal ties each side to the
angle facing it. The result is the law of sines: each side divided by the sine of the angle facing
it gives the same number.

:::theorem[The law of sines]{#law-of-sines}

In a triangle with corners $$A$$, $$B$$ and $$C$$, let $$a$$, $$b$$ and $$c$$ be the sides opposite
them, and $$\alpha$$, $$\beta$$ and $$\gamma$$ the angles at them. Then

```math
\frac{a}{\sin\alpha} = \frac{b}{\sin\beta} = \frac{c}{\sin\gamma}
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Law_of_Sines"}

By :ref{#area-sine} at each corner in turn, with the two sides that meet there, the area of the
triangle is

```math
\tfrac{1}{2}ab\sin\gamma = \tfrac{1}{2}bc\sin\alpha = \tfrac{1}{2}ca\sin\beta
```

Divide all three by $$\frac{1}{2}abc$$, which is positive:

```math
\frac{\sin\gamma}{c} = \frac{\sin\alpha}{a} = \frac{\sin\beta}{b}
```

Each angle is between $$0$$ and $$\pi$$, so each sine is positive, as in the proof of
:ref{#area-sine}. Turning all three fractions upside down gives the law.

:::

As a check, take the half equilateral triangle of [Special angles](./special-angles.mdx), with sides
$$1$$, $$\sqrt3$$ and $$2$$ facing the angles $$\frac{\pi}{6}$$, $$\frac{\pi}{3}$$ and
$$\frac{\pi}{2}$$. The three fractions are $$\frac{1}{1/2} = 2$$, $$\frac{\sqrt3}{\sqrt3/2} = 2$$
and $$\frac{2}{1} = 2$$.

:::example[A tree across a river]

You stand at $$P$$ on a straight riverbank, and a friend stands at $$Q$$, $$40$$ m further along. A
tree $$T$$ grows on the far bank. You measure the angle at $$P$$ between the bank and the direction
of the tree as $$70^\circ$$, and your friend measures $$60^\circ$$ at $$Q$$. The angles of a
triangle add up to a straight angle (:ref{#distance-and-angles/triangle-angle-sum}), so the angle at
$$T$$ is $$180^\circ - 70^\circ - 60^\circ = 50^\circ$$.

The side $$PT$$ faces the angle at $$Q$$, and the side $$PQ$$ faces the angle at $$T$$. By
:ref{#law-of-sines},

```math
\frac{d(P, T)}{\sin 60^\circ} = \frac{40}{\sin 50^\circ} \qquad\text{so}\qquad d(P, T) = \frac{40 \sin 60^\circ}{\sin 50^\circ} \approx \frac{40 \cdot 0.866}{0.766} \approx 45.2 \text{ m}
```

The width of the river is the height of $$T$$ above the bank. The side $$PT$$ leans at $$70^\circ$$
to the bank, so the width is about $$45.2 \sin 70^\circ \approx 42.5$$ m. Working from $$Q$$ instead
gives $$d(Q, T) = \frac{40 \sin 70^\circ}{\sin 50^\circ} \approx 49.1$$ m and the same width,
$$49.1 \sin 60^\circ \approx 42.5$$ m.

:::

### Two sides and an angle that isn't between them

Two sides and the angle between them fix a triangle. Two sides and an angle that is not between them
may not. The law of sines gives the sine of the missing angle, and two different angles from $$0$$
to $$\pi$$ share each sine between $$0$$ and $$1$$ (:ref{#same-sine}). For example, take an angle of
$$\frac{\pi}{6}$$ ($$30^\circ$$) at $$A$$, with $$d(A, B) = 10$$ and $$d(B, C) = 6$$, and $$C$$ on a
fixed ray from $$A$$. By :ref{#law-of-sines},

```math
\sin\gamma = \frac{10 \sin(\pi/6)}{6} = \frac{10 \cdot \frac{1}{2}}{6} = \frac{5}{6}
```

Both $$\gamma \approx 0.985$$ ($$56.4^\circ$$) and $$\gamma \approx \pi - 0.985 \approx 2.156$$
($$123.6^\circ$$) have that sine, and either one leaves room for the angle at $$A$$. So there are
two triangles. Swing a side of length $$6$$ about $$B$$, and it meets the ray from $$A$$ at two
points, $$C_1$$ and $$C_2$$.

## Zooms keep angles

Zoom in on a photo and a square corner stays square. Every other corner keeps its size too. That was
rule 3 of :ref{#area-and-scaling/zoom-rules}, taken as given there. With :ref{#angle-from-dot} it
can be proved. A zoom by $$r$$ multiplies every step by $$r$$, so it multiplies the dot product by
$$r^2$$ and each length by $$r$$, and those factors cancel in the cosine.

Here are numbers first. The zoom by $$2$$ about $$Q = (1, 0)$$ sends $$X$$ to $$Q + 2(X - Q)$$
(:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/zoom-formula}). It sends $$P = (1, 1)$$, $$M = (3, 1)$$ and
$$N = (2, 2)$$ to $$P' = (1, 2)$$, $$M' = (5, 2)$$ and $$N' = (3, 4)$$. Before the zoom, the steps
from $$P$$ are $$(2, 0)$$ and $$(1, 1)$$, with dot product $$2$$ and lengths $$2$$ and $$\sqrt2$$.
After it, the steps from $$P'$$ are $$(4, 0)$$ and $$(2, 2)$$, with dot product $$8$$ and lengths
$$4$$ and $$2\sqrt2$$. Both times the cosine is $$\frac{1}{\sqrt2}$$, since
$$\frac{2}{2\sqrt2} = \frac{8}{8\sqrt2}$$.

```plane
scale 1.4
point Q 1 0 s
point P 1 1 w
point M 3 1 s
point N 2 2 n
point P' 1 2 w
point M' 5 2 se
point N' 3 4 n
ray P M color=blue extend=0.3
ray P N color=blue extend=0.3
ray P' M' color=orange extend=0.3
ray P' N' color=orange extend=0.3
angle M P N color=blue r=0.5
angle M' P' N' color=orange r=0.9
caption "The zoom by 2 about Q = (1, 0) sends the angle at P to the angle at P', and both measure π/4"
```

:::theorem[Zooms keep angles]{#zoom-angles}

Let $$Q$$ be a point and $$r > 0$$, and write $$X'$$ for $$Z_{r,Q}(X)$$. Let $$P$$, $$M$$ and $$N$$
be points with $$M \ne P$$ and $$N \ne P$$. Then the zoom sends the rays $$PM$$ and $$PN$$ to the
rays $$P'M'$$ and $$P'N'$$, and

```math
\angle M'P'N' = \angle MPN
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homothety#Properties"}

**Steps are multiplied by r.** By :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/zoom-formula}, the two copies of
$$Q$$ cancel:

```math
M' - P' = \big(Q + r(M - Q)\big) - \big(Q + r(P - Q)\big) = r(M - Q) - r(P - Q) = r(M - P)
```

In the same way $$N' - P' = r(N - P)$$. Since $$r > 0$$ and $$M - P \ne O$$, these steps are not
$$O$$, so $$M' \ne P'$$ and $$N' \ne P'$$.

**Arms go to arms.** The ray $$PM$$ is made of the points $$P + t(M - P)$$ with $$t \ge 0$$
(:ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/ray-formula}). The zoom sends such a point to

```math
Q + r\big(P + t(M - P) - Q\big) = Q + r(P - Q) + t\,r(M - P) = P' + t(M' - P')
```

with the same $$t$$. So it sends each point of the ray $$PM$$ to a point of the ray $$P'M'$$, and
each point of the ray $$P'M'$$ comes from one of the ray $$PM$$. The same holds for the rays $$PN$$
and $$P'N'$$.

**The angle.** By :ref{#angle-from-dot}, then parts 1 and 3 of
:ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/dot-rules} and :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/norm-scale},

```math
\cos\angle M'P'N' = \frac{r(M - P) \cdot r(N - P)}{|r(M - P)|\,|r(N - P)|} = \frac{r^2\,(M - P) \cdot (N - P)}{r^2\,|M - P|\,|N - P|} = \cos\angle MPN
```

Both angles are between $$0$$ and $$\pi$$ (:ref{#distance-and-angles/angle-name}), and by
:ref{#inverse-sin-cos} only one angle in that range has a given cosine. So
$$\angle M'P'N' = \angle MPN$$.

:::

The reflex angle at $$P$$ is what is left of a full turn, so the zoom keeps it too. This proves rule
3 of :ref{#area-and-scaling/zoom-rules}, for zooms about any centre. The other two rules are proved
already, by :ref{#coordinates/origin-zoom} and :ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/moves-keep-lines}, so
none of the rules for zooms is still taken as given.

It also settles the angles of similar shapes. A shape similar to another is a zoom of it followed by
an isometry (:ref{#area-and-scaling/similar}), and both keep angles (:ref{#zoom-angles},
:ref{#isometries/isometry-angles}). So two similar triangles have the same three angles, matched up
corner by corner.

::::exercise{#ex-triangles-angle-between}

Find the angle $$\angle AOB$$ for each pair of points, in radians and in degrees.

1. $$A = (2, 0)$$ and $$B = (1, \sqrt3)$$
2. $$A = (3, 3)$$ and $$B = (-4, 0)$$

:::solution

By :ref{#angle-from-dot}, the cosine of the angle is the dot product divided by the two lengths.

1. $$A \cdot B = 2$$, $$|A| = 2$$ and $$|B| = \sqrt{1 + 3} = 2$$, so
   $$\cos\angle AOB = \frac{2}{4} = \frac{1}{2}$$. By :ref{#sin-cos-30-60},
   $$\cos\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{1}{2}$$, so $$\angle AOB = \frac{\pi}{3}$$, which is $$60^\circ$$.
2. $$A \cdot B = -12$$, $$|A| = \sqrt{18} = 3\sqrt2$$ and $$|B| = 4$$, so
   $$\cos\angle AOB = \frac{-12}{12\sqrt2} = -\frac{1}{\sqrt2}$$. By :ref{#supplement-identities}
   and :ref{#sin-cos-45}, $$\cos\frac{3\pi}{4} = -\cos\frac{\pi}{4} = -\frac{1}{\sqrt2}$$, so
   $$\angle AOB = \frac{3\pi}{4}$$, which is $$135^\circ$$.

In both parts the angle is between $$0$$ and $$\pi$$, so it is the one :ref{#inverse-sin-cos} picks.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-triangles-third-side}

Two sides of a triangle are $$5$$ cm and $$8$$ cm, with an angle of $$\frac{\pi}{3}$$ ($$60^\circ$$)
between them. Find the third side and the area.

:::solution

By :ref{#law-of-cosines}, with $$\cos\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{1}{2}$$,

```math
c^2 = 25 + 64 - 2 \cdot 5 \cdot 8 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} = 89 - 40 = 49
```

so the third side is $$7$$ cm. By :ref{#area-sine}, with $$\sin\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$,
the area is $$\frac{1}{2} \cdot 5 \cdot 8 \cdot \frac{\sqrt3}{2} = 10\sqrt3 \approx 17.3$$ square
cm.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-triangles-buoy}

Two friends stand $$200$$ m apart on a straight beach, at $$A$$ and $$B$$, and both look at a buoy
$$C$$ out at sea. The angle at $$A$$ between the beach and the buoy is $$40^\circ$$, and at $$B$$ it
is $$75^\circ$$. How far is the buoy from each friend?

:::solution

The angles add up to $$180^\circ$$ (:ref{#distance-and-angles/triangle-angle-sum}), so the angle at
$$C$$ is $$180^\circ - 40^\circ - 75^\circ = 65^\circ$$, facing the side $$AB = 200$$. By
:ref{#law-of-sines}, each side is $$\frac{200}{\sin 65^\circ}$$ times the sine of the angle facing
it. The side $$AC$$ faces the angle at $$B$$, and $$BC$$ faces the angle at $$A$$:

```math
d(A, C) = \frac{200 \sin 75^\circ}{\sin 65^\circ} \approx \frac{200 \cdot 0.966}{0.906} \approx 213.2 \text{ m} \qquad d(B, C) = \frac{200 \sin 40^\circ}{\sin 65^\circ} \approx \frac{200 \cdot 0.643}{0.906} \approx 141.8 \text{ m}
```

So the friend at $$B$$ is nearer to the buoy.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-triangles-how-many}

A triangle has an angle of $$40^\circ$$ at $$A$$ and $$d(A, B) = 9$$. For each length of the side
$$BC$$ below, say how many triangles there are, and find the angle at $$C$$ in each.

1. $$d(B, C) = 7$$
2. $$d(B, C) = 5$$
3. $$d(B, C) = 10$$

:::solution

By :ref{#law-of-sines}, $$\frac{\sin\gamma}{9} = \frac{\sin 40^\circ}{d(B, C)}$$, so
$$\sin\gamma = \frac{9\sin 40^\circ}{d(B, C)}$$, with $$\sin 40^\circ \approx 0.643$$. An angle
between $$0^\circ$$ and $$180^\circ$$ with a given sine below $$1$$ is one of two, $$\gamma$$ or
$$180^\circ - \gamma$$ (:ref{#same-sine}). Each one gives a triangle exactly when it leaves room for
the $$40^\circ$$ at $$A$$, that is, when it is less than $$140^\circ$$.

1. $$\sin\gamma \approx 0.826$$, so $$\gamma \approx 55.7^\circ$$ or $$\gamma \approx 124.3^\circ$$.
   Both are less than $$140^\circ$$, so there are two triangles.
2. $$\sin\gamma \approx 1.157$$, but no sine is more than $$1$$ (:ref{#sine-and-cosine-bounds}). So
   there is no triangle: the side $$BC$$ is too short to reach the ray from $$A$$.
3. $$\sin\gamma \approx 0.579$$, so $$\gamma \approx 35.3^\circ$$ or $$\gamma \approx 144.7^\circ$$.
   Only the first is less than $$140^\circ$$, so there is one triangle.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-triangles-obtuse-test}

In a triangle with sides $$a$$, $$b$$ and $$c$$, and the angle $$\gamma$$ facing $$c$$, show that
$$\gamma$$ is wider than a right angle exactly when $$c^2 > a^2 + b^2$$. Then decide whether the
triangle with sides $$4$$, $$5$$ and $$6$$ has an angle wider than a right angle.

:::solution

By :ref{#law-of-cosines}, $$c^2 - (a^2 + b^2) = -2ab\cos\gamma$$. Since $$2ab > 0$$, this is
positive exactly when $$\cos\gamma < 0$$. The angle is between $$0$$ and $$\pi$$, and there
$$\cos\gamma$$ is positive for $$\gamma < \frac{\pi}{2}$$, $$0$$ at $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$ and negative
for $$\gamma > \frac{\pi}{2}$$ (:ref{#quadrant-signs}, :ref{#quarter-values}). So
$$c^2 > a^2 + b^2$$ exactly when $$\gamma > \frac{\pi}{2}$$.

Test each angle with the side it faces. For the side $$6$$, $$36 < 16 + 25 = 41$$. For the side
$$5$$, $$25 < 16 + 36$$, and for the side $$4$$, $$16 < 25 + 36$$. So no angle is wider than a right
angle.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-triangles-four-five-six}

In the triangle with sides $$4$$, $$5$$ and $$6$$, find the cosines of the angles facing the sides
$$4$$ and $$6$$. Then use :ref{#double-angle} to show that the angle facing $$6$$ is exactly twice
the angle facing $$4$$.

:::solution

Let $$x$$ be the angle facing the side $$4$$ and $$y$$ the angle facing the side $$6$$. By
:ref{#law-of-cosines}, solved for the cosine,

```math
\cos x = \frac{25 + 36 - 16}{2 \cdot 5 \cdot 6} = \frac{45}{60} = \frac{3}{4} \qquad \cos y = \frac{16 + 25 - 36}{2 \cdot 4 \cdot 5} = \frac{5}{40} = \frac{1}{8}
```

By :ref{#double-angle},

```math
\cos 2x = 2\cos^2 x - 1 = 2 \cdot \tfrac{9}{16} - 1 = \tfrac{18}{16} - \tfrac{16}{16} = \tfrac{1}{8}
```

Since $$\cos x > 0$$, the angle $$x$$ is less than $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$ (:ref{#quadrant-signs}), so
$$2x$$ is between $$0$$ and $$\pi$$. So $$2x$$ and $$y$$ are both between $$0$$ and $$\pi$$ with
cosine $$\frac{1}{8}$$, and by :ref{#inverse-sin-cos} they are equal. In degrees,
$$x \approx 41.4^\circ$$ and $$y \approx 82.8^\circ$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-triangles-projection}

In a triangle with sides $$a$$, $$b$$ and $$c$$ facing the angles $$\alpha$$, $$\beta$$ and
$$\gamma$$, show that

```math
a = b\cos\gamma + c\cos\beta
```

Check it with the triangle with sides $$a = 7$$, $$b = 3$$ and $$c = 5$$.

:::solution

The angle $$\gamma$$ sits between the sides $$a$$ and $$b$$, and $$\beta$$ between $$a$$ and $$c$$.
By :ref{#law-of-cosines}, solved for each cosine,

```math
b\cos\gamma = b \cdot \frac{a^2 + b^2 - c^2}{2ab} = \frac{a^2 + b^2 - c^2}{2a} \qquad c\cos\beta = c \cdot \frac{a^2 + c^2 - b^2}{2ac} = \frac{a^2 + c^2 - b^2}{2a}
```

Adding, the $$b^2$$ and $$c^2$$ cancel, and

```math
b\cos\gamma + c\cos\beta = \frac{2a^2}{2a} = a
```

For the check, $$\cos\gamma = \frac{49 + 9 - 25}{42} = \frac{33}{42}$$ and
$$\cos\beta = \frac{49 + 25 - 9}{70} = \frac{65}{70}$$. Then
$$3 \cdot \frac{33}{42} + 5 \cdot \frac{65}{70} = \frac{33}{14} + \frac{65}{14} = \frac{98}{14} = 7$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-triangles-zoom}

The triangle with corners $$P = (0, 0)$$, $$M = (4, 0)$$ and $$N = (1, 3)$$ is zoomed by $$3$$ about
$$Q = (2, 1)$$. Find the corners $$P'$$, $$M'$$ and $$N'$$ of the image, and check with
:ref{#angle-from-dot} that the angle at $$P'$$ has the same cosine as the angle at $$P$$.

:::solution

By :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/zoom-formula}, each point $$X$$ goes to $$Q + 3(X - Q)$$:

```math
P' = (2, 1) + 3(-2, -1) = (-4, -2) \qquad M' = (2, 1) + 3(2, -1) = (8, -2) \qquad N' = (2, 1) + 3(-1, 2) = (-1, 7)
```

At $$P$$, the steps are $$(4, 0)$$ and $$(1, 3)$$, with dot product $$4$$ and lengths $$4$$ and
$$\sqrt{10}$$, so the cosine is $$\frac{4}{4\sqrt{10}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}$$. At $$P'$$, the steps
are $$(12, 0)$$ and $$(3, 9)$$, three times as long, with dot product $$36$$ and lengths $$12$$ and
$$\sqrt{90} = 3\sqrt{10}$$. The cosine is $$\frac{36}{36\sqrt{10}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}$$, the
same, as :ref{#zoom-angles} says. The angle is about $$1.25$$ radians, or $$71.6^\circ$$.

:::
