  The cosine and sine of a sum or difference of two angles, proved from one chord of the unit

Imagine the dial on an oven with a white dot painted on its rim. Turn the dial part of the way round
and the dot moves to a new place; turn it again and it moves on. The two turns together are one turn
by the two angles added up. So if you know where the dot sits after each turn on its own, how far
across and how far up, there should be a way to work out where it sits after both.

There is, and the rules that do it are called the **addition formulas**. They give the cosine and
sine of $$a + b$$ from the cosines and sines of $$a$$ and $$b$$. In this guide we'll prove them from
one picture, a chord of the unit circle and a turned copy of it, and then use them to find exact
values we couldn't reach before.

## Sines don't add

The first thing to try is adding. If the dot rises by $$\sin a$$ in the first turn, it seems that it
should rise by another $$\sin b$$ in the second. But it doesn't, and it is worth seeing that with
numbers before anything else.

Take $$a = b = \frac{\pi}{4}$$. Then $$a + b = \frac{\pi}{2}$$, a quarter turn, which takes
$$E_1 = (1, 0)$$ to $$E_2 = (0, 1)$$. So $$\sin(a + b) = 1$$ (:ref{#quarter-values}). Adding the
sines instead gives

```math
\sin\frac{\pi}{4} + \sin\frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{\sqrt2}{2} + \frac{\sqrt2}{2} = \sqrt2 \approx 1.41
```

by :ref{#sin-cos-45}, which is more than $$1$$. No point of the unit circle is that high.

So $$\sin(a + b)$$ is not $$\sin a + \sin b$$, and $$\cos(a + b)$$ is not $$\cos a + \cos b$$
either. It's a common slip, so this example is worth keeping in mind. The reason is in the picture:
the second turn starts from a tilted position, so the height it adds depends on where the dot
already is.

## The chord and its turned copy

Mark two points on the unit circle, and join them with a segment. A segment like that, between two
points of a circle, is called a **chord**. Its length depends only on how far apart the two points
are round the circle. Turn both points about $$O$$ by the same angle, and the chord turns with them
without changing length, because a turn keeps distances.

So turn the pair until one of the points lands on $$E_1$$. Let $$P$$ be the point on the unit circle
at angle $$a$$, and $$Q$$ the point at angle $$b$$. Turning both by $$-b$$ takes $$Q$$ to $$E_1$$,
at angle $$0$$, and takes $$P$$ to the point at angle $$a - b$$. Call that point $$R$$.

```plane
scale 3.2
point O 0 0 sw
point E₁ 1 0 e
point P -0.9397 0.3420 w
point Q -0.1736 0.9848 n
point R 0.5 0.8660 ne
circle O 1 color=muted
segment O P color=muted dashed
segment O Q color=muted dashed
segment O R color=muted dashed
segment O E₁ color=muted dashed
segment P Q color=blue
segment E₁ R color=orange
angle Q O P color=blue r=0.35 label="a − b"
angle E₁ O R color=orange r=0.3 label="a − b"
caption "P is at angle a and Q at angle b. Turning both by −b takes Q to E₁ and P to R, at angle a − b. The blue chord PQ and the orange chord E₁R have the same length."
```

The two chords have the same length, and the distance formula gives each length in terms of cosines
and sines. The blue chord brings in $$\cos a$$, $$\sin a$$, $$\cos b$$ and $$\sin b$$; the orange
chord brings in $$\cos(a - b)$$ and $$\sin(a - b)$$. Setting the two lengths equal is the whole
proof.

Here is the calculation once with numbers. Take $$a = \frac{\pi}{2}$$ and $$b = \frac{\pi}{6}$$, so
$$P = (0, 1)$$ and $$Q = \left(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}, \frac12\right)$$ (:ref{#sin-cos-30-60}). The blue
chord has squared length

```math
\left(0 - \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)^2 + \left(1 - \frac12\right)^2 = \frac34 + \frac14 = 1
```

The difference is $$a - b = \frac{\pi}{3}$$, so $$R = \left(\frac12, \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)$$, and
the orange chord has squared length

```math
\left(\frac12 - 1\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\sqrt3}{2} - 0\right)^2 = \frac14 + \frac34 = 1
```

The two agree, as they must. Move $$a$$ and $$b$$ below, and press the button to watch the blue
chord turn down onto the orange one.

## The cosine of a difference

The picture turns into a formula once both chord lengths are written out. Squaring each length gets
rid of the square roots, and $$\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x = 1$$ tidies what is left.

:::theorem[Cosine of a difference]{#cos-difference}

For all numbers $$a$$ and $$b$$,

```math
\cos(a - b) = \cos a\cos b + \sin a\sin b
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Cosine_of_Difference"}

**Step 1: name the points.** By :ref{#sine-cosine}, the three points

```math
\begin{aligned}
P &= G_a(E_1) = (\cos a, \sin a) \\
Q &= G_b(E_1) = (\cos b, \sin b) \\
R &= G_{a - b}(E_1) = \big(\cos(a - b), \sin(a - b)\big)
\end{aligned}
```

lie on the unit circle.

**Step 2: the turn by −b sends Q to E₁.** By :ref{#radian-turns}, turning by $$b$$ and then by
$$-b$$ is turning by $$b + (-b) = 0$$, and $$G_0 = G_{2\pi} = I$$ moves nothing. So

```math
G_{-b}(Q) = G_{-b}\big(G_b(E_1)\big) = G_0(E_1) = E_1
```

**Step 3: the same turn sends P to R.** Again by :ref{#radian-turns}, turning by $$a$$ and then by
$$-b$$ is turning by $$a - b$$. So

```math
G_{-b}(P) = G_{-b}\big(G_a(E_1)\big) = G_{a - b}(E_1) = R
```

**Step 4: the two chords have the same length.** A turn keeps distances
(:ref{#isometries/basic-isometries}). The turn $$G_{-b}$$ sends $$P$$ to $$R$$ and $$Q$$ to $$E_1$$,
so $$d(P, Q) = d(R, E_1)$$, and the squares of the two distances are equal too.

**Step 5: the blue chord.** By :ref{#coordinates/distance-formula},

```math
\begin{aligned}
d(P, Q)^2 &= (\cos a - \cos b)^2 + (\sin a - \sin b)^2 &&\qquad \text{distance formula} \\
&= \cos^2 a - 2\cos a\cos b + \cos^2 b + \sin^2 a - 2\sin a\sin b + \sin^2 b &&\qquad \text{multiply out each square} \\
&= (\cos^2 a + \sin^2 a) + (\cos^2 b + \sin^2 b) - 2(\cos a\cos b + \sin a\sin b) &&\qquad \text{regroup the terms} \\
&= 2 - 2(\cos a\cos b + \sin a\sin b) &&\qquad \text{each bracket is } 1
\end{aligned}
```

where the last line uses :ref{#pythagorean-identity} twice, once for $$a$$ and once for $$b$$.

**Step 6: the orange chord.** Write $$x = a - b$$ for short, so $$R = (\cos x, \sin x)$$ and
$$E_1 = (1, 0)$$. In the same way,

```math
\begin{aligned}
d(R, E_1)^2 &= (\cos x - 1)^2 + (\sin x - 0)^2 &&\qquad \text{distance formula} \\
&= \cos^2 x - 2\cos x + 1 + \sin^2 x &&\qquad \text{multiply out the square} \\
&= (\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x) + 1 - 2\cos x &&\qquad \text{regroup the terms} \\
&= 2 - 2\cos x &&\qquad \text{the bracket is } 1
\end{aligned}
```

**Step 7: set them equal.** By step 4 the two squared lengths are equal, so

```math
\begin{aligned}
2 - 2\cos(a - b) &= 2 - 2(\cos a\cos b + \sin a\sin b) &&\qquad \text{steps 5 and 6} \\
-2\cos(a - b) &= -2(\cos a\cos b + \sin a\sin b) &&\qquad \text{take 2 from both sides} \\
\cos(a - b) &= \cos a\cos b + \sin a\sin b &&\qquad \text{divide both sides by } -2
\end{aligned}
```

:::

Nothing in the proof needs $$a$$ to be bigger than $$b$$, or either of them to be positive. Turns
are defined for every number, and the rules for adding turns hold for all of them, so the formula
holds for every pair of numbers. The picture shows why the proof works; the steps themselves use
only the rules for turns and the distance formula.

Check it with $$a = \frac{\pi}{2}$$ and $$b = \frac{\pi}{3}$$. The left side is
$$\cos\frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$. The right side is

```math
\cos\frac{\pi}{2}\cos\frac{\pi}{3} + \sin\frac{\pi}{2}\sin\frac{\pi}{3} = 0 \cdot \frac12 + 1 \cdot \frac{\sqrt3}{2} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}
```

using :ref{#quarter-values} and :ref{#sin-cos-30-60}. Putting $$b = a$$ is a check of a different
kind: the left side is $$\cos 0 = 1$$, and the right side is $$\cos^2 a + \sin^2 a$$, so the theorem
contains :ref{#pythagorean-identity}.

The right side of the theorem is the dot product $$P \cdot Q$$ of the two points
(:ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/dot-product}). So the theorem says that the dot product of two points
on the unit circle is the cosine of the angle between them. The guide
[Angles in any triangle](./triangles.mdx) builds on that in :ref{#dot-angle}.

## Four formulas from one

Once the cosine of a difference is known, the other three formulas take one or two lines each. Two
moves from earlier guides do the work. Changing $$b$$ to $$-b$$ turns a difference into a sum, and
:ref{#negative-angle} says what that does to cosine and sine. Swapping $$x$$ for
$$\frac{\pi}{2} - x$$ swaps sine and cosine (:ref{#complement-identities}), which turns a formula
for cosine into one for sine.

:::theorem[The addition formulas]{#addition-formulas}

For all numbers $$a$$ and $$b$$,

```math
\begin{aligned}
\cos(a + b) &= \cos a\cos b - \sin a\sin b \\
\cos(a - b) &= \cos a\cos b + \sin a\sin b \\
\sin(a + b) &= \sin a\cos b + \cos a\sin b \\
\sin(a - b) &= \sin a\cos b - \cos a\sin b
\end{aligned}
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proofs_of_trigonometric_identities#Angle_sum_identities"}

**The cosine of a difference** is :ref{#cos-difference}.

**The cosine of a sum.** Write $$a + b$$ as $$a - (-b)$$, and use :ref{#cos-difference} with $$-b$$
in place of $$b$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
\cos(a + b) &= \cos\big(a - (-b)\big) &&\qquad a + b = a - (-b) \\
&= \cos a\cos(-b) + \sin a\sin(-b) &&\qquad \text{cosine of a difference} \\
&= \cos a\cos b - \sin a\sin b &&\qquad \cos(-b) = \cos b,\ \sin(-b) = -\sin b
\end{aligned}
```

where the last step is :ref{#negative-angle}.

**The sine of a sum.** By :ref{#complement-identities},
$$\sin x = \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - x\right)$$ for every $$x$$. Take $$x = a + b$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
\sin(a + b) &= \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - (a + b)\right) &&\qquad \text{complement identity} \\
&= \cos\left(\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - a\right) - b\right) &&\qquad \text{regroup inside the bracket} \\
&= \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - a\right)\cos b + \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - a\right)\sin b &&\qquad \text{cosine of a difference} \\
&= \sin a\cos b + \cos a\sin b &&\qquad \text{complement identities}
\end{aligned}
```

where the last step uses both parts of :ref{#complement-identities}:
$$\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - a\right) = \sin a$$ and $$\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - a\right) = \cos a$$.

**The sine of a difference.** Use the sine of a sum with $$-b$$ in place of $$b$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
\sin(a - b) &= \sin a\cos(-b) + \cos a\sin(-b) &&\qquad \text{sine of a sum} \\
&= \sin a\cos b - \cos a\sin b &&\qquad \text{negative angles}
\end{aligned}
```

again by :ref{#negative-angle}.

:::

The patterns are worth saying in words. Cosine keeps like with like, cosine times cosine and sine
times sine, and the sign in the middle is the opposite of the sign inside the bracket. Sine mixes
them, sine times cosine and cosine times sine, and keeps the sign it was given.

Go back to the example from the start, with $$a = b = \frac{\pi}{4}$$. The sine of a sum gives

```math
\sin\frac{\pi}{2} = \frac{\sqrt2}{2} \cdot \frac{\sqrt2}{2} + \frac{\sqrt2}{2} \cdot \frac{\sqrt2}{2} = \frac12 + \frac12 = 1
```

which is the right answer. Each sine in the formula is multiplied by a cosine, and a cosine is at
most $$1$$ in size, so neither height counts in full. That is the tilt from the start of the guide
at work.

## New exact values

The special angles give exact cosines and sines at $$\frac{\pi}{6}$$, $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$ and
$$\frac{\pi}{3}$$. Sums and differences of these reach new angles. For example,
$$\frac{\pi}{3} - \frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{\pi}{12}$$, which is $$15^\circ$$, so the addition formulas
give its sine and cosine exactly.

:::example[The sine and cosine of a twelfth of a half turn]

By :ref{#sin-cos-30-60} and :ref{#sin-cos-45}, $$\cos\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac12$$,
$$\sin\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$ and
$$\cos\frac{\pi}{4} = \sin\frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{\sqrt2}{2}$$. The sine of a difference gives

```math
\begin{aligned}
\sin\frac{\pi}{12} &= \sin\frac{\pi}{3}\cos\frac{\pi}{4} - \cos\frac{\pi}{3}\sin\frac{\pi}{4} \\
&= \frac{\sqrt3}{2} \cdot \frac{\sqrt2}{2} - \frac12 \cdot \frac{\sqrt2}{2} \\
&= \frac{\sqrt6 - \sqrt2}{4}
\end{aligned}
```

and the cosine of a difference gives

```math
\begin{aligned}
\cos\frac{\pi}{12} &= \cos\frac{\pi}{3}\cos\frac{\pi}{4} + \sin\frac{\pi}{3}\sin\frac{\pi}{4} \\
&= \frac12 \cdot \frac{\sqrt2}{2} + \frac{\sqrt3}{2} \cdot \frac{\sqrt2}{2} \\
&= \frac{\sqrt6 + \sqrt2}{4}
\end{aligned}
```

using $$\sqrt3\sqrt2 = \sqrt6$$. As decimals these are about $$0.2588$$ and $$0.9659$$, and a
calculator in radian mode gives the same for $$\sin(0.2618)$$ and $$\cos(0.2618)$$. As a check,
their squares are $$\frac{8 - 2\sqrt{12}}{16}$$ and $$\frac{8 + 2\sqrt{12}}{16}$$, which add to
$$1$$.

:::

The same values turn up further round the circle. The angle $$\frac{7\pi}{12}$$ is
$$\frac{\pi}{3} + \frac{\pi}{4}$$, so the cosine of a sum gives

```math
\cos\frac{7\pi}{12} = \frac12 \cdot \frac{\sqrt2}{2} - \frac{\sqrt3}{2} \cdot \frac{\sqrt2}{2} = \frac{\sqrt2 - \sqrt6}{4} \approx -0.2588
```

The answer is negative, which fits :ref{#quadrant-signs}: $$\frac{7\pi}{12}$$ is a little more than
$$\frac{\pi}{2}$$, in the second quadrant, where cosine is negative.

## Double angles

Turning by $$x$$ twice is turning by $$2x$$. So putting $$b = a$$ in the addition formulas gives the
cosine and sine of a double angle. For cosine there are three ways to write the answer, because
:ref{#pythagorean-identity} lets you trade $$\sin^2 x$$ for $$1 - \cos^2 x$$ and back.

:::corollary[Double angles]{#double-angle}

For every number $$x$$,

```math
\begin{aligned}
\sin 2x &= 2\sin x\cos x \\
\cos 2x &= \cos^2 x - \sin^2 x = 2\cos^2 x - 1 = 1 - 2\sin^2 x
\end{aligned}
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proofs_of_trigonometric_identities#Double-angle_identities"}

Put $$a = b = x$$ in :ref{#addition-formulas}. The sine of a sum gives

```math
\sin 2x = \sin(x + x) = \sin x\cos x + \cos x\sin x = 2\sin x\cos x
```

The cosine of a sum gives

```math
\cos 2x = \cos(x + x) = \cos x\cos x - \sin x\sin x = \cos^2 x - \sin^2 x
```

By :ref{#pythagorean-identity}, $$\sin^2 x = 1 - \cos^2 x$$, so

```math
\cos^2 x - \sin^2 x = \cos^2 x - (1 - \cos^2 x) = 2\cos^2 x - 1
```

In the same way $$\cos^2 x = 1 - \sin^2 x$$, so

```math
\cos^2 x - \sin^2 x = (1 - \sin^2 x) - \sin^2 x = 1 - 2\sin^2 x
```

:::

Check with $$x = \frac{\pi}{6}$$. Then $$\sin 2x = \sin\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$, and
$$2\sin\frac{\pi}{6}\cos\frac{\pi}{6} = 2 \cdot \frac12 \cdot \frac{\sqrt3}{2} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$.
Notice that $$\sin 2x$$ is not $$2\sin x$$: at $$x = \frac{\pi}{2}$$, $$\sin\pi = 0$$ but
$$2\sin\frac{\pi}{2} = 2$$.

:::example[Doubling an angle with sine 5/13]

Suppose $$\sin x = \frac{5}{13}$$ and $$x$$ is in the first quadrant. By
:ref{#pythagorean-identity}, $$\cos^2 x = 1 - \frac{25}{169} = \frac{144}{169}$$, and cosine is
positive in the first quadrant (:ref{#quadrant-signs}), so $$\cos x = \frac{12}{13}$$. Then

```math
\sin 2x = 2 \cdot \frac{5}{13} \cdot \frac{12}{13} = \frac{120}{169} \qquad \cos 2x = \frac{144}{169} - \frac{25}{169} = \frac{119}{169}
```

As a check, $$120^2 + 119^2 = 14400 + 14161 = 28561 = 169^2$$, so the point $$(\cos 2x, \sin 2x)$$
is on the unit circle, as it should be.

:::

## Half angles

The last two forms of $$\cos 2x$$ can be read the other way round. Each has one square in it, so
solving for that square gives $$\cos^2 x$$ and $$\sin^2 x$$ in terms of $$\cos 2x$$, the cosine of
twice the angle. Read with $$2x$$ as the known angle, this halves an angle.

:::corollary[Half angles]{#half-angle}

For every number $$x$$,

```math
\cos^2 x = \frac{1 + \cos 2x}{2} \qquad \sin^2 x = \frac{1 - \cos 2x}{2}
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proofs_of_trigonometric_identities#Half-angle_identities"}

By :ref{#double-angle}, $$\cos 2x = 2\cos^2 x - 1$$. Add $$1$$ to both sides to get
$$1 + \cos 2x = 2\cos^2 x$$, and divide by $$2$$.

Also by :ref{#double-angle}, $$\cos 2x = 1 - 2\sin^2 x$$. Add $$2\sin^2 x - \cos 2x$$ to both sides
to get $$2\sin^2 x = 1 - \cos 2x$$, and divide by $$2$$.

:::

A square has two square roots, so the formula gives $$\cos x$$ only up to its sign. The quadrant of
$$x$$ settles the sign, as it did for the angle with sine $$\frac{5}{13}$$.

:::example[The cosine of an eighth of a half turn]

Take $$x = \frac{\pi}{8}$$, so $$2x = \frac{\pi}{4}$$ and $$\cos 2x = \frac{\sqrt2}{2}$$
(:ref{#sin-cos-45}). By :ref{#half-angle},

```math
\cos^2\frac{\pi}{8} = \frac{1 + \frac{\sqrt2}{2}}{2} = \frac{2 + \sqrt2}{4}
```

The angle $$\frac{\pi}{8}$$ is in the first quadrant, so its cosine is positive
(:ref{#quadrant-signs}), and

```math
\cos\frac{\pi}{8} = \frac{\sqrt{2 + \sqrt2}}{2} \approx 0.9239
```

In the same way $$\sin\frac{\pi}{8} = \frac{\sqrt{2 - \sqrt2}}{2} \approx 0.3827$$. A calculator in
radian mode gives the same two decimals for $$\cos(0.3927)$$ and $$\sin(0.3927)$$.

:::

## The tangent of a sum

The tangent is sine divided by cosine (:ref{#tangent}), so the tangent of a sum is the sine of a sum
over the cosine of a sum. Dividing the top and the bottom by $$\cos a\cos b$$ turns every sine and
cosine into a tangent.

:::proposition[Tangent of a sum]{#tan-addition}

Let $$a$$ and $$b$$ be numbers with $$\cos a$$, $$\cos b$$ and $$\cos(a + b)$$ all not $$0$$. Then

```math
\tan(a + b) = \frac{\tan a + \tan b}{1 - \tan a\tan b}
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Tangent_of_Sum"}

By :ref{#tangent} and :ref{#addition-formulas},

```math
\begin{aligned}
\tan(a + b) &= \frac{\sin(a + b)}{\cos(a + b)} &&\qquad \text{definition of tangent} \\
&= \frac{\sin a\cos b + \cos a\sin b}{\cos a\cos b - \sin a\sin b} &&\qquad \text{addition formulas} \\
&= \frac{\dfrac{\sin a\cos b}{\cos a\cos b} + \dfrac{\cos a\sin b}{\cos a\cos b}}{\dfrac{\cos a\cos b}{\cos a\cos b} - \dfrac{\sin a\sin b}{\cos a\cos b}} &&\qquad \text{divide top and bottom by } \cos a\cos b \\
&= \frac{\tan a + \tan b}{1 - \tan a\tan b} &&\qquad \text{cancel, and sine over cosine is tangent}
\end{aligned}
```

Dividing by $$\cos a\cos b$$ is allowed because neither cosine is $$0$$. The bottom line is never
$$0$$ either: $$1 - \tan a\tan b$$ is $$\frac{\cos(a + b)}{\cos a\cos b}$$, and
$$\cos(a + b) \ne 0$$.

:::

Check with $$a = b = \frac{\pi}{6}$$, where $$\tan\frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{1}{\sqrt3}$$. The formula
gives

```math
\tan\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{\frac{1}{\sqrt3} + \frac{1}{\sqrt3}}{1 - \frac13} = \frac{2}{\sqrt3} \cdot \frac32 = \frac{3}{\sqrt3} = \sqrt3
```

which matches the value from the special angles. The conditions matter. With
$$a = b = \frac{\pi}{4}$$ the bottom is $$1 - 1 \cdot 1 = 0$$, and indeed $$\tan\frac{\pi}{2}$$ is
not defined, since $$\cos\frac{\pi}{2} = 0$$.

Putting $$-b$$ in place of $$b$$ gives the tangent of a difference. Since $$\tan(-b) = -\tan b$$
(:ref{#tangent-period}),

```math
\tan(a - b) = \frac{\tan a - \tan b}{1 + \tan a\tan b}
```

whenever $$\cos a$$, $$\cos b$$ and $$\cos(a - b)$$ are not $$0$$.

### The angle between two roads

On a map, two straight roads leave a junction at $$O$$. One climbs $$1$$ for every $$2$$ across,
slope $$\frac12$$, and the other climbs $$3$$ for every $$1$$ across, slope $$3$$. The tangent of a
difference finds the angle between them without finding either angle first.

Let $$\theta_1$$ and $$\theta_2$$ be the angles the roads make with the $$x$$-axis, both between
$$0$$ and $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$. By :ref{#tangent-slope}, $$\tan\theta_1 = \frac12$$ and
$$\tan\theta_2 = 3$$. So

```math
\tan(\theta_2 - \theta_1) = \frac{3 - \frac12}{1 + 3 \cdot \frac12} = \frac{\frac52}{\frac52} = 1
```

The difference $$\theta_2 - \theta_1$$ lies between $$-\frac{\pi}{2}$$ and $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$, and
the only angle there with tangent $$1$$ is $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$ (:ref{#inverse-tan}). So the roads meet
at $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$. For lines with slopes $$m_1$$ and $$m_2$$ the bottom of the fraction is
$$1 + m_1m_2$$, which is $$0$$ exactly when the lines are perpendicular
(:ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/perpendicular-slopes}).

## Where a robot arm puts its hand

A robot arm on a workbench has a shoulder joint at $$O$$, an upper arm $$30$$ cm long and a forearm
$$20$$ cm long. The shoulder turns the upper arm by an angle $$a$$ from the $$x$$-axis. The elbow
then turns the forearm by a further angle $$b$$, measured from the line of the upper arm. So the
forearm points at angle $$a + b$$, and the addition formulas find the hand from the two joint
angles.

```plane
point O 0 0 sw
point M 2.598 1.5 se
point H 3.116 3.432 n
point X 3.464 2.0 hidden
point A 1 0 hidden
point F 3.6 0 hidden
segment O F color=muted
segment M X color=muted dashed
segment O M color=blue label="30 cm"
segment M H color=orange label="20 cm"
angle A O M color=blue r=0.8 label="a"
angle X M H color=orange r=0.5 label="b"
caption "The shoulder at O turns the upper arm by a = π/6. The elbow at M turns the forearm by b = π/4 more, so the forearm points at angle a + b and the hand is at H."
```

The elbow is at $$M = 30(\cos a, \sin a)$$ (:ref{#circle-point-radius}). The forearm points along
the upper arm turned by $$b$$, and turning the direction $$G_a(E_1)$$ by $$b$$ gives
$$G_{a + b}(E_1)$$ (:ref{#radian-turns}). So the hand is at

```math
H = 30(\cos a, \sin a) + 20\big(\cos(a + b), \sin(a + b)\big)
```

Take $$a = \frac{\pi}{6}$$ and $$b = \frac{\pi}{4}$$. The elbow is at
$$M = (15\sqrt3, 15) \approx (25.98, 15)$$. By :ref{#addition-formulas},

```math
\begin{aligned}
\cos\frac{5\pi}{12} &= \frac{\sqrt3}{2} \cdot \frac{\sqrt2}{2} - \frac12 \cdot \frac{\sqrt2}{2} = \frac{\sqrt6 - \sqrt2}{4} \\
\sin\frac{5\pi}{12} &= \frac12 \cdot \frac{\sqrt2}{2} + \frac{\sqrt3}{2} \cdot \frac{\sqrt2}{2} = \frac{\sqrt6 + \sqrt2}{4}
\end{aligned}
```

So the forearm adds $$\left(5(\sqrt6 - \sqrt2), 5(\sqrt6 + \sqrt2)\right) \approx (5.18, 19.32)$$,
and the hand is at about $$(31.16, 34.32)$$, in centimetres from the shoulder. The controller of a
real arm with more joints does the same sum once for each joint.

::::exercise{#ex-addition-formulas-check}

Work out $$\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{3} - \frac{\pi}{6}\right)$$ in two ways: directly, and with
:ref{#cos-difference}. Check that the answers agree.

:::solution

Directly, $$\frac{\pi}{3} - \frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{\pi}{6}$$, and
$$\cos\frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$ (:ref{#sin-cos-30-60}). With the formula,

```math
\cos\frac{\pi}{3}\cos\frac{\pi}{6} + \sin\frac{\pi}{3}\sin\frac{\pi}{6} = \frac12 \cdot \frac{\sqrt3}{2} + \frac{\sqrt3}{2} \cdot \frac12 = \frac{\sqrt3}{4} + \frac{\sqrt3}{4} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}
```

The two answers agree.

::::exercise{#ex-addition-formulas-seven-twelfths}

Use the sine of a sum to find $$\sin\frac{7\pi}{12}$$ exactly, writing $$\frac{7\pi}{12}$$ as
$$\frac{\pi}{3} + \frac{\pi}{4}$$. Give a decimal value too.

:::solution

By :ref{#addition-formulas}, :ref{#sin-cos-30-60} and :ref{#sin-cos-45},

```math
\sin\frac{7\pi}{12} = \sin\frac{\pi}{3}\cos\frac{\pi}{4} + \cos\frac{\pi}{3}\sin\frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2} \cdot \frac{\sqrt2}{2} + \frac12 \cdot \frac{\sqrt2}{2} = \frac{\sqrt6 + \sqrt2}{4}
```

which is about $$0.9659$$. That is the same as $$\cos\frac{\pi}{12}$$. It has to be:
$$\frac{7\pi}{12} = \frac{\pi}{2} + \frac{\pi}{12}$$, and a quarter turn more takes cosine to sine
(:ref{#quarter-turn-identities}).

::::exercise{#ex-addition-formulas-not-adding}

Is $$\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{6} + \frac{\pi}{3}\right)$$ equal to
$$\sin\frac{\pi}{6} + \sin\frac{\pi}{3}$$? Work out both, then show that the sine of a sum gives the
right one.

:::solution

The angle is $$\frac{\pi}{6} + \frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{\pi}{2}$$, so the left side is
$$\sin\frac{\pi}{2} = 1$$. The right side is $$\frac12 + \frac{\sqrt3}{2} \approx 1.37$$, which is
bigger than $$1$$. So they are not equal.

The sine of a sum gives

```math
\sin\frac{\pi}{6}\cos\frac{\pi}{3} + \cos\frac{\pi}{6}\sin\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac12 \cdot \frac12 + \frac{\sqrt3}{2} \cdot \frac{\sqrt3}{2} = \frac14 + \frac34 = 1
```

which is right.

::::exercise{#ex-addition-formulas-double}

Suppose $$\cos x = -\frac35$$ and $$x$$ is in the second quadrant. Find $$\sin x$$, $$\sin 2x$$ and
$$\cos 2x$$, and check that $$(\cos 2x, \sin 2x)$$ is on the unit circle.

:::solution

By :ref{#pythagorean-identity}, $$\sin^2 x = 1 - \frac{9}{25} = \frac{16}{25}$$. Sine is positive in
the second quadrant (:ref{#quadrant-signs}), so $$\sin x = \frac45$$. By :ref{#double-angle},

```math
\sin 2x = 2 \cdot \frac45 \cdot \left(-\frac35\right) = -\frac{24}{25} \qquad \cos 2x = \frac{9}{25} - \frac{16}{25} = -\frac{7}{25}
```

The check: $$\frac{576}{625} + \frac{49}{625} = \frac{625}{625} = 1$$.

::::exercise{#ex-addition-formulas-half}

Use :ref{#half-angle} with $$x = \frac{\pi}{12}$$ to show that
$$\sin\frac{\pi}{12} = \frac{\sqrt{2 - \sqrt3}}{2}$$. Then show that this is the same number as
$$\frac{\sqrt6 - \sqrt2}{4}$$, the value found from a difference of angles.

:::solution

Here $$2x = \frac{\pi}{6}$$, and $$\cos\frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$. By :ref{#half-angle},

```math
\sin^2\frac{\pi}{12} = \frac{1 - \frac{\sqrt3}{2}}{2} = \frac{2 - \sqrt3}{4}
```

The angle $$\frac{\pi}{12}$$ is in the first quadrant, so its sine is positive, and
$$\sin\frac{\pi}{12} = \frac{\sqrt{2 - \sqrt3}}{2}$$.

Both numbers are positive, so they are equal exactly when their squares are. The square of the
second is

```math
\left(\frac{\sqrt6 - \sqrt2}{4}\right)^2 = \frac{6 - 2\sqrt{12} + 2}{16} = \frac{8 - 4\sqrt3}{16} = \frac{2 - \sqrt3}{4}
```

using $$\sqrt{12} = 2\sqrt3$$. The squares agree, so the two numbers agree. Both are about
$$0.2588$$.

::::exercise{#ex-addition-formulas-tan}

Use the tangent of a difference to find $$\tan\frac{\pi}{12}$$ exactly, from
$$\frac{\pi}{12} = \frac{\pi}{3} - \frac{\pi}{4}$$. Write the answer without a square root on the
bottom.

:::solution

Here $$\tan\frac{\pi}{3} = \sqrt3$$ and $$\tan\frac{\pi}{4} = 1$$. So

```math
\tan\frac{\pi}{12} = \frac{\sqrt3 - 1}{1 + \sqrt3 \cdot 1} = \frac{\sqrt3 - 1}{\sqrt3 + 1}
```

Multiply the top and the bottom by $$\sqrt3 - 1$$:

```math
\frac{(\sqrt3 - 1)^2}{(\sqrt3 + 1)(\sqrt3 - 1)} = \frac{3 - 2\sqrt3 + 1}{3 - 1} = \frac{4 - 2\sqrt3}{2} = 2 - \sqrt3
```

That is about $$0.2679$$, which matches $$\sin\frac{\pi}{12} \div \cos\frac{\pi}{12}$$ from the
example: $$0.2588 \div 0.9659 \approx 0.2679$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-addition-formulas-product}

Show that for all numbers $$a$$ and $$b$$,

```math
\sin(a + b)\sin(a - b) = \sin^2 a - \sin^2 b
```

Then check it with $$a = \frac{\pi}{2}$$ and $$b = \frac{\pi}{6}$$.

:::solution

By :ref{#addition-formulas}, the left side is a sum times a difference of the same two terms:

```math
\begin{aligned}
\sin(a + b)\sin(a - b) &= (\sin a\cos b + \cos a\sin b)(\sin a\cos b - \cos a\sin b) &&\qquad \text{addition formulas} \\
&= \sin^2 a\cos^2 b - \cos^2 a\sin^2 b &&\qquad (u + v)(u - v) = u^2 - v^2 \\
&= \sin^2 a\,(1 - \sin^2 b) - (1 - \sin^2 a)\sin^2 b &&\qquad \text{Pythagorean identity, twice} \\
&= \sin^2 a - \sin^2 a\sin^2 b - \sin^2 b + \sin^2 a\sin^2 b &&\qquad \text{multiply out} \\
&= \sin^2 a - \sin^2 b &&\qquad \text{the middle terms cancel}
\end{aligned}
```

The check:
$$\sin\frac{2\pi}{3}\sin\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2} \cdot \frac{\sqrt3}{2} = \frac34$$, and
$$\sin^2\frac{\pi}{2} - \sin^2\frac{\pi}{6} = 1 - \frac14 = \frac34$$. Here
$$\sin\frac{2\pi}{3} = \sin\frac{\pi}{3}$$ by :ref{#supplement-identities}.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-addition-formulas-triple}

Write $$3x$$ as $$2x + x$$ to show that $$\cos 3x = 4\cos^3 x - 3\cos x$$ for every $$x$$. Check it
with $$x = \frac{\pi}{3}$$.

:::solution

By :ref{#addition-formulas} and :ref{#double-angle},

```math
\begin{aligned}
\cos 3x &= \cos 2x\cos x - \sin 2x\sin x &&\qquad \text{cosine of a sum} \\
&= (2\cos^2 x - 1)\cos x - 2\sin x\cos x\sin x &&\qquad \text{double angles} \\
&= 2\cos^3 x - \cos x - 2\sin^2 x\cos x &&\qquad \text{multiply out} \\
&= 2\cos^3 x - \cos x - 2(1 - \cos^2 x)\cos x &&\qquad \text{Pythagorean identity} \\
&= 2\cos^3 x - \cos x - 2\cos x + 2\cos^3 x &&\qquad \text{multiply out} \\
&= 4\cos^3 x - 3\cos x &&\qquad \text{collect terms}
\end{aligned}
```

The check: $$\cos\pi = -1$$, and $$4 \cdot \frac18 - 3 \cdot \frac12 = \frac12 - \frac32 = -1$$.
