Imagine someone measuring a footpath with a trundle wheel, a wheel on a long handle that clicks as
it rolls. She never lays a tape on the ground. She walks the wheel along the path, and the distance
comes from how far the wheel has turned. One whole turn rolls out one circumference of path, half a
turn rolls out half of that, and a tenth of a turn rolls out a tenth.

So the size of a turn can be read as a distance. Mathematicians measure angles this way, with a
wheel of radius $$1$$: the size of a turn is how far a point on the rim travels. That unit is the
**radian**, and a full turn is $$2\pi$$ radians, about $$6.28$$. In this guide we'll get used to
radians, convert them to and from degrees, and see how they fit arcs, sectors and turning wheels.

## Measuring a turn by distance

Degrees cut a full turn into $$360$$ equal parts. The number $$360$$ is a choice made thousands of
years ago, and nothing about a circle forces it. Radians use the circle itself instead: roll a wheel
of radius $$1$$, and measure the turn by the distance a point on its rim covers.

A full turn of that wheel covers its circumference, $$2\pi \cdot 1 = 2\pi$$
(:ref{#area-and-scaling/circumference-formula}). An angle of $$x^\circ$$ is $$\frac{x}{360}$$ of a
full turn (:ref{#distance-and-angles/degree}), so it should cover $$\frac{x}{360}$$ of that
distance. And $$\frac{x}{360} \cdot 2\pi = \frac{\pi x}{180}$$, which is the definition.

:::definition[Radians]{#radian}

An angle of $$x^\circ$$ has $$\frac{\pi x}{180}$$ **radians**.

:::

Radians are a new unit for the same sizes, not a new way to find them. An angle still gets its size
from the share of a disc its sector takes up, as in :ref{#distance-and-angles/degree}, and this
chapter keeps that as its starting point. What changes is the number we write: a straight angle is
$$180^\circ$$, and in radians it is $$\frac{\pi \cdot 180}{180} = \pi$$.

From now on, an angle written without the degree sign is in radians. So an angle of $$2$$ means
$$2$$ radians, and an angle of $$2^\circ$$ means $$2$$ degrees. The angles you'll meet most often
are these:

| Degrees              | Radians           | Roughly  |
| -------------------- | ----------------- | -------- |
| $$360^\circ$$        | $$2\pi$$          | $$6.28$$ |
| $$180^\circ$$        | $$\pi$$           | $$3.14$$ |
| $$90^\circ$$         | $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$ | $$1.57$$ |
| $$60^\circ$$         | $$\frac{\pi}{3}$$ | $$1.05$$ |
| $$45^\circ$$         | $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$ | $$0.79$$ |
| $$30^\circ$$         | $$\frac{\pi}{6}$$ | $$0.52$$ |
| about $$57.3^\circ$$ | $$1$$             | $$1$$    |

Turn the radius in the diagram below and watch the orange arc grow from $$E_1 = (1, 0)$$. The ticks
along it mark each whole radius length, so you can count the radians as you turn. A full turn holds
six whole radii and a little more, since $$2\pi \approx 6.28$$. That the arc is exactly $$x$$ radii
long is proved in [Arcs and sectors in radians](#arcs-and-sectors-in-radians).

## Converting between degrees and radians

A half turn is $$180^\circ$$ and also $$\pi$$ radians, and every conversion comes from that one
pair. To go from degrees to radians, multiply by $$\frac{\pi}{180}$$. To go back, multiply by
$$\frac{180}{\pi}$$, which undoes it.

:::example[Converting both ways]

- **$$135^\circ$$ to radians**: $$135 \cdot \frac{\pi}{180} = \frac{135\pi}{180} = \frac{3\pi}{4}$$,
  dividing the top and bottom by $$45$$
- **$$20^\circ$$ to radians**: $$\frac{20\pi}{180} = \frac{\pi}{9} \approx 0.349$$
- **$$\frac{5\pi}{6}$$ to degrees**:
  $$\frac{5\pi}{6} \cdot \frac{180}{\pi} = \frac{5 \cdot 180}{6} = 150$$, so the angle is
  $$150^\circ$$
- **$$2.5$$ radians to degrees**: $$2.5 \cdot \frac{180}{\pi} = \frac{450}{\pi} \approx 143.2$$, so
  the angle is about $$143.2^\circ$$

:::

Notice that when an angle is a fraction of $$\pi$$, the $$\pi$$ cancels on the way to degrees. That
is why angles in radians are usually written with $$\pi$$ in them: $$\frac{3\pi}{4}$$ says "three
quarters of a half turn" at a glance, and $$2.356$$ hides it.

The angle of exactly $$1$$ radian has $$\frac{180}{\pi}$$ degrees, about $$57.3^\circ$$. It is a
little less than $$60^\circ$$, because $$60^\circ$$ is $$\frac{\pi}{3}$$ radians and $$\pi$$ is a
little more than $$3$$.

## Arcs and sectors in radians

On a wheel of radius $$1$$, the arc an angle cuts out is as long as the angle in radians. A bigger
wheel rolls out more ground for the same turn: radius $$3$$ gives three times as much. So on a
circle of radius $$r$$ the arc should be $$r$$ times the angle, and the sector's area should follow
in the same way from :ref{#distance-and-angles/sector-area}.

There is one gap to fill first. An **arc** is the part of a circle inside an angle
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/sector}), and earlier chapters found the length of a whole circle, but
never of an arc. Cut a circle into $$8$$ equal slices from its centre, and the turns about the
centre carry each slice onto the next, so the $$8$$ arcs match and each is $$\frac{1}{8}$$ of the
circle. In the same way, an angle that is $$\frac{3}{8}$$ of a turn cuts $$3$$ of those arcs, which
is $$\frac{3}{8}$$ of the circle, matching its sector. We take this from the picture for every
angle:

:::remark[Rules for arcs]{#arc-rule}

Let $$A$$ be an angle with vertex $$P$$, and let $$C$$ be a circle with centre $$P$$. The arc that
$$A$$ cuts from $$C$$ is the same fraction of the length of $$C$$ as the sector that $$A$$ cuts from
the disc inside $$C$$ is of the area of that disc. So an angle of $$x^\circ$$ cuts an arc that is
$$\frac{x}{360}$$ of the circle.

This is taken as given.

::see-also{src="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arc_length#Arcs_of_circles"}

:::

With that rule, both lengths and areas come straight from the share of the turn.

:::proposition[Arc length and sector area in radians]{#radian-arc-sector}

In a circle of radius $$r$$, an angle of $$x$$ radians at the centre cuts out:

1. **An arc** of length $$rx$$
2. **A sector** of area $$\frac{1}{2}r^2x$$

So an angle of $$1$$ radian cuts an arc as long as the radius.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circular_sector#Area"}

An angle of $$x$$ radians has $$d$$ degrees, where $$\frac{\pi d}{180} = x$$ (:ref{#radian}).
Multiplying both sides by $$\frac{180}{\pi}$$, $$d = \frac{180x}{\pi}$$. So the share of a full turn
is

```math
\frac{d}{360} = \frac{180x}{360\pi} = \frac{x}{2\pi}
```

**The arc.** By :ref{#arc-rule}, the arc is $$\frac{d}{360}$$ of the circle. The circle has length
$$2\pi r$$ (:ref{#area-and-scaling/circumference-formula}), so the arc has length

```math
\frac{x}{2\pi} \cdot 2\pi r = rx
```

**The sector.** By :ref{#distance-and-angles/sector-area}, the sector has area $$\frac{d}{360}$$
times $$\pi r^2$$, so

```math
\frac{x}{2\pi} \cdot \pi r^2 = \frac{1}{2}r^2x
```

With $$x = 1$$, the arc has length $$r \cdot 1 = r$$.

:::

The name comes from that last fact: one radian is the angle whose arc is one radius long. So the
radian measure of an angle counts how many radii fit along its arc, on a circle of any size.

:::example[An angle of 2 in a circle of radius 3]

An angle of $$2$$ radians at the centre of a circle of radius $$3$$ cuts an arc of length
$$3 \cdot 2 = 6$$ and a sector of area $$\frac{1}{2} \cdot 3^2 \cdot 2 = 9$$.

Check with degrees. The angle is $$\frac{180 \cdot 2}{\pi} = \frac{360}{\pi} \approx 114.6^\circ$$,
so by :ref{#distance-and-angles/sector-area} the sector has area

```math
\frac{360/\pi}{360} \cdot \pi \cdot 3^2 = \frac{1}{\pi} \cdot 9\pi = 9
```

the same. The degree version needs $$360$$, $$\pi$$ and a cancellation, and the radian version needs
none of them.

:::

## Turns measured in radians

The rest of this chapter turns points about the origin $$O$$ all the time, so we'll measure those
turns in radians as well. A turn by $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$ is a quarter turn, a turn by $$\pi$$ is a half
turn, and a turn by $$2\pi$$ goes once round. As in
[Slides and turns](../isometries/slides-and-turns.mdx), a negative number means turning clockwise,
and a number bigger than $$2\pi$$ means going round more than once.

:::definition[Turns measured in radians]{#radians-turn-notation}

For every number $$x$$, in this chapter $$G_x$$ is the turn about $$O$$ by $$x$$ radians: the turn
by $$\left(\frac{180x}{\pi}\right)^\circ$$ of :ref{#isometries/rotation}. It turns anticlockwise
when $$x > 0$$ and clockwise when $$x < 0$$.

:::

Earlier chapters wrote turns in degrees, so the same letter now means something different. There,
$$G_{90}$$ was the quarter turn; here it would be a turn by $$90$$ radians, more than $$14$$ whole
turns. We keep the name $$G_{90}$$ for one thing only, the formula $$G_{90}(x, y) = (-y, x)$$ of
:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/quarter-turn}, and the next proposition shows it is $$G_{\pi/2}$$.

:::proposition[Turns in radians]{#radian-turns}

For all numbers $$x$$ and $$y$$ and every integer $$n$$:

1. **Turns add**: $$G_x \circ G_y = G_{x + y}$$
2. **A full turn**: $$G_{2\pi} = G_0 = I$$, the identity
3. **Whole turns make no difference**: $$G_{x + 2\pi n} = G_x$$, so every turn is $$G_w$$ for some
   $$w$$ with $$0 \le w < 2\pi$$
4. **The quarter turn**: $$G_{\pi/2}(x, y) = (-y, x)$$, the formula $$G_{90}$$
5. **The half turn**: $$G_\pi(x, y) = (-x, -y)$$, the half turn $$H_O$$

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_(mathematics)#Two_dimensions"}

Each part is a fact about turns in degrees, from the Isometries chapter, written with
$$\frac{180x}{\pi}$$ degrees in place of $$x$$ radians (:ref{#radians-turn-notation}).

1. $$G_x \circ G_y$$ turns by $$\frac{180y}{\pi}$$ degrees and then by $$\frac{180x}{\pi}$$ degrees.
   By rule 2 of :ref{#isometries/rotation-rules}, that is a turn by
   $$\frac{180y}{\pi} + \frac{180x}{\pi} = \frac{180(x + y)}{\pi}$$ degrees, which is $$G_{x + y}$$.
2. $$2\pi$$ radians is $$\frac{180 \cdot 2\pi}{\pi} = 360$$ degrees, and $$0$$ radians is $$0$$
   degrees. Both turns are the identity, by rule 3 of :ref{#isometries/rotation-rules}.
3. $$x + 2\pi n$$ radians is $$\frac{180x}{\pi} + 360n$$ degrees, so the turn is the same as the
   turn by $$\frac{180x}{\pi}$$ degrees, by :ref{#isometries/rotation-whole-turns}. For the last
   part, that proposition gives $$\frac{180x}{\pi} = 360n + v$$ with $$0 \le v < 360$$; take
   $$w = \frac{\pi v}{180}$$, which has $$0 \le w < 2\pi$$.
4. $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$ radians is $$90$$ degrees, anticlockwise. The formula
   $$G_{90}(x, y) = (-y, x)$$ is that turn: it is $$S \circ R_1$$
   (:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/combining-moves-flips}), which is the turn by $$90^\circ$$
   anticlockwise by :ref{#isometries/crossing-mirrors}.
5. $$\pi$$ radians is $$180$$ degrees. The half turn about $$O$$ is the reflection through $$O$$
   (:ref{#isometries/half-turn}), and its formula is $$H_O(P) = -P$$
   (:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/half-turn-formula}), which is $$(-x, -y)$$ for $$P = (x, y)$$.

:::

Part 1 also says that the order of two turns about $$O$$ never matters, because $$x + y = y + x$$.
Part 4 rests on :ref{#isometries/crossing-mirrors}, and the proof of that theorem follows the
directions of points round $$O$$ in the picture.

:::example[Cutting turns down to size]

- $$\frac{9\pi}{2} = 4\pi + \frac{\pi}{2}$$, which is two whole turns and a quarter, so
  $$G_{9\pi/2} = G_{\pi/2}$$. It sends $$(3, 1)$$ to $$(-1, 3)$$.
- $$-\frac{\pi}{2} = -2\pi + \frac{3\pi}{2}$$, so $$G_{-\pi/2} = G_{3\pi/2}$$: a quarter turn
  clockwise is three quarter turns anticlockwise.
- $$-3\pi = -4\pi + \pi$$, so $$G_{-3\pi} = G_\pi = H_O$$. It sends $$(3, 1)$$ to $$(-3, -1)$$.

To cut a turn down, add or take away whole multiples of $$2\pi$$ until the angle lies from $$0$$ up
to $$2\pi$$.

:::

## Wheels and angular speed

A car's speedometer never measures the road. A sensor counts how fast a wheel turns, and the car's
computer turns that into a speed using the size of the tyre. Radians make that step one
multiplication.

Say the wheels have radius $$0.3$$ metres and each turns through $$50$$ radians every second. In one
second a point on the tread travels $$0.3 \cdot 50 = 15$$ metres round the wheel
(:ref{#radian-arc-sector}), and the tread rolls that much road under the car. So the car moves at
$$15$$ metres per second, which is $$15 \cdot 3.6 = 54$$ kilometres per hour. In whole turns, the
wheel goes round $$\frac{50}{2\pi} \approx 7.96$$ times a second.

The number of radians turned each second is the **angular speed**. For a wheel of radius $$r$$
turning at angular speed $$\omega$$ (the Greek letter omega), the rim moves at $$r\omega$$, and so
does a car rolling on that wheel. So fitting bigger tyres without telling the computer makes the
speedometer read low. With radius $$0.33$$ metres, the same $$50$$ radians a second carries the car
$$16.5$$ metres, about $$59.4$$ kilometres per hour, while the computer, still assuming $$0.3$$,
shows $$54$$.

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on radians, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-radians-to-radians}

Write each angle in radians, as a fraction of $$\pi$$:

1. $$120^\circ$$
2. $$270^\circ$$
3. $$15^\circ$$
4. $$225^\circ$$

:::solution

Multiply by $$\frac{\pi}{180}$$ (:ref{#radian}) and cancel.

1. $$\frac{120\pi}{180} = \frac{2\pi}{3}$$, dividing the top and bottom by $$60$$
2. $$\frac{270\pi}{180} = \frac{3\pi}{2}$$, dividing by $$90$$
3. $$\frac{15\pi}{180} = \frac{\pi}{12}$$, dividing by $$15$$
4. $$\frac{225\pi}{180} = \frac{5\pi}{4}$$, dividing by $$45$$

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-radians-to-degrees}

Write each angle in degrees:

1. $$\frac{5\pi}{3}$$
2. $$\frac{7\pi}{6}$$
3. $$\frac{\pi}{10}$$
4. $$3$$, to one decimal place

:::solution

Multiply by $$\frac{180}{\pi}$$, which undoes multiplying by $$\frac{\pi}{180}$$ (:ref{#radian}).

1. $$\frac{5\pi}{3} \cdot \frac{180}{\pi} = \frac{5 \cdot 180}{3} = 300$$, so $$300^\circ$$
2. $$\frac{7 \cdot 180}{6} = 210$$, so $$210^\circ$$
3. $$\frac{180}{10} = 18$$, so $$18^\circ$$
4. $$3 \cdot \frac{180}{\pi} = \frac{540}{\pi} \approx 171.9$$, so about $$171.9^\circ$$

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-radians-one-radian}

Using only $$3 < \pi < 4$$, show that an angle of $$1$$ radian is more than $$45^\circ$$ and less
than $$60^\circ$$.

:::solution

By :ref{#radian}, $$45^\circ$$ is $$\frac{45\pi}{180} = \frac{\pi}{4}$$ radians and $$60^\circ$$ is
$$\frac{\pi}{3}$$ radians. Since $$\pi < 4$$, dividing by $$4$$ gives $$\frac{\pi}{4} < 1$$. Since
$$\pi > 3$$, dividing by $$3$$ gives $$\frac{\pi}{3} > 1$$. So

```math
\frac{\pi}{4} < 1 < \frac{\pi}{3}
```

and an angle of $$1$$ radian lies between $$45^\circ$$ and $$60^\circ$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-radians-arc-to-angle}

An arc of length $$10$$ cm lies on a circle of radius $$4$$ cm. Find the angle it makes at the
centre, in radians and in degrees, and the area of its sector.

:::solution

By :ref{#radian-arc-sector}, the arc has length $$rx$$, so $$4x = 10$$ and $$x = 2.5$$ radians. In
degrees that is $$2.5 \cdot \frac{180}{\pi} \approx 143.2^\circ$$. The sector has area

```math
\frac{1}{2} r^2 x = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 16 \cdot 2.5 = 20
```

square centimetres.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-radians-wiper}

A windscreen wiper turns about a pivot through $$\frac{2\pi}{3}$$ radians. Its rubber blade runs
from $$15$$ cm to $$60$$ cm from the pivot.

1. How far does the tip of the blade travel in one sweep?
2. What area of windscreen does the blade clean in one sweep?

:::solution

1. The tip moves along an arc of radius $$60$$, so by :ref{#radian-arc-sector} it travels
   $$60 \cdot \frac{2\pi}{3} = 40\pi \approx 125.7$$ cm.
2. The cleaned region is the sector of radius $$60$$ with the sector of radius $$15$$ taken away. By
   :ref{#radian-arc-sector} their areas are

   ```math
   \frac{1}{2} \cdot 60^2 \cdot \frac{2\pi}{3} = 1200\pi \qquad \frac{1}{2} \cdot 15^2 \cdot \frac{2\pi}{3} = 75\pi
   ```

   so the blade cleans $$1200\pi - 75\pi = 1125\pi \approx 3534$$ square centimetres.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-radians-reduce-turns}

Write each turn as $$G_w$$ with $$0 \le w < 2\pi$$:

1. $$G_{7\pi/2}$$
2. $$G_{-\pi/3}$$
3. $$G_{13\pi/4}$$

Then find where $$G_{7\pi/2}$$ sends the point $$(2, 1)$$.

:::solution

Add or take away multiples of $$2\pi$$ (part 3 of :ref{#radian-turns}).

1. $$\frac{7\pi}{2} = 2\pi + \frac{3\pi}{2}$$, so $$G_{7\pi/2} = G_{3\pi/2}$$
2. $$-\frac{\pi}{3} = -2\pi + \frac{5\pi}{3}$$, so $$G_{-\pi/3} = G_{5\pi/3}$$
3. $$\frac{13\pi}{4} = 2\pi + \frac{5\pi}{4}$$, so $$G_{13\pi/4} = G_{5\pi/4}$$

For the point, $$\frac{3\pi}{2} = \frac{\pi}{2} + \pi$$, so by part 1 of :ref{#radian-turns},
$$G_{3\pi/2} = G_\pi \circ G_{\pi/2}$$: first a quarter turn, then a half turn. By parts 4 and 5,

```math
(2, 1) \xrightarrow{\ G_{\pi/2}\ } (-1, 2) \xrightarrow{\ G_\pi\ } (1, -2)
```

So $$G_{7\pi/2}$$ sends $$(2, 1)$$ to $$(1, -2)$$. Check: a turn by $$\frac{3\pi}{2}$$ anticlockwise
is a quarter turn clockwise, and turning $$(2, 1)$$ a quarter turn clockwise lands at $$(1, -2)$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-radians-quarter-turns}

Using only the formula $$G_{\pi/2}(x, y) = (-y, x)$$ and part 1 of :ref{#radian-turns}:

1. Find a formula for $$G_\pi$$, and check that it agrees with part 5.
2. Find a formula for $$G_{3\pi/2}$$.
3. Show that doing $$G_{\pi/2}$$ four times gives the identity, and explain why part 2 of
   :ref{#radian-turns} says the same.

:::solution

1. $$G_\pi = G_{\pi/2} \circ G_{\pi/2}$$, since $$\frac{\pi}{2} + \frac{\pi}{2} = \pi$$. Then
   $$(x, y)$$ goes to $$(-y, x)$$, and that goes to $$(-x, -y)$$. So $$G_\pi(x, y) = (-x, -y)$$,
   which is $$H_O$$, as part 5 says.
2. $$G_{3\pi/2} = G_{\pi/2} \circ G_\pi$$. Then $$(x, y)$$ goes to $$(-x, -y)$$, and the quarter
   turn sends that to $$(-(-y), -x) = (y, -x)$$. So $$G_{3\pi/2}(x, y) = (y, -x)$$.
3. One more quarter turn sends $$(y, -x)$$ to $$(-(-x), y) = (x, y)$$, so four quarter turns move
   every point back to where it started. By part 1 the four together are the turn by
   $$4 \cdot \frac{\pi}{2} = 2\pi$$, which is the identity by part 2.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-radians-earth}

The Earth turns once about its axis in roughly $$24$$ hours. Take the radius at the equator to be
$$6400$$ km.

1. What is its angular speed, in radians per hour?
2. How fast is a point on the equator moving, in kilometres per hour?

:::solution

1. One full turn is $$2\pi$$ radians, so the angular speed is $$\frac{2\pi}{24} = \frac{\pi}{12}$$
   radians per hour, about $$0.26$$.
2. In one hour a point on the equator moves along an arc of radius $$6400$$ km and angle
   $$\frac{\pi}{12}$$, so by :ref{#radian-arc-sector} it travels

   ```math
   6400 \cdot \frac{\pi}{12} = \frac{1600\pi}{3} \approx 1676
   ```

   kilometres. So it moves at roughly $$1676$$ kilometres per hour.

:::
