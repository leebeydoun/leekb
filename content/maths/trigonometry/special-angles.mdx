  Exact values of sine and cosine at 30, 45 and 60 degrees, read from half a square and half an
  equilateral triangle.
---

Open a school geometry set and you'll find two plastic set squares. One has corners of $$45^\circ$$,
$$45^\circ$$ and $$90^\circ$$, and its two short edges are the same length: it is half of a square,
cut along the diagonal. The other has corners of $$30^\circ$$, $$60^\circ$$ and $$90^\circ$$, and
two copies laid back to back make a triangle with three equal sides.

A square and a triangle with equal sides are shapes whose lengths we know exactly, so the two set
squares carry exact lengths with them. Put the corner of one at the centre of the unit circle, with
its longest side along a radius, and its far corner lands at a point whose coordinates are fractions
and square roots. Those coordinates are the sine and cosine of $$30^\circ$$, $$45^\circ$$ and
$$60^\circ$$, which are called the **special angles**. In this guide we'll read the values off the
two set squares, and then prove them from the turn that defines sine and cosine.

## Two set squares

The first set square is half of a square. Take the square with sides of length $$1$$. Its diagonal
is the long side of a right triangle with legs $$1$$ and $$1$$, so by the Pythagoras theorem
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/pythagoras}) its length squared is $$1^2 + 1^2 = 2$$, and the diagonal is
$$\sqrt{2}$$. Fold the square along that diagonal and the two halves match, so the diagonal cuts
each of the two corners it joins into equal halves of a right angle, $$45^\circ$$ each.

<HalfSquare caption="Half of a square of side 1: two sides of 1 meet at a right angle, the diagonal is √2, and the angles at its two ends are 45°" />

The second set square is half of an equilateral triangle, a triangle with three equal sides. Take
the one with sides of length $$2$$. Its three angles are equal and add up to $$180^\circ$$, so each
is $$60^\circ$$. Fold it from the top corner down to the middle of the base: the halves match, so
the fold meets the base square on, splits the base into $$1$$ and $$1$$, and splits the top angle
into $$30^\circ$$ and $$30^\circ$$. The height $$h$$ of the fold has $$h^2 + 1^2 = 2^2$$, so
$$h^2 = 3$$ and $$h = \sqrt{3}$$.

<HalfEquilateral caption="Half of an equilateral triangle of side 2: the sides are 1, √3 and 2, and the angles are 30°, 60° and 90°" />

These lengths and angles are read from the folds in the pictures. The proofs later in the guide
check every value they need from the definitions, without the folds.

## Set squares in the unit circle

The point on the unit circle after a turn of $$x$$ radians from $$E_1 = (1, 0)$$ is
$$(\cos x, \sin x)$$ (:ref{#sine-cosine}): cosine is how far across, sine is how far up. So to read
sine and cosine off a set square, put its corner at the centre $$O$$ with one short side along the
positive $$x$$-axis and the triangle above it. Its far corner should land on the unit circle, so the
long side must have length $$1$$.

The half square has long side $$\sqrt{2}$$, so shrink it by the factor $$\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$$. A
zoom keeps every angle (rule 3 of :ref{#area-and-scaling/zoom-rules}), so the angles stay
$$45^\circ$$, $$45^\circ$$ and $$90^\circ$$, and the legs become $$\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$$ each. With
the $$45^\circ$$ corner at $$O$$, the far corner is $$\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$$ across and
$$\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$$ up, at the point $$\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}, \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)$$. A
turn of $$45^\circ$$ is a turn of $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$ radians (:ref{#radian}), so the picture says
$$\cos\frac{\pi}{4} = \sin\frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$$, about $$0.71$$.

The half equilateral triangle has long side $$2$$, so halve it. Its sides become $$\frac{1}{2}$$,
$$\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$$ and $$1$$. With the $$60^\circ$$ corner at $$O$$, the short side
$$\frac{1}{2}$$ lies along the $$x$$-axis and the far corner is at
$$\left(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)$$. With the $$30^\circ$$ corner at $$O$$ instead, the
side $$\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$$ lies along the $$x$$-axis and the far corner is at
$$\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \frac{1}{2}\right)$$. So the picture says that a turn of
$$\frac{\pi}{3}$$ ($$60^\circ$$) lands at $$\left(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)$$, and a
turn of $$\frac{\pi}{6}$$ ($$30^\circ$$) lands at $$\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \frac{1}{2}\right)$$.

The pictures are convincing, but they place each set square by eye. The rest of the guide proves
that the three points are exactly where a turn of $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$ ($$45^\circ$$),
$$\frac{\pi}{3}$$ ($$60^\circ$$) or $$\frac{\pi}{6}$$ ($$30^\circ$$) sends $$E_1$$.

## From an angle to a turn

A set square tells you an angle at $$O$$, between the ray along the $$x$$-axis and the ray to the
far corner. The definition of sine and cosine needs a turn instead. For a point above the $$x$$-axis
the two agree, because the turn from $$E_1$$ to it goes anticlockwise less than a half turn, and
sweeps out exactly that angle. Below the axis they disagree: the point $$(0, -1)$$ makes an angle of
$$90^\circ$$ with the ray $$OE_1$$, but the anticlockwise turn to it is $$270^\circ$$.

Angles here are measured as in earlier chapters, by their share of a disc
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/degree}), and that is taken as given rather than worked out from
formulas. The lemma below links those angles to turns, for points above the $$x$$-axis.

:::lemma[From an angle to a turn]{#special-angles-angle-to-turn}

Let $$U$$ be a point of the unit circle above the $$x$$-axis, and suppose the angle $$\angle E_1OU$$
measures $$\theta^\circ$$. Then

```math
U = (\cos x, \sin x) \qquad\text{with}\qquad x = \frac{\pi\theta}{180}
```

the angle $$\theta^\circ$$ written in radians.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_circle"}

By part 2 of :ref{#unit-circle-point}, there is exactly one number $$x$$ with $$0 \le x < 2\pi$$ and
$$U = (\cos x, \sin x)$$, that is, $$U = G_x(E_1)$$. The turn by $$x$$ radians is the turn by
$$w^\circ$$, where $$w = \frac{180x}{\pi}$$ (:ref{#radians-turn-notation}), and $$0 \le w < 360$$.

**Two choices.** By the definition of a turn (:ref{#isometries/rotation}), the angle turning
anticlockwise from the ray $$OE_1$$ to the ray $$OU$$ measures $$w^\circ$$. The two rays make two
angles, which together fill the disc, so their sizes add up to $$360^\circ$$
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/degree}). The angle $$\angle E_1OU$$ is the one that is at most a
straight angle (:ref{#distance-and-angles/angle-name}), so the other is $$(360 - \theta)^\circ$$.
The anticlockwise angle is one of the two, so $$w = \theta$$ or $$w = 360 - \theta$$.

**Ruling one out.** The point $$U$$ is not on the $$x$$-axis, so $$\theta$$ is neither $$0$$ nor
$$180$$, and $$0 < \theta < 180$$. If $$w = 360 - \theta$$, then $$180 < w < 360$$, so
$$\pi < x < 2\pi$$. For those $$x$$ the point $$(\cos x, \sin x)$$ lies below the $$x$$-axis:
$$\sin x < 0$$ in the third and fourth quadrants (:ref{#quadrant-signs}), and
$$\sin\frac{3\pi}{2} = -1$$ (:ref{#quarter-values}). But $$U$$ is above the $$x$$-axis, so
$$\sin x > 0$$. So $$w = \theta$$, and $$x = \frac{\pi w}{180} = \frac{\pi\theta}{180}$$.

:::

So for a point above the $$x$$-axis, you can find its turn by measuring one angle at $$O$$. The next
two sections find that angle for each set square's corner, using moves that keep angles.

## Half a right angle

Flip the plane in the line $$y = x$$. The positive $$x$$-axis and the positive $$y$$-axis swap
places, and every point of the mirror stays where it is. The corner of the half square,
$$U = \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}, \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)$$, is on the mirror, so the flip carries
the angle from the $$x$$-axis to $$U$$ onto the angle from the $$y$$-axis to $$U$$. The two angles
are equal and together make a right angle, so each is half a right angle.

First check that $$U$$ is on the unit circle. Its length squared is
$$\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2} = 1$$, so $$|U| = 1$$ (:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/norm}). The
number $$\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$$ is also written $$\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$$: multiply the top and bottom by
$$\sqrt{2}$$, and the bottom becomes $$\sqrt{2} \cdot \sqrt{2} = 2$$.

:::proposition[Sine and cosine of half a right angle]{#sin-cos-45}

```math
\cos\frac{\pi}{4} = \sin\frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Sine_of_45_Degrees"}

Let $$U = \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}, \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)$$ and $$E_2 = (0, 1)$$. Then
$$|U| = 1$$, as worked out above, and $$U$$ is above the $$x$$-axis.

**The flip keeps the angle.** Let $$S(x, y) = (y, x)$$, the flip in the line $$y = x$$
(:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/diagonal-reflection}). Flips are isometries
(:ref{#isometries/basic-isometries}). The flip gives

```math
S(O) = O \qquad S(E_1) = E_2 \qquad S(U) = U
```

So by :ref{#isometries/isometry-angles}, $$S$$ sends the angle at $$O$$ between the rays $$OE_1$$
and $$OU$$ to the angle at $$O$$ between the rays $$OE_2$$ and $$OU$$, of the same size:

```math
\angle E_1OU = \angle E_2OU
```

**The two angles make a right angle.** The angle $$\angle E_1OE_2$$ is a right angle, by part 2 of
:ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/perpendicular-dot}, since
$$E_1 \cdot E_2 = 1 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 1 = 0$$ and $$O$$, $$E_1$$ and $$E_2$$ are not on one line.
The point $$U$$ has both coordinates positive, so the ray $$OU$$ lies inside $$\angle E_1OE_2$$,
between the two positive axes; this is read from the picture. By
:ref{#distance-and-angles/angle-addition},

```math
\angle E_1OU + \angle UOE_2 = 90^\circ
```

The two angles are equal, so $$2\,\angle E_1OU = 90^\circ$$, and $$\angle E_1OU = 45^\circ$$.

**From the angle to the turn.** By :ref{#special-angles-angle-to-turn} with $$\theta = 45$$,
$$U = (\cos x, \sin x)$$ with $$x = \frac{45\pi}{180} = \frac{\pi}{4}$$. Comparing coordinates,
$$\cos\frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$$ and $$\sin\frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$$.

:::

As a check, :ref{#pythagorean-identity} says $$\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x = 1$$ for every $$x$$. At
$$x = \frac{\pi}{4}$$ ($$45^\circ$$) it gives
$$\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2} = 1$$.

## A third and two thirds of a right angle

Take the point $$Q = \left(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)$$. It is $$1$$ from $$O$$, and it
is also $$1$$ from $$E_1$$, so $$O$$, $$E_1$$ and $$Q$$ are the corners of an equilateral triangle
with sides $$1$$. That makes the angle at $$O$$ a third of a straight angle, $$60^\circ$$. Then the
flip in $$y = x$$ moves $$Q$$ to $$V = \left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \frac{1}{2}\right)$$, and carries
the $$30^\circ$$ left over between $$OQ$$ and the $$y$$-axis into the angle between the $$x$$-axis
and $$OV$$.


:::proposition[Sine and cosine of a third and two thirds of a right angle]{#sin-cos-30-60}

```math
\cos\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{1}{2} \qquad \sin\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \qquad \cos\frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \qquad \sin\frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{1}{2}
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_right_triangle#30-60-90_triangle"}

Let $$Q = \left(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)$$ and $$E_2 = (0, 1)$$.

**Three equal sides.** By :ref{#coordinates/distance-formula},

```math
\begin{aligned}
d(O, E_1)^2 &= 1^2 + 0^2 = 1 \\
d(O, Q)^2 &= \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^2 + \left(\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)^2 = \tfrac{1}{4} + \tfrac{3}{4} = 1 \\
d(E_1, Q)^2 &= \left(\tfrac{1}{2} - 1\right)^2 + \left(\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)^2 = \tfrac{1}{4} + \tfrac{3}{4} = 1
\end{aligned}
```

So all three distances are $$1$$. The point $$Q$$ is not on the $$x$$-axis, which is the line
through $$O$$ and $$E_1$$, so $$O$$, $$E_1$$ and $$Q$$ are the corners of a triangle.

**Three equal angles.** Since $$d(O, E_1) = d(O, Q)$$, the triangle is isosceles with apex $$O$$,
and by :ref{#isometries/isosceles}, $$\angle OE_1Q = \angle OQE_1$$. Since
$$d(E_1, O) = d(E_1, Q)$$, it is also isosceles with apex $$E_1$$, so
$$\angle E_1OQ = \angle E_1QO$$. So all three angles are equal. They add up to $$180^\circ$$
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/triangle-angle-sum}), so each is $$60^\circ$$. In particular
$$\angle E_1OQ = 60^\circ$$.

**The turn for Q.** The point $$Q$$ is on the unit circle and above the $$x$$-axis. By
:ref{#special-angles-angle-to-turn} with $$\theta = 60$$, $$Q = (\cos x, \sin x)$$ with
$$x = \frac{60\pi}{180} = \frac{\pi}{3}$$. So $$\cos\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{1}{2}$$ and
$$\sin\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$$.

**The angle left over.** As in the proof of :ref{#sin-cos-45}, $$\angle E_1OE_2 = 90^\circ$$. Both
coordinates of $$Q$$ are positive, so the ray $$OQ$$ lies inside $$\angle E_1OE_2$$; this is read
from the picture. By :ref{#distance-and-angles/angle-addition},
$$\angle E_1OQ + \angle QOE_2 = 90^\circ$$, so $$\angle QOE_2 = 90^\circ - 60^\circ = 30^\circ$$.

**The flip.** Let $$S(x, y) = (y, x)$$, the flip in $$y = x$$
(:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/diagonal-reflection}), and let
$$V = S(Q) = \left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \frac{1}{2}\right)$$. The flip is an isometry
(:ref{#isometries/basic-isometries}) with $$S(O) = O$$ and $$S(E_2) = E_1$$. By
:ref{#isometries/isometry-angles}, it sends the angle at $$O$$ between the rays $$OE_2$$ and $$OQ$$
to the angle at $$O$$ between the rays $$OE_1$$ and $$OV$$, of the same size. So
$$\angle E_1OV = \angle E_2OQ = 30^\circ$$.

**The turn for V.** The flip keeps the distance from $$O = S(O)$$, so $$|V| = |Q| = 1$$, and $$V$$
is above the $$x$$-axis. By :ref{#special-angles-angle-to-turn} with $$\theta = 30$$,
$$V = (\cos x, \sin x)$$ with $$x = \frac{30\pi}{180} = \frac{\pi}{6}$$. So
$$\cos\frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$$ and $$\sin\frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{1}{2}$$.

:::

As a check with :ref{#pythagorean-identity}, at $$x = \frac{\pi}{3}$$ ($$60^\circ$$) the squares add
up to $$\frac{1}{4} + \frac{3}{4} = 1$$, and at $$x = \frac{\pi}{6}$$ ($$30^\circ$$) they are the
same two squares in the other order.

The values for $$\frac{\pi}{6}$$ ($$30^\circ$$) are the values for $$\frac{\pi}{3}$$ ($$60^\circ$$)
swapped round, and the reason is the flip in $$y = x$$, which swaps the two coordinates. The two
angles add up to a right angle, and the guide [Identities you can see](./symmetries.mdx) shows that
the same swap happens for any two angles that add up to a right angle
(:ref{#complement-identities}).

## The table of special angles

Turning from $$E_1$$ towards $$E_2 = (0, 1)$$, the point on the unit circle climbs and moves in
towards the $$y$$-axis. So the sine grows from $$0$$ to $$1$$ while the cosine shrinks from $$1$$ to
$$0$$. The values from :ref{#sin-cos-45} and :ref{#sin-cos-30-60}, with those at $$0$$ and
$$\frac{\pi}{2}$$ ($$90^\circ$$) from :ref{#quarter-values}, fill in the steps between.

| Angle $$x$$       | Degrees      | $$\sin x$$             | $$\cos x$$             |
| ----------------- | ------------ | ---------------------- | ---------------------- |
| $$0$$             | $$0^\circ$$  | $$0$$                  | $$1$$                  |
| $$\frac{\pi}{6}$$ | $$30^\circ$$ | $$\frac{1}{2}$$        | $$\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$$ |
| $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$ | $$45^\circ$$ | $$\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$$ | $$\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$$ |
| $$\frac{\pi}{3}$$ | $$60^\circ$$ | $$\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$$ | $$\frac{1}{2}$$        |
| $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$ | $$90^\circ$$ | $$1$$                  | $$0$$                  |

A pattern helps you rebuild the sine column. Write each value with a square root on top and $$2$$
underneath:

```math
\frac{\sqrt{0}}{2}, \quad \frac{\sqrt{1}}{2}, \quad \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \quad \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \quad \frac{\sqrt{4}}{2}
```

These are $$0$$, $$\frac{1}{2}$$, $$\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$$, $$\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$$ and $$1$$, the
sines of the five angles in order. The cosine column is the same list read backwards. This is a
memory aid that happens to work for these five angles. The reason for the values is the two set
squares, and the pattern doesn't continue to other angles.

In decimals, $$\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \approx 0.707$$ and $$\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \approx 0.866$$. A
calculator in radian mode agrees: it gives $$\sin\frac{\pi}{4} \approx 0.7071$$ for $$45^\circ$$ and
$$\cos\frac{\pi}{6} \approx 0.8660$$ for $$30^\circ$$. The calculator shows rounded decimals, while
the table gives the exact numbers.

The table covers the first quarter of the circle. The guide
[Identities you can see](./symmetries.mdx) uses flips and turns to carry it round the rest, to
angles such as $$\frac{2\pi}{3}$$ ($$120^\circ$$) and $$\frac{5\pi}{4}$$ ($$225^\circ$$). The next
guide, [Sine and cosine in right triangles](./right-triangles.mdx), uses the same values to find
lengths in right triangles.

## A Ferris wheel car

Picture a Ferris wheel of radius $$20$$ m turning anticlockwise as you look at it. Put the hub at
$$O$$, and start with your car level with the hub on the right, at $$(20, 0)$$, measured in metres.
After the wheel has turned by $$x$$, your car is at $$20(\cos x, \sin x)$$, by
:ref{#circle-point-radius}: $$20\cos x$$ metres across from the hub and $$20\sin x$$ metres above
it.

:::example[Thirty degrees round the wheel]

After a turn of $$\frac{\pi}{6}$$ ($$30^\circ$$), the car is at

```math
20\left(\cos\frac{\pi}{6}, \sin\frac{\pi}{6}\right) = 20\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \frac{1}{2}\right) = \left(10\sqrt{3}, 10\right)
```

So the car is $$10$$ m above the hub and $$10\sqrt{3} \approx 17.3$$ m across from it. After a turn
of $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$ ($$45^\circ$$), it is $$\frac{20}{\sqrt{2}} = 10\sqrt{2} \approx 14.1$$ m
across and the same distance up. After $$\frac{\pi}{3}$$ ($$60^\circ$$), it is $$10$$ m across and
$$10\sqrt{3} \approx 17.3$$ m up.

:::

Doubling the turn from $$\frac{\pi}{6}$$ to $$\frac{\pi}{3}$$ ($$30^\circ$$ to $$60^\circ$$) takes
the car from $$10$$ m up to about $$17.3$$ m up, not to $$20$$ m. That is a slip worth naming: the
sine of twice an angle is not twice its sine. The car climbs more slowly as it nears the top, and
its height can never go past the radius of $$20$$ m.

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on special angles, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-special-angles-values}

Work out each number exactly, without a calculator. Recall that $$\frac{\pi}{6}$$, $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$
and $$\frac{\pi}{3}$$ are $$30^\circ$$, $$45^\circ$$ and $$60^\circ$$.

1. $$\sin\frac{\pi}{6} + \cos\frac{\pi}{3}$$
2. $$\sin\frac{\pi}{4}\cos\frac{\pi}{4}$$
3. $$\cos^2\frac{\pi}{6} - \sin^2\frac{\pi}{6}$$

:::solution

From the table of special angles:

1. $$\sin\frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{1}{2}$$ and $$\cos\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{1}{2}$$, so the sum is $$1$$.
2. Both are $$\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$$, so the product is $$\frac{2}{4} = \frac{1}{2}$$.
3. $$\cos^2\frac{\pi}{6} = \left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{3}{4}$$ and
   $$\sin^2\frac{\pi}{6} = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{4}$$, so the difference is
   $$\frac{1}{2}$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-special-angles-clock}

The minute hand of a wall clock is $$12$$ cm long. Put the centre of the clock at $$O$$, with the
positive $$x$$-axis pointing at the $$3$$ and the positive $$y$$-axis at the $$12$$. Find exactly
where the tip of the hand is at $$5$$ past, $$7\frac{1}{2}$$ minutes past and $$10$$ past the hour,
and give each coordinate to two decimal places.

:::solution

Each minute the hand moves $$\frac{360^\circ}{60} = 6^\circ$$ clockwise. At $$15$$ past it points at
the $$3$$, so at $$m$$ minutes past, for $$m$$ from $$0$$ to $$15$$, it is $$6(15 - m)$$ degrees
anticlockwise from the positive $$x$$-axis. By :ref{#circle-point-radius}, the tip is at
$$12(\cos x, \sin x)$$ with $$x$$ that angle in radians.

- **5 past**: $$60^\circ = \frac{\pi}{3}$$, so the tip is at
  $$12\left(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right) = (6, 6\sqrt{3}) \approx (6.00, 10.39)$$
- **7½ past**: $$45^\circ = \frac{\pi}{4}$$, so the tip is at
  $$12\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right) = (6\sqrt{2}, 6\sqrt{2}) \approx (8.49, 8.49)$$
- **10 past**: $$30^\circ = \frac{\pi}{6}$$, so the tip is at
  $$12\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \frac{1}{2}\right) = (6\sqrt{3}, 6) \approx (10.39, 6.00)$$

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-special-angles-radius-four}

Find exactly the points of the circle with centre $$O$$ and radius $$4$$ on the rays at angles
$$\frac{\pi}{6}$$, $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$ and $$\frac{\pi}{3}$$ ($$30^\circ$$, $$45^\circ$$ and
$$60^\circ$$) anticlockwise from the positive $$x$$-axis. Check with the distance formula that each
is $$4$$ from $$O$$.

:::solution

By :ref{#circle-point-radius} the points are $$4(\cos x, \sin x)$$:

- $$x = \frac{\pi}{6}$$: $$4\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \frac{1}{2}\right) = (2\sqrt{3}, 2)$$, and
  $$(2\sqrt{3})^2 + 2^2 = 12 + 4 = 16$$
- $$x = \frac{\pi}{4}$$:
  $$4\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right) = (2\sqrt{2}, 2\sqrt{2})$$, and
  $$8 + 8 = 16$$
- $$x = \frac{\pi}{3}$$: $$4\left(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right) = (2, 2\sqrt{3})$$, and
  $$4 + 12 = 16$$

Each squared distance is $$16 = 4^2$$, so each point is $$4$$ from $$O$$
(:ref{#coordinates/distance-formula}).

:::

::::exercise{#ex-special-angles-doubling}

1. Compare $$\sin\frac{\pi}{3}$$ ($$60^\circ$$) with $$2\sin\frac{\pi}{6}$$ ($$30^\circ$$), and
   $$\sin\frac{\pi}{2}$$ ($$90^\circ$$) with $$2\sin\frac{\pi}{4}$$ ($$45^\circ$$).
2. Explain, using the unit circle, why $$\sin 2x = 2\sin x$$ can't hold for every $$x$$.

:::solution

1. $$\sin\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \approx 0.866$$, but
   $$2\sin\frac{\pi}{6} = 2 \cdot \frac{1}{2} = 1$$. And $$\sin\frac{\pi}{2} = 1$$
   (:ref{#quarter-values}), but $$2\sin\frac{\pi}{4} = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.414$$. Neither pair is
   equal.
2. The sine of any number is the height of a point of the unit circle, so it is never more than
   $$1$$ (:ref{#sine-and-cosine-bounds}). If $$\sin 2x = 2\sin x$$ held for every $$x$$, then at
   $$x = \frac{\pi}{4}$$ it would give $$\sin\frac{\pi}{2} = \sqrt{2}$$, which is more than $$1$$.
   So doubling the angle does not double the sine.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-special-angles-from-one-value}

1. Suppose you remember only that $$\cos\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{1}{2}$$, for $$60^\circ$$. Use
   :ref{#pythagorean-identity} to find $$\sin\frac{\pi}{3}$$, and say how you choose the sign.
2. Suppose you remember only that $$\cos\frac{\pi}{4} = \sin\frac{\pi}{4}$$, from the flip in
   $$y = x$$. Find their value in the same way.

:::solution

1. By :ref{#pythagorean-identity}, $$\sin^2\frac{\pi}{3} = 1 - \frac{1}{4} = \frac{3}{4}$$, so
   $$\sin\frac{\pi}{3}$$ is $$\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$$ or $$-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$$. The angle
   $$\frac{\pi}{3}$$ is between $$0$$ and $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$, so the point is in the first quadrant
   and its sine is positive (:ref{#quadrant-signs}). So $$\sin\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$$.
2. Write $$c$$ for the common value. By :ref{#pythagorean-identity}, $$c^2 + c^2 = 1$$, so
   $$c^2 = \frac{1}{2}$$, and $$c$$ is $$\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$$ or $$-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$$. Again
   $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$ is in the first quadrant, so $$c > 0$$ and $$c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-special-angles-ray}

Let $$P = (3, \sqrt{3})$$. Write $$P$$ as $$r(\cos x, \sin x)$$ with $$r > 0$$ and
$$0 \le x < 2\pi$$ ($$360^\circ$$), and explain why there is only one such $$x$$.

:::solution

The length of $$P$$ is $$|P| = \sqrt{9 + 3} = \sqrt{12} = 2\sqrt{3}$$
(:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/norm}). By :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/direction},
$$P = 2\sqrt{3}\,U$$ with the unit point

```math
U = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{3}}(3, \sqrt{3}) = \left(\frac{3}{2\sqrt{3}}, \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2\sqrt{3}}\right) = \left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \frac{1}{2}\right)
```

using $$\frac{3}{\sqrt{3}} = \sqrt{3}$$. By :ref{#sin-cos-30-60}, the turn of $$\frac{\pi}{6}$$
($$30^\circ$$) lands at this point: $$U = \left(\cos\frac{\pi}{6}, \sin\frac{\pi}{6}\right)$$. So

```math
P = 2\sqrt{3}\left(\cos\frac{\pi}{6}, \sin\frac{\pi}{6}\right)
```

with $$r = 2\sqrt{3}$$ and $$x = \frac{\pi}{6}$$. If also $$P = s(\cos y, \sin y)$$ with $$s > 0$$,
then $$s = |P|$$, since $$(\cos y, \sin y)$$ has length $$1$$
(:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/norm-scale}). So $$(\cos y, \sin y) = \frac{1}{|P|}P = U$$. By part
2 of :ref{#unit-circle-point}, only one $$y$$ with $$0 \le y < 2\pi$$ gives $$U$$, so
$$y = \frac{\pi}{6}$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-special-angles-twelve-sides}

Take the regular $$12$$-sided polygon in the unit circle (:ref{#area-and-scaling/inscribed-polygon})
with corners at $$E_1$$ and $$V = \left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \frac{1}{2}\right)$$.

1. Show that the side from $$E_1$$ to $$V$$ has length $$\sqrt{2 - \sqrt{3}}$$.
2. Every side has the same length. Find the perimeter divided by the diameter to four decimal
   places, and compare it with the table in the guide on the circumference of a circle.

:::solution

1. By :ref{#coordinates/distance-formula},

   ```math
   d(E_1, V)^2 = \left(1 - \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^2 = 1 - \sqrt{3} + \frac{3}{4} + \frac{1}{4} = 2 - \sqrt{3}
   ```

   So $$d(E_1, V) = \sqrt{2 - \sqrt{3}}$$. The two corners are neighbours, since the angle
   $$\angle E_1OV$$ is $$30^\circ = \frac{360^\circ}{12}$$, as shown in the proof of
   :ref{#sin-cos-30-60}.

2. The perimeter is $$12\sqrt{2 - \sqrt{3}}$$ and the diameter is $$2$$, so the ratio is
   $$6\sqrt{2 - \sqrt{3}} \approx 6 \times 0.517638 \approx 3.1058$$. That is the value for
   $$n = 12$$ in the table after :ref{#area-and-scaling/circumference-formula}, a little less than
   $$\pi \approx 3.1416$$.

:::
