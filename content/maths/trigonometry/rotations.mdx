Imagine the radar screen on a ship's bridge at night. A bright line sweeps round from the centre
like the hand of a clock, and each boat nearby shows up as a glowing dot as the line passes over it.
The radar never works out how far east or north a boat is. It knows how far away the boat is, and
which way the line was pointing when the dot lit up.

Those two numbers, a distance and a direction, pin down a point as well as "across and up" do. They
are called **polar coordinates**, and they suit turning. When a turntable spins, every point on the
record keeps its distance from the spindle and adds the same angle to its direction. In this guide
we'll describe points by distance and direction, and use them to find the formula for a turn by any
angle.

## Distance and direction

Stand at the origin and face along the positive $$x$$-axis. To reach any other point, you can turn
until you face it and then walk straight to it. The turn is an angle and the walk is a distance. By
:ref{#circle-point-radius}, turning through $$\theta$$ and walking $$r$$ brings you to the point
$$(r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta)$$.

For example, turn through $$\frac{\pi}{6}$$ and walk $$4$$. The point on the unit circle in that
direction is $$(\cos\frac{\pi}{6}, \sin\frac{\pi}{6}) = (\frac{\sqrt3}{2}, \frac12)$$
(:ref{#sin-cos-30-60}). Walking $$4$$ times as far lands at $$(2\sqrt3, 2)$$, which is about
$$(3.46, 2)$$.

```plane

point Y2 0 4.5 hidden
point O 0 0 sw
point E 4 0 hidden

caption "Turning through π/6 points at U = (cos π/6, sin π/6) on the unit circle. Walking 4 along that ray reaches P = 4U = (2√3, 2)."
```

:::definition[Polar coordinates]{#polar-coordinates}

Let $$P$$ be a point. Two numbers $$r$$ and $$\theta$$, with $$r \ge 0$$, are **polar coordinates**
for $$P$$ when

```math
P = (r\cos\theta,\ r\sin\theta)
```

The number $$r$$ is the **distance** and $$\theta$$ is the **angle**. To tell them apart from polar
coordinates, the usual coordinates $$(x, y)$$ are called **Cartesian coordinates**.

:::

The distance $$r$$ is always the length of $$P$$, its distance from $$O$$
(:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/norm}). The point $$(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$$ has length $$1$$, by
:ref{#pythagorean-identity}, so by :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/norm-scale}

```math
|P| = |r(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)| = |r| \cdot 1 = r
```

The angle is not fixed in the same way. Sine and cosine repeat every $$2\pi$$ (:ref{#periodic}), so
the angles $$\frac{\pi}{6}$$, $$\frac{13\pi}{6}$$ and $$-\frac{11\pi}{6}$$ all go with the distance
$$4$$ to give $$(2\sqrt3, 2)$$. At the origin the angle can be anything at all, since $$r = 0$$
makes both coordinates $$0$$.

A pair of numbers on its own doesn't say which kind of coordinates it is. The polar coordinates
$$4, \frac{\pi}{6}$$ give the point $$(2\sqrt3, 2)$$, but the Cartesian point $$(4, \frac{\pi}{6})$$
is about $$(4, 0.52)$$, somewhere else entirely. So in this guide we always say "polar" when we mean
it, and write a point in brackets only for its Cartesian coordinates.

:::proposition[Every point has polar coordinates]{#polar-exist}

Every point $$P$$ has polar coordinates. If $$P \ne O$$, the distance is $$r = |P|$$, and exactly
one angle $$\theta$$ with $$0 \le \theta < 2\pi$$ works. Every other angle that works is
$$\theta + 2\pi n$$ for an integer $$n$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_coordinate_system#Converting_between_polar_and_Cartesian_coordinates"}

**The origin.** The distance $$r = 0$$ works with any angle, since
$$(0 \cdot \cos\theta, 0 \cdot \sin\theta) = (0, 0)$$.

**Other points.** Let $$P \ne O$$. By :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/direction}, $$P = |P|\,U$$,
where $$U = \frac{1}{|P|}P$$ is a unit point. By :ref{#unit-circle-point},
$$U = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$$ for exactly one $$\theta$$ with $$0 \le \theta < 2\pi$$. Then, by
:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/scalar-multiple},

```math
P = |P|\,(\cos\theta, \sin\theta) = \big(|P|\cos\theta,\ |P|\sin\theta\big)
```

So $$r = |P|$$ and this $$\theta$$ are polar coordinates for $$P$$.

**No other angles.** Suppose $$r$$ and $$\theta'$$ are polar coordinates for $$P$$. The distance is
$$r = |P| > 0$$, as shown after :ref{#polar-coordinates}, so
$$(\cos\theta', \sin\theta') = \frac{1}{|P|}P = U$$. Let $$n$$ be the largest integer with
$$2\pi n \le \theta'$$, and write $$w = \theta' - 2\pi n$$, so that $$0 \le w < 2\pi$$. By
:ref{#periodic}, $$(\cos w, \sin w) = (\cos\theta', \sin\theta') = U$$, so $$w = \theta$$ by the
"exactly one" of :ref{#unit-circle-point}. That is, $$\theta' = \theta + 2\pi n$$.

:::

## Converting between the two

Going from polar coordinates to Cartesian ones means putting the numbers into $$r\cos\theta$$ and
$$r\sin\theta$$. For example, the polar coordinates $$3, \frac{3\pi}{4}$$ give

```math
\Big(3\cos\tfrac{3\pi}{4},\ 3\sin\tfrac{3\pi}{4}\Big) = \Big({-\tfrac{3\sqrt2}{2}},\ \tfrac{3\sqrt2}{2}\Big) \approx (-2.12,\ 2.12)
```

using the values at $$\frac{3\pi}{4}$$ from [Identities you can see](./symmetries.mdx).

Going back takes three steps. The distance comes from the distance formula. Dividing the point by
its distance gives the point on the unit circle in the same direction, and that point is
$$(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$$. The last step is to recognise which angle it belongs to:

1. Find $$r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$$.
2. Find the unit point $$\big(\frac{x}{r}, \frac{y}{r}\big)$$, which is
   $$(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$$.
3. Find $$\theta$$ from the special angles, using the signs to pick the quadrant
   (:ref{#quadrant-signs}).

:::example[The point (2, 2)]

The distance is $$r = \sqrt{4 + 4} = \sqrt8 = 2\sqrt2$$. The unit point is

```math
\Big(\frac{2}{2\sqrt2},\ \frac{2}{2\sqrt2}\Big) = \Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt2},\ \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\Big)
```

which is $$(\cos\frac{\pi}{4}, \sin\frac{\pi}{4})$$ (:ref{#sin-cos-45}). So $$(2, 2)$$ has polar
coordinates $$2\sqrt2, \frac{\pi}{4}$$.

:::

:::example[The point (−3, 0)]

The distance is $$r = \sqrt{9 + 0} = 3$$, and the unit point is $$(-1, 0)$$. That is
$$(\cos\pi, \sin\pi)$$ (:ref{#quarter-values}), so $$(-3, 0)$$ has polar coordinates $$3, \pi$$.

:::

:::example[The point (1, −√3)]

The distance is $$r = \sqrt{1 + 3} = 2$$, and the unit point is $$(\frac12, -\frac{\sqrt3}{2})$$.
Its first coordinate is positive and its second is negative, so the direction is in the fourth
quadrant, below the positive $$x$$-axis. The acute angle with cosine $$\frac12$$ is
$$\frac{\pi}{3}$$, so the direction is $$\frac{\pi}{3}$$ below the axis:
$$\theta = -\frac{\pi}{3}$$.

As a check, :ref{#negative-angle} gives $$\cos(-\frac{\pi}{3}) = \cos\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac12$$ and
$$\sin(-\frac{\pi}{3}) = -\sin\frac{\pi}{3} = -\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$. The angle between $$0$$ and
$$2\pi$$ is $$-\frac{\pi}{3} + 2\pi = \frac{5\pi}{3}$$, so the polar coordinates
$$2, \frac{5\pi}{3}$$ work too.

:::

### Finding the angle with a calculator

Most points don't sit at a special angle, and then a calculator finds $$\theta$$ with the
$$\tan^{-1}$$ button. The ratio $$\frac{y}{x}$$ is the slope of the line from $$O$$ through the
point, and that slope is $$\tan\theta$$ (:ref{#tangent-slope}). But the line through $$O$$ is made
of two rays pointing opposite ways, and both have the same slope.

That is where a common slip happens. Take $$(-1, -1)$$. Its slope is $$\frac{-1}{-1} = 1$$, and
$$\tan^{-1} 1 = \frac{\pi}{4}$$. But the angle $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$ points up and to the right, towards
$$(1, 1)$$, while $$(-1, -1)$$ is down and to the left. The calculator's answer is out by a half
turn, and the right angle is $$\frac{\pi}{4} + \pi = \frac{5\pi}{4}$$.

```plane
point L1 -2 -2 hidden
point L2 2 2 hidden
point O 0 0 se
point E 1.8 0 hidden
point A 1 1 se
point B -1 -1 nw
segment L1 L2 color=muted
ray O A color=blue
ray O B color=orange
angle E O A color=blue arrow r=0.6 label="π/4"
angle E O B color=orange arrow r=0.35
caption "The points (1, 1) and (−1, −1) lie on one line through O with slope 1. The angle π/4 points to (1, 1); the angle for (−1, −1) is π/4 + π = 5π/4."
```

The button $$\tan^{-1}$$ always answers with an angle between $$-\frac{\pi}{2}$$ and
$$\frac{\pi}{2}$$ (:ref{#inverse-tan}), which points into the right half of the plane, where
$$x > 0$$. So for a point on the left, add a half turn.

:::proposition[The angle from the tangent]{#rotations-polar-angle}

Let $$P = (x, y)$$ with $$x \ne 0$$, and let $$\theta_0 = \tan^{-1}\frac{y}{x}$$.

1. If $$x > 0$$, then $$\theta_0$$ is an angle for $$P$$.
2. If $$x < 0$$, then $$\theta_0 + \pi$$ is an angle for $$P$$.

If $$x = 0$$ and $$y \ne 0$$, the point is on the $$y$$-axis, and $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$ is an angle for
it when $$y > 0$$, and $$-\frac{\pi}{2}$$ when $$y < 0$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_coordinate_system#Converting_between_polar_and_Cartesian_coordinates"}

Since $$-\frac{\pi}{2} < \theta_0 < \frac{\pi}{2}$$, its cosine is positive. For $$\theta_0 > 0$$
this is :ref{#quadrant-signs}, for $$\theta_0 = 0$$ it is $$\cos 0 = 1$$ (:ref{#quarter-values}),
and for $$\theta_0 < 0$$ it follows with :ref{#negative-angle}. By :ref{#tangent} and the choice of
$$\theta_0$$, $$\frac{\sin\theta_0}{\cos\theta_0} = \frac{y}{x}$$, so

```math
\sin\theta_0 = \frac{y}{x}\cos\theta_0
```

**If $$x > 0$$.** Let $$r = \frac{x}{\cos\theta_0}$$, which is positive. Then $$r\cos\theta_0 = x$$,
and

```math
r\sin\theta_0 = \frac{x}{\cos\theta_0} \cdot \frac{y}{x}\cos\theta_0 = y
```

So $$r$$ and $$\theta_0$$ are polar coordinates for $$P$$.

**If $$x < 0$$.** Let $$r = -\frac{x}{\cos\theta_0}$$, which is positive. By
:ref{#half-turn-identities}, $$\cos(\theta_0 + \pi) = -\cos\theta_0$$ and
$$\sin(\theta_0 + \pi) = -\sin\theta_0$$. So

```math
\begin{aligned}
r\cos(\theta_0 + \pi) &= -\frac{x}{\cos\theta_0} \cdot (-\cos\theta_0) = x \\
r\sin(\theta_0 + \pi) &= -\frac{x}{\cos\theta_0} \cdot \Big({-\frac{y}{x}}\cos\theta_0\Big) = y
\end{aligned}
```

So $$r$$ and $$\theta_0 + \pi$$ are polar coordinates for $$P$$.

**If $$x = 0$$.** For $$y > 0$$, $$P = (0, y) = (y\cos\frac{\pi}{2}, y\sin\frac{\pi}{2})$$ by
:ref{#quarter-values}. For $$y < 0$$,
$$P = \big({-y}\cos(-\frac{\pi}{2}), -y\sin(-\frac{\pi}{2})\big)$$, since
$$\cos(-\frac{\pi}{2}) = 0$$ and $$\sin(-\frac{\pi}{2}) = -1$$ (:ref{#negative-angle}).

:::

:::example[The point (−4, 3)]

The distance is $$r = \sqrt{16 + 9} = 5$$. The slope is $$\frac{3}{-4} = -0.75$$, and a calculator
in radian mode gives $$\tan^{-1}(-0.75) \approx -0.644$$. That angle points down and to the right,
but $$(-4, 3)$$ is up and to the left, since $$x < 0$$. So

```math
\theta \approx -0.644 + \pi \approx 2.498
```

which is about $$143.1^\circ$$. As a check, $$5\cos 2.498 \approx -4.00$$ and
$$5\sin 2.498 \approx 3.00$$.

:::

Spreadsheets and most programming languages have a function, usually called `atan2`, that takes
$$y$$ and $$x$$ separately and makes this check for you.

## Turning in polar coordinates

Back to the turntable. A turn about $$O$$ keeps every point's distance from $$O$$ and adds the same
angle to its direction. In polar coordinates that is the whole story: the distance stays the same,
and the angle goes up by the size of the turn.

Take the point with polar coordinates $$4, \frac{\pi}{6}$$, which is $$(2\sqrt3, 2)$$. Turning it by
$$\frac{\pi}{3}$$ should give the polar coordinates $$4, \frac{\pi}{6} + \frac{\pi}{3}$$, that is
$$4, \frac{\pi}{2}$$. That is the point $$(0, 4)$$ at the top of the circle of radius $$4$$.

```plane
point X1 -1.5 0 hidden
point X2 5 0 hidden
point Y1 0 -1.5 hidden
point Y2 0 5 hidden
point O 0 0 sw
point E 4 0 hidden
point P 3.46 2 e
point P' 0 4 ne
segment X1 X2 color=muted
segment Y1 Y2 color=muted
circle O 4 color=muted dashed
segment O P color=blue
segment O P' color=orange
angle E O P color=blue r=1.2 label="π/6"
angle P O P' color=orange arrow r=1.9 label="π/3"
caption "Turning P, with polar coordinates 4 and π/6, by π/3 about O gives P' with polar coordinates 4 and π/2, the point (0, 4)"
```

:::theorem[Turning a point in polar coordinates]{#polar-turn}

For every $$r \ge 0$$ and all numbers $$\theta$$ and $$\varphi$$,

```math
G_\varphi(r\cos\theta,\ r\sin\theta) = \big(r\cos(\theta + \varphi),\ r\sin(\theta + \varphi)\big)
```

So the turn by $$\varphi$$ about $$O$$ sends the point with polar coordinates $$r, \theta$$ to the
point with polar coordinates $$r, \theta + \varphi$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_(mathematics)#In_Euclidean_geometry"}

**If $$r = 0$$**, both sides are $$O$$, and a turn about $$O$$ leaves $$O$$ where it is
(:ref{#isometries/rotation}).

**If $$r > 0$$**, follow the point step by step. Here $$E_1 = (1, 0)$$, and
$$(\cos\theta, \sin\theta) = G_\theta(E_1)$$ is the definition of cosine and sine
(:ref{#sine-cosine}):

```math
\begin{aligned}
G_\varphi(r\cos\theta,\ r\sin\theta) &= G_\varphi\big(r\,G_\theta(E_1)\big) && \text{taking out } r \text{, and } (\cos\theta, \sin\theta) = G_\theta(E_1) \\
&= r\,G_\varphi\big(G_\theta(E_1)\big) && \text{a turn about } O \text{ keeps rays} \\
&= r\,G_{\theta + \varphi}(E_1) && \text{turns add} \\
&= r\big(\cos(\theta + \varphi),\ \sin(\theta + \varphi)\big) && \text{the definition again} \\
&= \big(r\cos(\theta + \varphi),\ r\sin(\theta + \varphi)\big) && \text{multiplying in } r
\end{aligned}
```

The second step is :ref{#turn-scales}, with $$c = r$$, and the third is part 1 of
:ref{#radian-turns}. Taking out $$r$$ and multiplying it back in are
:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/scalar-multiple}.

:::

This is where defining sine and cosine by a turn pays off. A point in direction $$\theta$$ is
$$E_1$$ turned by $$\theta$$ and then zoomed by $$r$$, so turning it again by $$\varphi$$ adds the
two turns together.

## The formula for a turn

Now we can keep a promise from
[Moves as formulas](../adding-and-scaling-points/moves-as-formulas.mdx). That guide found the
quarter turn $$G_{90}(x, y) = (-y, x)$$ and said that turns by other angles need trigonometry. The
plan is to write the point in polar coordinates, turn it by adding to its angle, and then unpack the
cosine and sine of a sum with the [addition formulas](./addition-formulas.mdx).

Try it first with numbers. Turn $$(2\sqrt3, 2)$$ by $$\frac{\pi}{6}$$. In polar coordinates it is
$$4, \frac{\pi}{6}$$, so the turn gives $$4, \frac{\pi}{3}$$, which is
$$(4\cos\frac{\pi}{3}, 4\sin\frac{\pi}{3}) = (2, 2\sqrt3)$$. Now build the same answer from
$$x = 2\sqrt3$$, $$y = 2$$, $$\cos\frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$ and
$$\sin\frac{\pi}{6} = \frac12$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
x\cos\tfrac{\pi}{6} - y\sin\tfrac{\pi}{6} &= 2\sqrt3 \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} - 2 \cdot \tfrac12 = 3 - 1 = 2 \\
x\sin\tfrac{\pi}{6} + y\cos\tfrac{\pi}{6} &= 2\sqrt3 \cdot \tfrac12 + 2 \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} = \sqrt3 + \sqrt3 = 2\sqrt3
\end{aligned}
```

Both coordinates match. That pattern works for every turn.

:::theorem[The formula for a turn]{#rotation-formula}

For every number $$\varphi$$ and every point $$(x, y)$$,

```math
G_\varphi(x, y) = (x\cos\varphi - y\sin\varphi,\ x\sin\varphi + y\cos\varphi)
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_matrix#In_two_dimensions"}

By :ref{#polar-exist}, the point has polar coordinates: $$(x, y) = (r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta)$$ for
some $$r \ge 0$$ and some $$\theta$$. By :ref{#polar-turn},

```math
G_\varphi(x, y) = \big(r\cos(\theta + \varphi),\ r\sin(\theta + \varphi)\big)
```

**The first coordinate.** This uses the addition formula for cosine (:ref{#addition-formulas}).

```math
\begin{aligned}
r\cos(\theta + \varphi) &= r(\cos\theta\cos\varphi - \sin\theta\sin\varphi) && \text{the addition formula for cosine} \\
&= (r\cos\theta)\cos\varphi - (r\sin\theta)\sin\varphi && \text{multiplying out the bracket} \\
&= x\cos\varphi - y\sin\varphi && \text{since } x = r\cos\theta \text{ and } y = r\sin\theta
\end{aligned}
```

**The second coordinate.** This uses the addition formula for sine.

```math
\begin{aligned}
r\sin(\theta + \varphi) &= r(\sin\theta\cos\varphi + \cos\theta\sin\varphi) && \text{the addition formula for sine} \\
&= (r\sin\theta)\cos\varphi + (r\cos\theta)\sin\varphi && \text{multiplying out the bracket} \\
&= y\cos\varphi + x\sin\varphi && \text{since } y = r\sin\theta \text{ and } x = r\cos\theta
\end{aligned}
```

The last line is $$x\sin\varphi + y\cos\varphi$$, with the two terms in the other order.

:::

Two turns we already know make good checks. For $$\varphi = \frac{\pi}{2}$$, the cosine is $$0$$ and
the sine is $$1$$ (:ref{#quarter-values}), and the formula gives
$$(x \cdot 0 - y \cdot 1, x \cdot 1 + y \cdot 0) = (-y, x)$$. That is the formula $$G_{90}$$ of
:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/quarter-turn}, which part 4 of :ref{#radian-turns} found is the
quarter turn $$G_{\pi/2}$$. For $$\varphi = \pi$$, the cosine is $$-1$$ and the sine is $$0$$, and
the formula gives $$(-x, -y)$$, the half turn $$H_O = G_\pi$$ (part 5 of :ref{#radian-turns}).

Notice that each new coordinate uses both old ones. It's tempting to write the turned point as
$$(x\cos\varphi, y\sin\varphi)$$, but that can't be right. For $$\varphi = 0$$ it would send every
point $$(x, y)$$ to $$(x, 0)$$, when a turn by $$0$$ leaves every point where it is.

:::example[Turning (4, 1) by π/6]

With $$\cos\frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$ and $$\sin\frac{\pi}{6} = \frac12$$, the formula gives

```math
\begin{aligned}
x' &= 4 \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} - 1 \cdot \tfrac12 = 2\sqrt3 - \tfrac12 \approx 2.96 \\
y' &= 4 \cdot \tfrac12 + 1 \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} = 2 + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} \approx 2.87
\end{aligned}
```

A turn about $$O$$ keeps the distance from $$O$$, and the numbers agree. Before the turn,
$$4^2 + 1^2 = 17$$. After it, the two $$2\sqrt3$$ terms cancel:

```math
\Big(2\sqrt3 - \tfrac12\Big)^2 + \Big(2 + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\Big)^2 = \Big(12 - 2\sqrt3 + \tfrac14\Big) + \Big(4 + 2\sqrt3 + \tfrac34\Big) = 17
```

:::

```plane

point X2 5 0 hidden
point Y1 0 -1 hidden
point Y2 0 4.5 hidden
point O 0 0 sw
point P 4 1 e
point P' 2.96 2.87 ne

segment Y1 Y2 color=muted

caption "The turn by π/6 about O sends P = (4, 1) to P' = (2√3 − 1/2, 2 + √3/2) ≈ (2.96, 2.87), on the same circle of radius √17"
```

This is how a computer turns a picture. A drawing program or a game stores each corner of a shape as
a pair of numbers, works out $$\cos\varphi$$ and $$\sin\varphi$$ once, and puts every corner through
the formula. That costs four multiplications and two additions per corner, however many corners the
shape has.

## Turning about another point

A door turns about its hinge, not about the origin. In
[Combining and undoing moves](../adding-and-scaling-points/combining-moves.mdx), a zoom about a
point $$Q$$ was built from three moves: slide $$Q$$ to the origin, zoom there, and slide back
(:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/zoom-composite}). The same three moves turn about $$Q$$. The hinge
goes to $$O$$, stays put while everything turns, and comes back.

Try it with $$P = (4, 2)$$, turned by $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$ about $$Q = (1, 1)$$. The step from $$Q$$ to
$$P$$ is $$P - Q = (3, 1)$$. Turning that step by $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$, with
$$\cos\frac{\pi}{4} = \sin\frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{\sqrt2}{2}$$, gives

```math
\Big(3 \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2} - 1 \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2},\ 3 \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2} + 1 \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\Big) = (\sqrt2,\ 2\sqrt2)
```

Adding $$Q$$ back gives $$P' = (1 + \sqrt2, 1 + 2\sqrt2) \approx (2.41, 3.83)$$. Both $$P$$ and
$$P'$$ are $$\sqrt{10}$$ from $$Q$$, since $$3^2 + 1^2 = 10$$ and
$$(\sqrt2)^2 + (2\sqrt2)^2 = 2 + 8 = 10$$.

```plane
point X1 -0.5 0 hidden
point X2 5 0 hidden
point Y1 0 -0.5 hidden
point Y2 0 4.5 hidden
point Q 1 1 sw
point P 4 2 e
point P' 2.41 3.83 n
segment X1 X2 color=muted
segment Y1 Y2 color=muted
segment Q P color=blue
segment Q P' color=orange
ticks Q P 1
ticks Q P' 1
angle P Q P' color=orange arrow r=1 label="π/4"
caption "The turn by π/4 about Q = (1, 1) sends P = (4, 2) to P' = (1 + √2, 1 + 2√2), both √10 from Q"
```

:::proposition[A turn about any point]{#rotations-about-point}

Let $$Q$$ be a point and $$\varphi$$ a number. The turn by $$\varphi$$ about $$Q$$ sends each point
$$P$$ to

```math
Q + G_\varphi(P - Q)
```

In coordinates, with $$Q = (a, b)$$, it sends $$(x, y)$$ to

```math
\big(a + (x - a)\cos\varphi - (y - b)\sin\varphi,\ \ b + (x - a)\sin\varphi + (y - b)\cos\varphi\big)
```

In the notation of slides, the turn about $$Q$$ is $$T_Q \circ G_\varphi \circ T_{-Q}$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_plane_isometry#Rotations"}

Write $$A = P - Q$$ and $$P' = Q + G_\varphi(A)$$. By :ref{#radian-turns}, $$G_\varphi$$ is the same
turn as $$G_w$$ for some $$w$$ with $$0 \le w < 2\pi$$, so it is enough to check that $$P'$$ is
where the turn by $$w$$ about $$Q$$ sends $$P$$ (:ref{#isometries/rotation}).

**The centre.** If $$P = Q$$, then $$A = O$$, and the turn about $$O$$ leaves $$O$$ where it is. So
$$P' = Q + O = Q$$, and the centre stays put.

**Same distance.** Let $$P \ne Q$$. By :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/distance-norm},
$$d(Q, P') = |G_\varphi(A)|$$. A turn about $$O$$ keeps the distance from $$O$$
(:ref{#isometries/rotation}), so $$|G_\varphi(A)| = |A| = d(Q, P)$$.

**Turned by $$w$$.** The slide $$T_Q$$ (:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/slide-formula}) sends the
points $$tA$$ with $$t \ge 0$$, which make up the ray from $$O$$ through $$A$$, to the points
$$Q + tA$$, which make up the ray from $$Q$$ through $$P$$
(:ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/ray-formula}). In the same way it sends the ray from $$O$$ through
$$G_\varphi(A)$$ to the ray from $$Q$$ through $$P'$$. A slide keeps the size of every angle
(:ref{#isometries/isometry-angles}). It also keeps the direction of turning, since it carries the
picture along without flipping it over; that part we take from the picture, as the Isometries
chapter does. So the angle turning anticlockwise from the ray $$QP$$ to the ray $$QP'$$ is the angle
from the ray $$OA$$ to the ray $$OG_\varphi(A)$$, which is $$w$$.

**Coordinates.** With $$Q = (a, b)$$, the step is $$A = (x - a, y - b)$$. Turn it with
:ref{#rotation-formula} and add $$Q$$ (:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/point-sum}).

**Slides.** $$T_{-Q}$$ sends $$P$$ to $$P - Q$$, then $$G_\varphi$$ turns that, and $$T_Q$$ adds
$$Q$$, which is the formula above.

:::

## Matrices

Look at the formula for a turn once more. The turned point is built from $$x$$ and $$y$$ with four
numbers, $$\cos\varphi$$, $$-\sin\varphi$$, $$\sin\varphi$$ and $$\cos\varphi$$, and nothing else
about the turn is needed. Several moves from earlier chapters have the same shape. For example, the
flip $$R_1(x, y) = (x, -y)$$ is $$(1 \cdot x + 0 \cdot y,\ 0 \cdot x + (-1) \cdot y)$$, with the
four numbers $$1$$, $$0$$, $$0$$ and $$-1$$.

Writing the four numbers in a square keeps them together, in the order they appear in the formula.

:::definition[Matrix]{#matrix}

A **2 × 2 matrix** is an array of four numbers in two rows and two columns,

```math
M = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}
```

The **product** of $$M$$ with a point $$(x, y)$$, written as a column, is the point

```math
M\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} ax + by \\ cx + dy \end{pmatrix}
```

read "$$M$$ times $$(x, y)$$". Each coordinate of the answer is a row of $$M$$ dotted with
$$(x, y)$$ (:ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/dot-product}).

:::

For example,

```math
\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ -1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 4 \\ -1 \cdot 1 + 3 \cdot 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 6 \\ 11 \end{pmatrix}
```

Multiplying any matrix by $$E_1 = (1, 0)$$ gives $$(a, c)$$, its first column, and multiplying by
$$E_2 = (0, 1)$$ gives $$(b, d)$$, its second column. So a matrix is a record of where $$E_1$$ and
$$E_2$$ go.

:::proposition[The matrix of a turn]{#rotation-matrix}

For every number $$\varphi$$ and every point $$(x, y)$$,

```math
G_\varphi(x, y) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\varphi & -\sin\varphi \\ \sin\varphi & \cos\varphi \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix}
```

This matrix, written $$M_\varphi$$, is called the **matrix** of the turn $$G_\varphi$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_matrix#In_two_dimensions"}

By :ref{#matrix}, the product is the point
$$(x\cos\varphi - y\sin\varphi, x\sin\varphi + y\cos\varphi)$$. By :ref{#rotation-formula}, that is
$$G_\varphi(x, y)$$.

:::

The first column of $$M_\varphi$$ is $$(\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi)$$, where $$E_1$$ lands: the
picture this whole chapter is built on. The second column, $$(-\sin\varphi, \cos\varphi)$$, is where
$$E_2$$ lands, and it is the first column turned a further quarter turn. For example,

```math
M_{\pi/6} = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{\sqrt3}{2} & -\frac12 \\ \frac12 & \frac{\sqrt3}{2} \end{pmatrix} \approx \begin{pmatrix} 0.87 & -0.50 \\ 0.50 & 0.87 \end{pmatrix}
```

The moves about $$O$$ from [Moves as formulas](../adding-and-scaling-points/moves-as-formulas.mdx)
each have a matrix too. Read each one off the formula, or from where $$E_1$$ and $$E_2$$ go.

| Move                              | Formula      | Matrix                                             |
| --------------------------------- | ------------ | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Identity $$I$$                    | $$(x, y)$$   | $$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$   |
| Quarter turn $$G_{\pi/2}$$        | $$(-y, x)$$  | $$\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$  |
| Half turn $$H_O = G_\pi$$         | $$(-x, -y)$$ | $$\begin{pmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$$ |
| Flip $$R_1$$ in the $$x$$-axis    | $$(x, -y)$$  | $$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$$  |
| Flip $$R_2$$ in the $$y$$-axis    | $$(-x, y)$$  | $$\begin{pmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$  |
| Flip $$S$$ in $$y = x$$           | $$(y, x)$$   | $$\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$   |
| Zoom $$Z_r$$ by $$r$$ about $$O$$ | $$(rx, ry)$$ | $$\begin{pmatrix} r & 0 \\ 0 & r \end{pmatrix}$$   |

Slides are missing from the table, and they have to be. Every matrix sends $$O$$ to $$O$$, since
$$a \cdot 0 + b \cdot 0 = 0$$ and $$c \cdot 0 + d \cdot 0 = 0$$. But a slide by $$A \ne O$$ sends
$$O$$ to $$A$$ (:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/slide-formula}).

## Two turns, two matrices

Turning by $$\frac{\pi}{6}$$ and then by $$\frac{\pi}{6}$$ again is turning by $$\frac{\pi}{3}$$,
because turns add (:ref{#radian-turns}). Doing one move after another should combine their two
matrices into one, and for these two turns the result should be $$M_{\pi/3}$$. Working out how the
matrices combine gives a way to multiply them.

Suppose a move $$F$$ multiplies by $$M = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$$ and a move
$$G$$ multiplies by $$N = \begin{pmatrix} e & f \\ g & h \end{pmatrix}$$. Follow a point through
$$F$$ and then $$G$$, and collect the terms in $$x$$ and in $$y$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
(x, y) &\mapsto (ax + by,\ cx + dy) && \text{the move } F \\
&\mapsto \big(e(ax + by) + f(cx + dy),\ g(ax + by) + h(cx + dy)\big) && \text{the move } G \\
&= \big((ea + fc)x + (eb + fd)y,\ (ga + hc)x + (gb + hd)y\big) && \text{multiplying out}
\end{aligned}
```

So $$G \circ F$$ multiplies by a matrix too, made of the four numbers in brackets.

:::definition[Multiplying matrices]{#rotations-matrix-product}

The **product** of the matrices $$N$$ and $$M$$ is

```math
NM = \begin{pmatrix} e & f \\ g & h \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} ea + fc & eb + fd \\ ga + hc & gb + hd \end{pmatrix}
```

The entry in row $$i$$ and column $$j$$ of $$NM$$ is row $$i$$ of $$N$$ dotted with column $$j$$ of
$$M$$. If $$F$$ multiplies by $$M$$ and $$G$$ multiplies by $$N$$, then $$G \circ F$$ multiplies by
$$NM$$.

:::

The matrix of the move done first goes on the right, in the same order as $$G \circ F$$. For
example, :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/combining-moves-flips} found that $$R_1$$ and then $$S$$ is
the quarter turn. The matrices agree:

```math
\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 0 & 0 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot (-1) \\ 1 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 0 & 1 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot (-1) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
```

The order matters. Multiplying the other way round gives
$$\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$, the matrix of $$(x, y) \mapsto (y, -x)$$, which
is the quarter turn clockwise, as that proposition found for $$R_1 \circ S$$.

Now the two turns by $$\frac{\pi}{6}$$. Each entry of $$M_{\pi/6}M_{\pi/6}$$ is a row dotted with a
column:

```math
\begin{aligned}
\text{top left} &= \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} + \big({-\tfrac12}\big) \cdot \tfrac12 = \tfrac34 - \tfrac14 = \tfrac12 \\
\text{top right} &= \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} \cdot \big({-\tfrac12}\big) + \big({-\tfrac12}\big) \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} = -\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} \\
\text{bottom left} &= \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} \cdot \tfrac12 = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} \\
\text{bottom right} &= \tfrac12 \cdot \big({-\tfrac12}\big) + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} = -\tfrac14 + \tfrac34 = \tfrac12
\end{aligned}
```

With $$\cos\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac12$$ and $$\sin\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$
(:ref{#sin-cos-30-60}), that is exactly $$M_{\pi/3}$$.

:::proposition[Two turns, two matrices]{#rotation-composite}

For all numbers $$\alpha$$ and $$\beta$$,

```math
M_\alpha M_\beta = M_{\alpha + \beta}
```

So turning by $$\beta$$ and then by $$\alpha$$, which is the turn $$G_{\alpha + \beta}$$
(:ref{#radian-turns}), multiplies by the matrix $$M_{\alpha + \beta}$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_matrix#Multiplication"}

By :ref{#rotations-matrix-product}, each entry of $$M_\alpha M_\beta$$ is a row of $$M_\alpha$$
dotted with a column of $$M_\beta$$. Each one matches one of the addition formulas
(:ref{#addition-formulas}):

```math
\begin{aligned}
\text{top left} &= \cos\alpha\cos\beta - \sin\alpha\sin\beta = \cos(\alpha + \beta) \\
\text{top right} &= -\cos\alpha\sin\beta - \sin\alpha\cos\beta = -\sin(\alpha + \beta) \\
\text{bottom left} &= \sin\alpha\cos\beta + \cos\alpha\sin\beta = \sin(\alpha + \beta) \\
\text{bottom right} &= -\sin\alpha\sin\beta + \cos\alpha\cos\beta = \cos(\alpha + \beta)
\end{aligned}
```

These are the four entries of $$M_{\alpha + \beta}$$ (:ref{#rotation-matrix}).

:::

The entries of the product are the two sides of the addition formulas. That is no coincidence, since
the formula for a turn came from the addition formulas. So this isn't a second proof of them, but it
is a good way to remember them. Multiply two turn matrices, and the first column of the answer holds
$$\cos(\alpha + \beta)$$ and $$\sin(\alpha + \beta)$$.

Matrices go much further than this. A course in linear algebra uses larger arrays of numbers to
describe moves in space, and to solve several linear equations at once.

::::exercise{#ex-rotations-polar-to-cartesian}

Find the Cartesian coordinates of the points with these polar coordinates:

1. distance $$6$$, angle $$\frac{\pi}{3}$$
2. distance $$\sqrt2$$, angle $$\frac{3\pi}{4}$$
3. distance $$5$$, angle $$\frac{3\pi}{2}$$
4. distance $$2$$, angle $$-\frac{\pi}{6}$$

:::solution

In each case the point is $$(r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta)$$ (:ref{#polar-coordinates}).

1. $$(6 \cdot \frac12, 6 \cdot \frac{\sqrt3}{2}) = (3, 3\sqrt3) \approx (3, 5.20)$$, by
   :ref{#sin-cos-30-60}
2. $$\cos\frac{3\pi}{4} = -\frac{\sqrt2}{2}$$ and $$\sin\frac{3\pi}{4} = \frac{\sqrt2}{2}$$
   (:ref{#supplement-identities}), so the point is
   $$(\sqrt2 \cdot (-\frac{\sqrt2}{2}), \sqrt2 \cdot \frac{\sqrt2}{2}) = (-1, 1)$$
3. $$\cos\frac{3\pi}{2} = 0$$ and $$\sin\frac{3\pi}{2} = -1$$ (:ref{#quarter-values}), so the point
   is $$(0, -5)$$
4. $$\cos(-\frac{\pi}{6}) = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$ and $$\sin(-\frac{\pi}{6}) = -\frac12$$
   (:ref{#negative-angle}), so the point is $$(\sqrt3, -1)$$

:::

::::exercise{#ex-rotations-cartesian-to-polar}

Find polar coordinates, with $$0 \le \theta < 2\pi$$, for $$(0, 5)$$, $$(-2, 2)$$, $$(-\sqrt3, -1)$$
and $$(4, -4)$$.

:::solution

Follow the three steps: the distance, the unit point, and then the angle.

- **$$(0, 5)$$**: $$r = 5$$, and the unit point is
  $$(0, 1) = (\cos\frac{\pi}{2}, \sin\frac{\pi}{2})$$ (:ref{#quarter-values}). Polar coordinates
  $$5, \frac{\pi}{2}$$.
- **$$(-2, 2)$$**: $$r = \sqrt8 = 2\sqrt2$$, and the unit point is
  $$(-\frac{1}{\sqrt2}, \frac{1}{\sqrt2})$$. It is in the second quadrant, $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$ short
  of the negative $$x$$-axis, so $$\theta = \pi - \frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{3\pi}{4}$$
  (:ref{#supplement-identities}). Polar coordinates $$2\sqrt2, \frac{3\pi}{4}$$.
- **$$(-\sqrt3, -1)$$**: $$r = \sqrt{3 + 1} = 2$$, and the unit point is
  $$(-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}, -\frac12)$$. Both signs are negative, so it is in the third quadrant, and
  $$(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}, \frac12)$$ is the point for $$\frac{\pi}{6}$$. A half turn changes both signs
  (:ref{#half-turn-identities}), so $$\theta = \frac{\pi}{6} + \pi = \frac{7\pi}{6}$$. Polar
  coordinates $$2, \frac{7\pi}{6}$$.
- **$$(4, -4)$$**: $$r = \sqrt{32} = 4\sqrt2$$, and the unit point is
  $$(\frac{1}{\sqrt2}, -\frac{1}{\sqrt2})$$, in the fourth quadrant. It is the point for
  $$-\frac{\pi}{4}$$ (:ref{#negative-angle}), and $$-\frac{\pi}{4} + 2\pi = \frac{7\pi}{4}$$. Polar
  coordinates $$4\sqrt2, \frac{7\pi}{4}$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-rotations-turn}

Use :ref{#rotation-formula} to turn $$(2, -1)$$ by $$\frac{\pi}{3}$$ about the origin, exactly and
to two decimal places. Check that the distance from $$O$$ hasn't changed.

:::solution

With $$\cos\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac12$$ and $$\sin\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$, and $$y = -1$$,

```math
\begin{aligned}
x' &= 2 \cdot \tfrac12 - (-1) \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} = 1 + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} \approx 1.87 \\
y' &= 2 \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} + (-1) \cdot \tfrac12 = \sqrt3 - \tfrac12 \approx 1.23
\end{aligned}
```

Before the turn, the squared distance is $$2^2 + (-1)^2 = 5$$. After it, the two $$\sqrt3$$ terms
cancel:

```math
\Big(1 + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\Big)^2 + \Big(\sqrt3 - \tfrac12\Big)^2 = \Big(1 + \sqrt3 + \tfrac34\Big) + \Big(3 - \sqrt3 + \tfrac14\Big) = 5
```

:::

::::exercise{#ex-rotations-calculator}

A friend says the polar angle of $$(-2, -5)$$ is
$$\tan^{-1}\frac{-5}{-2} = \tan^{-1} 2.5 \approx 1.19$$. Explain what has gone wrong, and find polar
coordinates for $$(-2, -5)$$.

:::solution

The angle $$1.19$$ is between $$0$$ and $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$, so it points up and to the right, towards
$$(2, 5)$$. The point $$(-2, -5)$$ lies on the same line through $$O$$, but on the opposite ray,
down and to the left. Since $$x < 0$$, :ref{#rotations-polar-angle} says to add a half turn:

```math
\theta \approx 1.190 + \pi \approx 4.332
```

which is about $$248.2^\circ$$. The distance is $$r = \sqrt{4 + 25} = \sqrt{29} \approx 5.39$$. As a
check, $$5.39\cos 4.332 \approx -2.00$$ and $$5.39\sin 4.332 \approx -5.00$$.

::::exercise{#ex-rotations-clock}

A clock's minute hand is $$15$$ cm long. Put the centre of the clock at $$O$$, so at the hour the
tip is at $$(0, 15)$$. Where is the tip at ten past the hour, and at twenty-five past?

:::solution

The hand turns clockwise, a full turn of $$2\pi$$ every $$60$$ minutes. At the hour the tip has
polar coordinates $$15, \frac{\pi}{2}$$.

**Ten past.** Ten minutes is $$\frac{10}{60}$$ of a turn, $$\frac{\pi}{3}$$, clockwise. By
:ref{#polar-turn}, the angle becomes $$\frac{\pi}{2} - \frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{\pi}{6}$$, so the tip
is at

```math
\Big(15\cos\tfrac{\pi}{6},\ 15\sin\tfrac{\pi}{6}\Big) = \Big(\tfrac{15\sqrt3}{2},\ \tfrac{15}{2}\Big) \approx (12.99,\ 7.5)
```

The formula agrees:
$$G_{-\pi/3}(0, 15) = (0 - 15\sin(-\frac{\pi}{3}), 0 + 15\cos(-\frac{\pi}{3}))$$, which is
$$(\frac{15\sqrt3}{2}, \frac{15}{2})$$ by :ref{#negative-angle}.

**Twenty-five past.** Twenty-five minutes is $$\frac{25}{60} \cdot 2\pi = \frac{5\pi}{6}$$
clockwise, so the angle becomes $$\frac{\pi}{2} - \frac{5\pi}{6} = -\frac{\pi}{3}$$. The tip is at

```math
\Big(15\cos\big({-\tfrac{\pi}{3}}\big),\ 15\sin\big({-\tfrac{\pi}{3}}\big)\Big) = \Big(\tfrac{15}{2},\ -\tfrac{15\sqrt3}{2}\Big) \approx (7.5,\ -12.99)
```

down and to the right, pointing at the 5, as it should.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-rotations-about-point}

1. Show that the turn by $$\pi$$ about a point $$Q$$ is the half turn $$H_Q(P) = 2Q - P$$ of
   :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/half-turn-formula}.
2. Turn $$P = (5, 1)$$ by $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$ about $$Q = (2, 3)$$. Check that the image is the same
   distance from $$Q$$, and that the angle at $$Q$$ is a right angle.

:::solution

1. By :ref{#rotation-formula} with $$\cos\pi = -1$$ and $$\sin\pi = 0$$, $$G_\pi(x, y) = (-x, -y)$$,
   so $$G_\pi(A) = -A$$ for every point $$A$$. By :ref{#rotations-about-point}, the turn by $$\pi$$
   about $$Q$$ sends $$P$$ to

   ```math
   Q + G_\pi(P - Q) = Q - (P - Q) = 2Q - P
   ```

   which is $$H_Q(P)$$.

2. The step is $$P - Q = (3, -2)$$. The quarter turn $$G_{\pi/2}$$ sends $$(x, y)$$ to $$(-y, x)$$
   (part 4 of :ref{#radian-turns}), so it sends the step to $$(2, 3)$$. Adding $$Q$$ gives
   $$P' = (4, 6)$$. Both steps have squared length $$9 + 4 = 13$$ and $$4 + 9 = 13$$, so
   $$d(Q, P) = d(Q, P') = \sqrt{13}$$. Their dot product is $$3 \cdot 2 + (-2) \cdot 3 = 0$$, so the
   angle at $$Q$$ is a right angle (:ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/perpendicular-dot}).

:::

::::exercise{#ex-rotations-flip-matrix}

Let $$R = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$$, the matrix of the flip $$R_1$$. Work out
$$(RM_\varphi)R$$, and show that it is $$M_{-\varphi}$$. Say in words what this tells you about
flipping, turning and flipping back.

:::solution

Write $$c = \cos\varphi$$ and $$s = \sin\varphi$$. By :ref{#rotations-matrix-product},

```math
\begin{aligned}
RM_\varphi &= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} c & -s \\ s & c \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} c & -s \\ -s & -c \end{pmatrix} \\
(RM_\varphi)R &= \begin{pmatrix} c & -s \\ -s & -c \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} c & s \\ -s & c \end{pmatrix}
\end{aligned}
```

By :ref{#negative-angle}, $$\cos(-\varphi) = c$$ and $$\sin(-\varphi) = -s$$, so

```math
M_{-\varphi} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos(-\varphi) & -\sin(-\varphi) \\ \sin(-\varphi) & \cos(-\varphi) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} c & s \\ -s & c \end{pmatrix}
```

which is the same matrix. The product $$(RM_\varphi)R$$ is the matrix of
$$R_1 \circ G_\varphi \circ R_1$$: flip, then turn by $$\varphi$$, then flip back. So that is the
turn by $$-\varphi$$, the same size of turn the other way, as :ref{#flip-reverses-turn} says.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-rotations-double-turn}

Work out $$M_\varphi M_\varphi$$ and compare it with $$M_{2\varphi}$$ to find formulas for
$$\cos 2\varphi$$ and $$\sin 2\varphi$$. Check both with $$\varphi = \frac{\pi}{6}$$.

:::solution

Write $$c = \cos\varphi$$ and $$s = \sin\varphi$$. By :ref{#rotations-matrix-product},

```math
M_\varphi M_\varphi = \begin{pmatrix} c & -s \\ s & c \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} c & -s \\ s & c \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} c^2 - s^2 & -2sc \\ 2sc & c^2 - s^2 \end{pmatrix}
```

By :ref{#rotation-composite}, this is $$M_{2\varphi}$$, whose first column is
$$(\cos 2\varphi, \sin 2\varphi)$$. Comparing first columns,

```math
\cos 2\varphi = \cos^2\varphi - \sin^2\varphi \qquad \sin 2\varphi = 2\sin\varphi\cos\varphi
```

the double angle formulas of :ref{#double-angle}. With $$\varphi = \frac{\pi}{6}$$:
$$\cos^2\frac{\pi}{6} - \sin^2\frac{\pi}{6} = \frac34 - \frac14 = \frac12 = \cos\frac{\pi}{3}$$, and
$$2 \cdot \frac12 \cdot \frac{\sqrt3}{2} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2} = \sin\frac{\pi}{3}$$.

:::
