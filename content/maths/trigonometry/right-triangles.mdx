Imagine leaning a ladder against a wall. Stand it up steeply and the top reaches high; pull the foot
out and the top slides down. Now take a ladder half as long and lean it at the same steep angle. Its
top reaches half as high, so the height is the same fraction of the ladder's length as before. That
fraction depends on the angle alone, and it is the **sine** of the angle.

It is the same sine as in [Sine and cosine](./sine-and-cosine.mdx), the height of the point on the
unit circle, because every right triangle is a copy of a triangle inside the unit circle. In this
guide we'll see why, and use it to find lengths and angles you can't measure directly.

## The triangle inside the unit circle

Take the point on the unit circle at an angle between $$0$$ and a right angle, and drop a line from
it straight down to the $$x$$-axis. That cuts off a right triangle with its corner at the origin.
Its long side runs from the origin to the point, so it has length $$1$$, and its other two sides are
how far across and how far up the point is: the cosine and the sine.

Try it with the angle $$\frac{\pi}{6}$$ ($$30^\circ$$). The point is
$$\left(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}, \frac12\right) \approx (0.87, 0.5)$$ (:ref{#sin-cos-30-60}), so the
triangle has sides $$1$$, $$0.5$$ and about $$0.87$$. Now zoom it up by $$3$$ about the origin. The
long side becomes $$3$$, the height $$1.5$$ and the base about $$2.6$$, and the angle stays
$$\frac{\pi}{6}$$, since a zoom keeps angles (:ref{#area-and-scaling/zoom-rules}). The height
divided by the long side is $$\frac{1.5}{3} = 0.5$$, which is still $$\sin\frac{\pi}{6}$$.

```plane

point P 0.866 0.5 nw
point F 0.866 0 hidden
point B 2.598 0 se
point C 2.598 1.5 ne

segment F P dashed
segment O B color=blue label="≈ 2.6" side=right
segment B C color=orange label="1.5" side=right
angle B O C label="30°" r=0.45
right O F P r=0.12
right O B C r=0.2
caption "The point P on the unit circle at 30° cuts off a triangle with sides 1, 0.5 and 0.87. Zoomed up by 3 it has sides 3, 1.5 and 2.6, and height ÷ long side is still 0.5."
```

The sides of a right triangle have names that say where they sit. The **hypotenuse** faces the right
angle (:ref{#distance-and-angles/right-triangle}). Of the two shorter sides, one touches the angle
you're looking at and the other faces it.

:::definition[Opposite and adjacent sides]{#right-triangles-sides}

Take a right triangle and one of its angles $$\theta$$ other than the right angle.

- **Opposite side**: the side facing $$\theta$$, which doesn't touch its corner
- **Adjacent side**: the side other than the hypotenuse that runs along one arm of $$\theta$$

The word "adjacent" means "next to".

:::

Which side is opposite depends on the angle. At the other corner the two names swap, as
:ref{#other-angle} shows. In the interactive diagram, change the angle and the size of the triangle,
and watch the two fractions.

## Sine and cosine as ratios of sides

The zoom by $$3$$ in the $$30^\circ$$ picture works for every size. A right triangle with an angle
$$\theta$$ is the triangle inside the unit circle at $$\theta$$, zoomed up and perhaps moved
somewhere else. So its height divided by its long side is $$\sin\theta$$ whatever its size, and its
base divided by its long side is $$\cos\theta$$.

:::theorem[Sine and cosine in a right triangle]{#right-triangle-ratios}

In a right triangle with an angle $$\theta$$ other than the right angle,

```math
\sin\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}} \qquad \cos\theta = \frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}}
```

:::

The proof moves the triangle until its angle $$\theta$$ sits at the origin with the adjacent side
along the $$x$$-axis. Then the third corner is a point at the end of the ray at angle $$\theta$$,
and its coordinates are the two shorter sides.

```plane
point O 0 0 sw
point B 4 0 se
point M 4 3 ne
point U 0.8 0.6 nw
point X -1.2 0 hidden
point X2 5.2 0 hidden
circle O 1 color=muted
segment X X2 color=muted
segment O M label="h"
segment O B color=blue label="adj" side=right
segment B M color=orange label="opp" side=right
angle B O M label="θ" r=0.6
right O B M r=0.3
caption "The triangle moved so that θ is at O and the adjacent side runs along the x-axis. The third corner is M = (adj, opp), and the ray OM crosses the unit circle at U = (cos θ, sin θ)."
```

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine_and_cosine#Right-angled_triangle_definition"}

Name the corners: $$P$$ has the angle $$\theta$$, $$Q$$ has the right angle, and $$M$$ is the third.
Write $$h$$ for the hypotenuse, $$\text{adj} = d(P, Q)$$ and $$\text{opp} = d(Q, M)$$.

**Step 1: slide the corner to the origin.** The slide by $$-P$$ sends $$P$$ to $$P - P = O$$
(:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/slide-formula}).

**Step 2: turn the adjacent side onto the x-axis.** After the slide, the image of $$Q$$ is
$$\text{adj}$$ from $$O$$, and so is the point $$(\text{adj}, 0)$$. Since $$\text{adj} > 0$$, rule 1
of :ref{#isometries/rotation-rules}, with the angle in radians, gives a turn about $$O$$ that sends
the one to the other.

**What the two moves keep.** Slides and turns are isometries (:ref{#isometries/basic-isometries}),
so doing one and then the other is an isometry too (:ref{#isometries/composite-isometry}). It keeps
distances and angles (:ref{#isometries/isometry-angles}). So the moved triangle has corners $$O$$,
$$B = (\text{adj}, 0)$$ and some point $$(x, y)$$, with the same sides and angles as before: the
right angle at $$B$$, the angle $$\theta$$ at $$O$$, and the hypotenuse $$h$$ from $$O$$ to
$$(x, y)$$.

**Step 3: the third corner is straight above or below B.** The angle at $$B$$ is a right angle, so
by :ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/perpendicular-dot}, taken at the corner $$B$$,

```math
\begin{aligned}
(O - B) \cdot \big((x, y) - B\big) &= 0 && \text{right angle at } B \\
(-\text{adj})(x - \text{adj}) + 0 \cdot y &= 0 && \text{the dot product, worked out} \\
x &= \text{adj} && \text{divide by } -\text{adj} \ne 0
\end{aligned}
```

So the third corner is $$(\text{adj}, y)$$, and its distance from $$B$$ is $$|y| = \text{opp}$$. If
$$y = -\text{opp}$$, flip everything in the $$x$$-axis with $$R_1$$
(:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/axis-reflections}). The flip is an isometry, it leaves $$O$$ and
$$B$$ where they are, and it sends $$(\text{adj}, -\text{opp})$$ to $$(\text{adj}, \text{opp})$$. So
we can take the third corner to be $$M' = (\text{adj}, \text{opp})$$.

**Step 4: the third corner is on the ray at angle θ.** The point $$M'$$ and the point $$(h, 0)$$ are
both $$h$$ from $$O$$. By rule 1 of :ref{#isometries/rotation-rules}, exactly one turn $$G_x$$ about
$$O$$, with $$0 \le x < 2\pi$$, sends $$(h, 0)$$ to $$M'$$, and by :ref{#turn-scales},

```math
M' = G_x(hE_1) = h\,G_x(E_1) = (h\cos x, h\sin x)
```

By the definition of a turn (:ref{#isometries/rotation}), $$x$$ is the angle turning anticlockwise
from the ray through $$(h, 0)$$, which is the ray $$OB$$, to the ray $$OM'$$. The two angles between
these rays add up to a full turn, and the angle $$\theta$$ of the triangle is the one that is at
most a straight angle (:ref{#distance-and-angles/angle-name}). So $$x = \theta$$ or
$$x = 2\pi - \theta$$.

**Step 5: rule out the wrong one.** The other two angles of a right triangle add up to a right angle
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/right-triangle-angles}), so $$\theta < \frac{\pi}{2}$$ ($$90^\circ$$).
Then $$2\pi - \theta$$ lies between $$\frac{3\pi}{2}$$ ($$270^\circ$$) and $$2\pi$$ ($$360^\circ$$),
where the sine is negative (:ref{#quadrant-signs}). But $$h\sin x = \text{opp}$$ is positive, so
$$x = \theta$$.

**Step 6: compare coordinates.** Now $$(\text{adj}, \text{opp}) = (h\cos\theta, h\sin\theta)$$, so
$$\text{adj} = h\cos\theta$$ and $$\text{opp} = h\sin\theta$$. Dividing both by $$h$$ gives the two
ratios.

:::

The heart of the proof is Step 4. Once the angle $$\theta$$ sits at $$O$$, the third corner is the
point at distance $$h$$ along the ray at angle $$\theta$$, which is $$(h\cos\theta, h\sin\theta)$$
(:ref{#circle-point-radius}). That is the point $$(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$$ on the unit circle
scaled up by $$h$$, so the moved triangle is the unit circle's triangle zoomed by $$h$$. Zooming
multiplies every side by $$h$$ (rule 1 of :ref{#area-and-scaling/zoom-rules}), and a fraction whose
top and bottom are both multiplied by $$h$$ doesn't change. That is why the size of the triangle
never matters: right triangles with the same angle $$\theta$$ are similar
(:ref{#area-and-scaling/similar}).

The picture of the moved triangle is drawn with $$h = 5$$, $$\text{adj} = 4$$ and
$$\text{opp} = 3$$. So $$\cos\theta = \frac45 = 0.8$$ and $$\sin\theta = \frac35 = 0.6$$, and the
ray meets the unit circle at $$U = (0.8, 0.6)$$. As a check, $$0.8^2 + 0.6^2 = 0.64 + 0.36 = 1$$, as
:ref{#pythagorean-identity} says it must.

If you learned trigonometry as "SOH CAH TOA", this theorem is the first two parts. **S**ine is
**O**pposite over **H**ypotenuse, and **C**osine is **A**djacent over **H**ypotenuse. The third
part, for the tangent, comes in [The tangent](./tangent.mdx). The rhyme is a memory aid, and the
reason behind it is the triangle inside the unit circle.

## The other angle

A right triangle has two angles besides the right angle, and they share the two shorter sides. The
side opposite one of them is adjacent to the other. So swapping angles swaps the two fractions, and
the sine of one angle is the cosine of the other.

:::corollary[The other angle swaps sine and cosine]{#other-angle}

For $$0 < \theta < \frac{\pi}{2}$$, that is, for angles between $$0^\circ$$ and $$90^\circ$$,

```math
\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - \theta\right) = \cos\theta \qquad \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - \theta\right) = \sin\theta
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_trigonometric_identities#Reflections"}

**A triangle with the angle θ.** Let $$P = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$$ and $$F = (\cos\theta, 0)$$.
Both coordinates of $$P$$ are positive (:ref{#quadrant-signs}), so the three points $$O$$, $$F$$ and
$$P$$ are different. The angle at $$F$$ is a right angle, since
$$(O - F) \cdot (P - F) = (-\cos\theta, 0) \cdot (0, \sin\theta) = 0$$
(:ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/perpendicular-dot}). The turn $$G_\theta$$ sends $$E_1$$ to $$P$$
(:ref{#sine-cosine}), and $$F = \cos\theta \, E_1$$ is on the ray $$OE_1$$ since $$\cos\theta > 0$$
(:ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/ray-formula}), so the angle at $$O$$ is $$\theta$$
(:ref{#isometries/rotation}, and :ref{#distance-and-angles/angle-name} since $$\theta < \pi$$).

**The other angle.** The angle at $$P$$ is $$\frac{\pi}{2} - \theta$$, since the two angles other
than the right angle add up to a right angle (:ref{#distance-and-angles/right-triangle-angles}). The
side opposite it is $$OF$$, of length $$\cos\theta$$, and the side adjacent to it is $$FP$$, of
length $$\sin\theta$$. The hypotenuse $$OP$$ has length $$1$$. By :ref{#right-triangle-ratios},
applied at $$P$$,

```math
\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - \theta\right) = \frac{\cos\theta}{1} \qquad \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - \theta\right) = \frac{\sin\theta}{1}
```

:::

For example, take $$\theta = \frac{\pi}{6}$$ ($$30^\circ$$). Then
$$\frac{\pi}{2} - \theta = \frac{\pi}{3}$$ ($$60^\circ$$), and the corollary says
$$\sin\frac{\pi}{3} = \cos\frac{\pi}{6}$$. Both are $$\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$, by :ref{#sin-cos-30-60}.
The same two equations hold for every number $$\theta$$, not only angles of a triangle, and
:ref{#complement-identities} proves them with a flip.

## Finding the sides

When you know one angle of a right triangle and one side, the theorem gives the other sides. Write
it as $$\text{opposite} = \text{hypotenuse} \cdot \sin\theta$$ and
$$\text{adjacent} = \text{hypotenuse} \cdot \cos\theta$$, and put in the numbers. A calculator gives
the values of sine and cosine, in the mode that matches your angle.

:::example[A ladder against a wall]

A ladder $$5$$ m long leans against a wall at $$70^\circ$$ to the ground, which is about $$1.22$$
radians. The ladder is the hypotenuse, the wall from the ground to the top of the ladder is opposite
the angle, and the ground from the wall to the foot is adjacent. So

```math
\begin{aligned}
\text{height of the top} &= 5\sin 70^\circ \approx 5 \cdot 0.940 \approx 4.70 \text{ m} \\
\text{foot to wall} &= 5\cos 70^\circ \approx 5 \cdot 0.342 \approx 1.71 \text{ m}
\end{aligned}
```

As a check, $$4.70^2 + 1.71^2 \approx 22.1 + 2.9 = 25.0 = 5^2$$, as
:ref{#distance-and-angles/pythagoras} says it should be.

:::

:::example[A kite on a string]

A kite flies on a taut string $$30$$ m long, at $$\frac{\pi}{6}$$ ($$30^\circ$$) above the ground.
Treat the string as straight, held at ground level. The special angles give exact answers
(:ref{#sin-cos-30-60}):

```math
\begin{aligned}
\text{height of the kite} &= 30\sin\frac{\pi}{6} = 30 \cdot \frac12 = 15 \text{ m} \\
\text{distance across the ground} &= 30\cos\frac{\pi}{6} = 30 \cdot \frac{\sqrt3}{2} = 15\sqrt3 \approx 25.98 \text{ m}
\end{aligned}
```

:::

Sometimes the side you know is one of the shorter ones, and then you divide instead of multiplying.
Say a roof beam climbs at $$\frac{\pi}{6}$$ ($$30^\circ$$) and rises $$2$$ m from its low end to its
high end. The rise is opposite the angle and the beam is the hypotenuse $$h$$, so
$$\frac{2}{h} = \sin\frac{\pi}{6} = \frac12$$. Then $$h = 2 \div \frac12 = 4$$ m: the beam is twice
as long as its rise.

## Finding the angle

Now turn the question round. A ramp climbs $$1$$ m along a slope $$12$$ m long, and you want the
angle of the slope. The theorem says the angle $$\theta$$ has $$\sin\theta = \frac{1}{12}$$. What's
missing is a way to go from a sine back to the angle.

On the unit circle, that means finding the point at a given height. A horizontal line at height
$$\frac{1}{12}$$ crosses the circle twice, once on the right half and once on the left. The right
half covers the angles from $$-\frac{\pi}{2}$$ to $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$ ($$-90^\circ$$ to $$90^\circ$$),
so exactly one of those angles has the sine you want. In the same way a vertical line crosses the
upper half of the circle once, and the upper half covers the angles from $$0$$ to $$\pi$$.

:::proposition[One angle for each sine and each cosine]{#right-triangles-one-angle}

Let $$c$$ be a number with $$-1 \le c \le 1$$.

1. Exactly one number $$x$$ with $$-\frac{\pi}{2} \le x \le \frac{\pi}{2}$$ has $$\sin x = c$$: one
   angle from $$-90^\circ$$ to $$90^\circ$$.
2. Exactly one number $$x$$ with $$0 \le x \le \pi$$ has $$\cos x = c$$: one angle from $$0^\circ$$
   to $$180^\circ$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_trigonometric_functions#Principal_values"}

**The angles of each half.** By :ref{#quarter-values} and :ref{#quadrant-signs}, for $$x$$ in
$$[0, 2\pi)$$ the cosine is $$0$$ or more exactly when $$x$$ is in $$[0, \frac{\pi}{2}]$$ or in
$$[\frac{3\pi}{2}, 2\pi)$$, and the sine is $$0$$ or more exactly when $$x$$ is in $$[0, \pi]$$.

**Part 1.** A point $$(u, c)$$ is on the unit circle exactly when $$u^2 + c^2 = 1$$
(:ref{#coordinates/circle-equation}), that is, $$u = \pm\sqrt{1 - c^2}$$. So exactly one point of
the circle at height $$c$$ has $$u \ge 0$$, namely $$V = (\sqrt{1 - c^2}, c)$$.

Adding $$2\pi$$ to each $$x$$ in $$[-\frac{\pi}{2}, 0)$$ moves it to $$[\frac{3\pi}{2}, 2\pi)$$ and
doesn't change its sine or cosine (:ref{#periodic}). So the numbers of
$$[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}]$$ match up, one for one, with the numbers $$y$$ of $$[0, 2\pi)$$
that have $$\cos y \ge 0$$. For each of these, $$(\cos y, \sin y)$$ is a point of the circle with
first coordinate $$0$$ or more, so its sine is $$c$$ exactly when the point is $$V$$. By
:ref{#unit-circle-point}, exactly one $$y$$ in $$[0, 2\pi)$$ gives the point $$V$$, and its cosine
$$\sqrt{1 - c^2}$$ is $$0$$ or more. So exactly one $$x$$ in $$[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}]$$ has
$$\sin x = c$$.

**Part 2.** In the same way, exactly one point of the circle with first coordinate $$c$$ has second
coordinate $$0$$ or more, namely $$W = (c, \sqrt{1 - c^2})$$. A number $$x$$ in $$[0, \pi]$$ has
$$\sin x \ge 0$$, so $$\cos x = c$$ exactly when $$(\cos x, \sin x) = W$$. By
:ref{#unit-circle-point}, exactly one $$x$$ in $$[0, 2\pi)$$ gives $$W$$, and its sine is $$0$$ or
more, so it lies in $$[0, \pi]$$.

:::

:::definition[Inverse sine and inverse cosine]{#inverse-sin-cos}

Let $$-1 \le c \le 1$$.

- The **inverse sine** $$\sin^{-1} c$$ is the one angle in $$[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}]$$ whose
  sine is $$c$$.
- The **inverse cosine** $$\cos^{-1} c$$ is the one angle in $$[0, \pi]$$ whose cosine is $$c$$.

They are also called $$\arcsin c$$ and $$\arccos c$$.

:::

These are the $$\sin^{-1}$$ and $$\cos^{-1}$$ buttons on a calculator. For example,
$$\sin^{-1}\frac12 = \frac{\pi}{6}$$ ($$30^\circ$$), since $$\sin\frac{\pi}{6} = \frac12$$ and
$$\frac{\pi}{6}$$ lies between $$-\frac{\pi}{2}$$ and $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$. Similarly
$$\cos^{-1} 0 = \frac{\pi}{2}$$ ($$90^\circ$$) and $$\cos^{-1}(-1) = \pi$$ ($$180^\circ$$), by
:ref{#quarter-values}.

By :ref{#right-triangles-one-angle}, the sine on $$[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}]$$ is one to one
with range $$[-1, 1]$$. So $$\sin^{-1}$$ is the inverse function of the sine on that stretch
(:ref{#functions/inverse-function}), and in the same way $$\cos^{-1}$$ is the inverse of the cosine
on $$[0, \pi]$$. It undoes the sine for angles in the stretch, and gives a different angle for the
rest: $$\sin^{-1}(\sin\frac{5\pi}{6}) = \sin^{-1}\frac12 = \frac{\pi}{6}$$.

As [Sine and cosine](./sine-and-cosine.mdx) warned, the little $$-1$$ in $$\sin^{-1}$$ is not a
power. It means "the angle whose sine is", so $$\sin^{-1} c$$ is not $$\frac{1}{\sin c}$$. With
$$c = \frac12$$, the angle $$\sin^{-1}\frac12$$ is about $$0.52$$, but $$\frac{1}{\sin(0.5)}$$ is
about $$2.09$$. When you mean the fraction, write $$\frac{1}{\sin x}$$ or $$(\sin x)^{-1}$$.

In a right triangle, every angle other than the right angle lies between $$0$$ and $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$
($$90^\circ$$). That is inside both ranges in :ref{#inverse-sin-cos}, so the calculator buttons
always give the angle of the triangle.

:::example[The angle of a ramp]

The ramp climbs $$1$$ m along a slope $$12$$ m long. The climb is opposite the angle and the slope
is the hypotenuse, so

```math
\theta = \sin^{-1}\frac{1}{12} \approx 0.0834 \text{ radians} \approx 4.78^\circ
```

The ground the ramp covers is the adjacent side, $$12\cos\theta \approx 11.96$$ m. As a check, by
:ref{#distance-and-angles/pythagoras} it is $$\sqrt{12^2 - 1^2} = \sqrt{143} \approx 11.96$$ m too.

:::

## Two angles with the same sine

Inside a triangle there is one angle for each sine. Round the whole circle there are two, because
the horizontal line at height $$c$$ crosses the circle at two points, mirror images in the
$$y$$-axis. Take the height $$\frac12$$. The point on the right is at $$\frac{\pi}{6}$$
($$30^\circ$$). Its mirror image on the left is $$\frac{\pi}{6}$$ short of a half turn, at
$$\pi - \frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{5\pi}{6}$$ ($$150^\circ$$). So both $$\frac{\pi}{6}$$ and
$$\frac{5\pi}{6}$$ have sine $$\frac12$$.

```plane
scale 3.2
point O 0 0 s
point P 0.866 0.5 ne
point Q -0.866 0.5 nw
point E 1 0 hidden
point X -1.3 0 hidden
point X2 1.3 0 hidden
point Y 0 -1.2 hidden
point Y2 0 1.2 hidden
point L -1.25 0.5 hidden
point R 1.25 0.5 hidden
circle O 1 color=muted
segment X X2 color=muted
segment Y Y2 color=muted
segment L R color=orange dashed
segment O P color=blue
segment O Q color=green
angle E O P label="π/6" r=0.4
angle E O Q label="5π/6" r=0.22 arrow
caption "The line at height 1/2 meets the unit circle at P, at π/6 (30°), and at its mirror image Q in the y-axis, at 5π/6 (150°)."
```

The same mirror works for every point above the $$x$$-axis. The flip in the $$y$$-axis keeps the
height of a point, and it swaps the two ends of the $$x$$-axis. So a point at angle $$x$$ from the
right-hand end of the axis lands at angle $$x$$ from the left-hand end. The two angles at $$O$$ on
either side of the image add up to a straight angle, $$\pi$$ ($$180^\circ$$), so the image is at
angle $$\pi - x$$ from the right-hand end.

:::proposition[Two angles with the same sine]{#same-sine}

For every number $$x$$ with $$0 < x < \pi$$, that is, for every angle between $$0^\circ$$ and
$$180^\circ$$,

```math
\sin(\pi - x) = \sin x
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_trigonometric_identities#Reflections"}

Let $$P = (\cos x, \sin x)$$, and let $$P' = (-\cos x, \sin x)$$, its mirror image in the
$$y$$-axis.

**Both points are above the axis.** For $$0 < x < \pi$$ the sine is positive: by
:ref{#quadrant-signs} in the first and second quadrants, and $$\sin\frac{\pi}{2} = 1$$ by
:ref{#quarter-values}. So $$P$$ and $$P'$$, which have height $$\sin x$$, are both above the
$$x$$-axis. The point $$P$$ is on the unit circle by part 1 of :ref{#unit-circle-point}.

**The angle for P.** Say $$\angle E_1OP$$ measures $$\theta^\circ$$. By
:ref{#special-angles-angle-to-turn}, $$P = (\cos y, \sin y)$$ with $$y = \frac{\pi\theta}{180}$$,
and $$0 < y < \pi$$ since $$0 < \theta < 180$$. But $$P$$ is also $$(\cos x, \sin x)$$, and part 2
of :ref{#unit-circle-point} says only one number from $$0$$ up to $$2\pi$$ gives $$P$$. So
$$y = x$$, and $$\theta = \frac{180x}{\pi}$$: the angle $$x$$, written in degrees.

**The flip keeps that angle.** The flip $$R_2(a, b) = (-a, b)$$ in the $$y$$-axis
(:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/axis-reflections}) is an isometry
(:ref{#isometries/basic-isometries}). It sends $$O$$ to $$O$$, $$E_1$$ to $$-E_1$$ and $$P$$ to
$$P'$$. So by :ref{#isometries/isometry-angles}, the angle $$\angle(-E_1)OP'$$ also measures
$$\theta^\circ$$. Because the flip keeps the distance from $$O = R_2(O)$$, the point $$P'$$ is on
the unit circle too.

**The angle for P'.** The point $$O$$ lies between $$E_1$$ and $$-E_1$$ on the $$x$$-axis, and
$$P'$$ is not on that line. By :ref{#distance-and-angles/angles-on-line},

```math
\angle E_1OP' + \angle P'O(-E_1) = 180^\circ
```

So $$\angle E_1OP'$$ measures $$(180 - \theta)^\circ$$.

**Comparing heights.** By :ref{#special-angles-angle-to-turn} again, $$P' = (\cos z, \sin z)$$ with

```math
z = \frac{\pi(180 - \theta)}{180} = \pi - \frac{\pi\theta}{180} = \pi - x
```

So $$P' = \big(\cos(\pi - x), \sin(\pi - x)\big)$$. The second coordinate of $$P'$$ is $$\sin x$$,
so $$\sin(\pi - x) = \sin x$$.

:::

Comparing the first coordinates in the same way gives $$\cos(\pi - x) = -\cos x$$: the mirror image
is as far across as $$P$$, on the other side. Both equations hold for every number $$x$$, not only
between $$0$$ and $$\pi$$. The guide [Identities you can see](./symmetries.mdx) proves them for
every $$x$$ (:ref{#supplement-identities}), by a different route that doesn't use this proposition.

So an equation $$\sin x = c$$ with $$-1 < c < 1$$ has two answers in each full turn, and the
calculator gives only one of them, $$\sin^{-1} c$$. The other is $$\pi - \sin^{-1} c$$, by
:ref{#supplement-identities}, which covers negative $$c$$ too. In a right triangle that second
answer is never needed, since it is bigger than $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$ ($$90^\circ$$) when the first is
smaller. But when you solve an equation round the whole circle, remember to look for it. The guide
[Working with identities](./working-with-identities.mdx) finds every solution
(:ref{#sine-solutions}).

## The side of a regular polygon

A regular polygon in a circle (:ref{#area-and-scaling/inscribed-polygon}) is made of thin isosceles
slices, each with two sides equal to the radius. Cut one slice down the middle, from the centre to
the middle of the polygon's side, and it falls into two right triangles. Each has the radius as its
hypotenuse and half the slice's angle at the centre, so half the polygon's side is the radius times
a sine.

```plane
point O 0 0 sw
point A 3 0 se
point B 2.1213 2.1213 ne
point M 2.5607 1.0607 e
circle O 3 color=muted
segment O A label="r" side=right
segment O B label="r"
segment A B color=orange
segment O M color=blue dashed
angle A O M label="π/n" r=1.1
right O M B r=0.25
caption "One slice of a regular polygon in a circle of radius r. The dashed line from O to the midpoint M of the side cuts it into two right triangles, each with angle π/n at O."
```

:::proposition[The side of a regular polygon in a circle]{#polygon-side}

The regular $$n$$-sided polygon in a circle of radius $$r$$ has sides of length
$$2r\sin\frac{\pi}{n}$$, and its perimeter is

```math
2nr\sin\frac{\pi}{n}
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_polygon#Circumradius"}

Let $$O$$ be the centre and $$A$$, $$B$$ two neighbouring corners, so $$d(O, A) = d(O, B) = r$$ and
the angle $$AOB$$ is $$\frac{360}{n}$$ degrees (:ref{#area-and-scaling/inscribed-polygon}), which is
$$\frac{2\pi}{n}$$ radians (:ref{#radian}). Let $$M$$ be the midpoint of $$AB$$.

**The right angle.** Both $$O$$ and $$M$$ are as far from $$A$$ as from $$B$$, so both lie on the
perpendicular bisector of $$AB$$ (:ref{#distance-and-angles/perpendicular-bisector-rule}). Since
$$n \ge 3$$, the angle $$AOB$$ is less than a straight angle, so $$O$$ is not on $$AB$$ and
$$O \ne M$$. So the line $$OM$$ is the perpendicular bisector, and the angle at $$M$$ in the
triangle $$OMA$$ is a right angle.

**The half angle.** The triangles $$OMA$$ and $$OMB$$ have the same three sides: $$r$$, the shared
side $$OM$$, and $$d(M, A) = d(M, B)$$. So they are congruent (:ref{#isometries/sss}), and the
isometry matching them keeps the angle at $$O$$ (:ref{#isometries/isometry-angles}), so
$$\angle AOM = \angle MOB$$. The point $$M$$ is on the segment $$AB$$, so these two angles add up to
$$\angle AOB = \frac{2\pi}{n}$$ (:ref{#distance-and-angles/angle-addition}). Each is
$$\frac{\pi}{n}$$.

**The side.** In the right triangle $$OMA$$ the hypotenuse is $$OA$$, of length $$r$$, and the side
opposite the angle $$\frac{\pi}{n}$$ at $$O$$ is $$MA$$. By :ref{#right-triangle-ratios},
$$d(M, A) = r\sin\frac{\pi}{n}$$. The side $$AB$$ is twice as long, $$2r\sin\frac{\pi}{n}$$, and the
$$n$$ equal sides make the perimeter $$2nr\sin\frac{\pi}{n}$$.

:::

For the hexagon, $$n = 6$$, and the angle $$\frac{\pi}{6}$$ ($$30^\circ$$) has sine $$\frac12$$, so
each side is $$2r \cdot \frac12 = r$$. That matches the hexagon in
[Around the circle](../area-and-scaling/circumference.mdx), whose slices are equilateral.

Dividing the perimeter by the diameter $$2r$$ leaves $$n\sin\frac{\pi}{n}$$, with no $$r$$ in it.
These are the numbers in the table under
[Pinning down pi](../area-and-scaling/circumference.mdx#pinning-down-pi), which creep up towards
$$\pi$$ as the polygon hugs the circle:

| Sides $$n$$ | $$n \sin\frac{\pi}{n}$$ |
| ----------- | ----------------------- |
| $$6$$       | $$3$$                   |
| $$12$$      | $$3.1058$$              |
| $$24$$      | $$3.1326$$              |
| $$48$$      | $$3.1394$$              |
| $$96$$      | $$3.1410$$              |

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on sine and cosine in right triangles, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-right-triangles-ratios}

A right triangle has hypotenuse $$10$$. The side opposite the angle $$\theta$$ is $$6$$ and the side
adjacent to it is $$8$$. Find $$\sin\theta$$ and $$\cos\theta$$, and check that
$$\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$$.

:::solution

By :ref{#right-triangle-ratios}, $$\sin\theta = \frac{6}{10} = 0.6$$ and
$$\cos\theta = \frac{8}{10} = 0.8$$. Then $$0.6^2 + 0.8^2 = 0.36 + 0.64 = 1$$, as
:ref{#pythagorean-identity} says.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-right-triangles-sides}

A right triangle has hypotenuse $$8$$ and an angle of $$\frac{\pi}{3}$$ ($$60^\circ$$). Find the
side opposite that angle and the side adjacent to it, exactly and to two decimal places.

:::solution

By :ref{#right-triangle-ratios} and :ref{#sin-cos-30-60},

```math
\begin{aligned}
\text{opposite} &= 8\sin\frac{\pi}{3} = 8 \cdot \frac{\sqrt3}{2} = 4\sqrt3 \approx 6.93 \\
\text{adjacent} &= 8\cos\frac{\pi}{3} = 8 \cdot \frac12 = 4
\end{aligned}
```

As a check, $$(4\sqrt3)^2 + 4^2 = 48 + 16 = 64 = 8^2$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-right-triangles-escalator}

An escalator $$15$$ m long climbs at $$\frac{\pi}{6}$$ ($$30^\circ$$) to the floor. How high does it
lift you, and how far across the floor does it carry you?

:::solution

The escalator is the hypotenuse. By :ref{#right-triangle-ratios} and :ref{#sin-cos-30-60}, the
height is $$15\sin\frac{\pi}{6} = 15 \cdot \frac12 = 7.5$$ m, and the distance across is
$$15\cos\frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{15\sqrt3}{2} \approx 12.99$$ m.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-right-triangles-mast}

A rope $$13$$ m long runs from the top of a mast straight to a peg in the ground, $$5$$ m from the
foot of the mast. Find the angle between the rope and the ground, and the height of the mast.

:::solution

The rope is the hypotenuse, and the ground from the peg to the foot of the mast is adjacent to the
angle $$\theta$$ at the peg. So $$\cos\theta = \frac{5}{13}$$, and by :ref{#inverse-sin-cos},

```math
\theta = \cos^{-1}\frac{5}{13} \approx 1.176 \text{ radians} \approx 67.38^\circ
```

The mast is the opposite side. By :ref{#distance-and-angles/pythagoras}, its height is
$$\sqrt{13^2 - 5^2} = \sqrt{144} = 12$$ m. As a check, $$\sin\theta = \frac{12}{13}$$, and
$$\left(\frac{5}{13}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{12}{13}\right)^2 = \frac{25 + 144}{169} = 1$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-right-triangles-inverse}

1. Find $$\sin^{-1}\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$, and both angles $$x$$ with $$0 \le x < 2\pi$$ that have
   $$\sin x = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$.
2. A friend works out $$\sin^{-1} 0.5$$ as $$\frac{1}{\sin 0.5}$$ and gets about $$2.09$$. Explain
   the slip, and give the right value.

:::solution

1. $$\sin\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$ (:ref{#sin-cos-30-60}), and $$\frac{\pi}{3}$$ lies in
   $$[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}]$$, so $$\sin^{-1}\frac{\sqrt3}{2} = \frac{\pi}{3}$$
   ($$60^\circ$$) by :ref{#inverse-sin-cos}. By :ref{#same-sine},
   $$\pi - \frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{2\pi}{3}$$ ($$120^\circ$$) has the same sine. So the two angles are
   $$\frac{\pi}{3}$$ and $$\frac{2\pi}{3}$$, the point on the unit circle at height
   $$\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$ and its mirror image in the $$y$$-axis.
2. The $$-1$$ in $$\sin^{-1}$$ is not a power: $$\sin^{-1} 0.5$$ is the angle in
   $$[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}]$$ whose sine is $$0.5$$. That angle is $$\frac{\pi}{6}$$
   ($$30^\circ$$), about $$0.52$$, since $$\sin\frac{\pi}{6} = \frac12$$ (:ref{#sin-cos-30-60}). The
   friend's number, $$\frac{1}{\sin 0.5} \approx \frac{1}{0.479} \approx 2.09$$, is the reciprocal
   of a sine, a different thing.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-right-triangles-square}

Use :ref{#polygon-side} to find the side of a square in a circle of radius $$r$$, and check it with
the Pythagoras theorem. Then find the perimeter divided by the diameter for the regular octagon, to
four decimal places.

:::solution

For the square, $$n = 4$$, so the side is
$$2r\sin\frac{\pi}{4} = 2r \cdot \frac{\sqrt2}{2} = r\sqrt2$$ (:ref{#sin-cos-45}). As a check,
neighbouring corners make a right angle at the centre ($$\frac{360}{4} = 90$$ degrees), with legs
$$r$$ and $$r$$, so by :ref{#distance-and-angles/pythagoras} the side is
$$\sqrt{r^2 + r^2} = r\sqrt2$$.

For the octagon, $$n = 8$$, and the perimeter divided by the diameter is
$$8\sin\frac{\pi}{8} \approx 8 \cdot 0.38268 \approx 3.0615$$. It lies between the hexagon's $$3$$
and the twelve-sided polygon's $$3.1058$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-right-triangles-pythagoras}

Use :ref{#right-triangle-ratios} and the Pythagoras theorem to show that
$$\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$$ for every angle $$\theta$$ of a right triangle other than the
right angle.

:::solution

Write $$h$$ for the hypotenuse and $$p$$ and $$q$$ for the sides opposite and adjacent to
$$\theta$$. By :ref{#right-triangle-ratios}, $$\sin\theta = \frac{p}{h}$$ and
$$\cos\theta = \frac{q}{h}$$. So

```math
\begin{aligned}
\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta &= \frac{p^2}{h^2} + \frac{q^2}{h^2} && \text{square each fraction} \\
&= \frac{p^2 + q^2}{h^2} && \text{same bottom} \\
&= \frac{h^2}{h^2} = 1 && p^2 + q^2 = h^2 \text{ by Pythagoras}
\end{aligned}
```

The last step is :ref{#distance-and-angles/pythagoras}, with the hypotenuse $$h$$ and the legs $$p$$
and $$q$$.

This is :ref{#pythagorean-identity} for angles between $$0$$ and $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$, seen from the
triangle instead of the circle.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-right-triangles-equilateral}

Cut an equilateral triangle with sides $$2$$ in half, from one corner to the midpoint of the
opposite side. Each half is a right triangle with angles $$\frac{\pi}{6}$$ ($$30^\circ$$) and
$$\frac{\pi}{3}$$ ($$60^\circ$$), hypotenuse $$2$$, and side $$1$$ opposite the angle
$$\frac{\pi}{6}$$. Find the third side, and use :ref{#right-triangle-ratios} to find the sine and
cosine of both angles. Compare with :ref{#sin-cos-30-60}.

:::solution

By :ref{#distance-and-angles/pythagoras}, the third side is $$\sqrt{2^2 - 1^2} = \sqrt3$$. The side
$$1$$ is opposite $$\frac{\pi}{6}$$ and adjacent to $$\frac{\pi}{3}$$. The side $$\sqrt3$$ is
opposite $$\frac{\pi}{3}$$ and adjacent to $$\frac{\pi}{6}$$. So by :ref{#right-triangle-ratios},

```math
\sin\frac{\pi}{6} = \cos\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac12 \qquad \cos\frac{\pi}{6} = \sin\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}
```

These are the values of :ref{#sin-cos-30-60}, and the pairs agree as :ref{#other-angle} says, since
$$\frac{\pi}{6} + \frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{\pi}{2}$$.

:::
