Imagine riding the Ferris wheel from [Sine and cosine](./sine-and-cosine.mdx) while it turns at a
steady speed. A friend on the ground writes down how high your car is above the centre of the wheel
every few seconds, and plots each height against the time. The dots climb, level off at the top,
fall through the middle, level off at the bottom and climb again. Turn after turn, the same wave
comes back.

<FerrisHeights caption="A twelfth of a turn passes between one dot and the next. The marked car is a sixth of a turn up from the right, and its height is carried across to its dot." />

Each height is the radius of the wheel times the sine of the angle the car has turned through. So
the wave has the shape of the **graph of sine**. In this guide we'll draw the graphs of sine and
cosine from the unit circle, and read what they tell us about the two numbers.

## From the circle to a wave

Picture the point on the unit circle as it turns once round, starting at $$(1, 0)$$. Sine is how far
up the point is. At the start the point is on the $$x$$-axis, so its height is $$0$$. It climbs to
height $$1$$ at the top of the circle after a quarter turn, comes back down to $$0$$ on the left
after a half turn, sinks to $$-1$$ at the bottom and returns to $$0$$ after a full turn.

So as $$x$$ goes from $$0$$ to $$2\pi$$, the height $$\sin x$$ goes $$0$$, $$1$$, $$0$$, $$-1$$,
$$0$$. The table fills in the values at every multiple of $$\frac{\pi}{6}$$ in between, from
:ref{#quarter-values}, :ref{#sin-cos-30-60} and the table round the whole circle in
[Identities you can see](./symmetries.mdx). Cosine, how far across the point is, has its own column.

| $$x$$               | $$\sin x$$            | $$\approx$$ | $$\cos x$$            | $$\approx$$ |
| ------------------- | --------------------- | ----------- | --------------------- | ----------- |
| $$0$$               | $$0$$                 | $$0$$       | $$1$$                 | $$1$$       |
| $$\frac{\pi}{6}$$   | $$\frac{1}{2}$$       | $$0.5$$     | $$\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$  | $$0.87$$    |
| $$\frac{\pi}{3}$$   | $$\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$  | $$0.87$$    | $$\frac{1}{2}$$       | $$0.5$$     |
| $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$   | $$1$$                 | $$1$$       | $$0$$                 | $$0$$       |
| $$\frac{2\pi}{3}$$  | $$\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$  | $$0.87$$    | $$-\frac{1}{2}$$      | $$-0.5$$    |
| $$\frac{5\pi}{6}$$  | $$\frac{1}{2}$$       | $$0.5$$     | $$-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$ | $$-0.87$$   |
| $$\pi$$             | $$0$$                 | $$0$$       | $$-1$$                | $$-1$$      |
| $$\frac{7\pi}{6}$$  | $$-\frac{1}{2}$$      | $$-0.5$$    | $$-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$ | $$-0.87$$   |
| $$\frac{4\pi}{3}$$  | $$-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$ | $$-0.87$$   | $$-\frac{1}{2}$$      | $$-0.5$$    |

Plot each pair $$(x, \sin x)$$ from the first two columns as a point, and the thirteen dots trace
out one wave. The curve through them is drawn from many more values worked out by a computer. Its
smooth shape between the dots is taken from that picture here, not proved.

<SineGraph caption="The thirteen points (x, sin x) from the table, with the curve through them: up to 1 at π/2, down to −1 at 3π/2, back to 0 at 2π." />

:::definition[Graph of sine and cosine]{#trig-graphs}

The **graph of sine** is the graph of the function $$x \mapsto \sin x$$ on all real numbers
(:ref{#functions/function-graph}): the set of all points $$(x, \sin x)$$. The **graph of cosine** is
the graph of the function $$x \mapsto \cos x$$, the set of all points $$(x, \cos x)$$.

:::

The point on the circle and the point on the graph share the same height. Move the slider below and
a dashed line carries the height from the circle across to the graph, which fills in as the point
turns. With the cosine button, the across distance is first stood upright by a quarter turn about
$$O$$, and then carried across in the same way.

<WaveTracer />

Plotting the points $$(x, \cos x)$$ from the table in the same way gives the graph of cosine. It is
the same shape of wave, but it starts at the top: $$1$$ at $$x = 0$$, down through $$0$$ at
$$\frac{\pi}{2}$$ to $$-1$$ at $$\pi$$, and back up to $$1$$ at $$2\pi$$.

<CosineGraph caption="The thirteen points (x, cos x) from the table, with the curve through them: down from 1 at 0 to −1 at π, back to 1 at 2π." />

Both waves stay between the lines $$y = 1$$ and $$y = -1$$, since every point of the unit circle is
at most $$1$$ from each axis (:ref{#sine-and-cosine-bounds}).

## The wave repeats

After a full turn the point on the circle is back where it started, so it goes round again through
the same heights in the same order. On the graph, the wave from $$2\pi$$ to $$4\pi$$ is a copy of
the wave from $$0$$ to $$2\pi$$, slid along by $$2\pi$$. The same goes backwards: turning by
negative angles takes the point clockwise through the same heights.

<SineRepeats caption="The graph of sine from −2π to 4π. Each stretch of length 2π holds a copy of the solid wave from 0 to 2π." />

For example, $$\sin\frac{13\pi}{6} = \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{6} + 2\pi\right) = \frac12$$, the same
height as at $$\frac{\pi}{6}$$. A wave that repeats like this gets a name, and so does the distance
after which it repeats.

:::definition[Period]{#period}

Let $$f$$ be a function defined at every real number (:ref{#functions/function}), such as
$$x \mapsto \sin x$$ or $$x \mapsto \sin 2x$$. A number $$p > 0$$ is a **period** of $$f$$ if

```math
f(x + p) = f(x) \quad \text{for every real number } x
```

A function with a period is called **periodic**.

:::

By :ref{#periodic} with $$n = 1$$, $$\sin(x + 2\pi) = \sin x$$ and $$\cos(x + 2\pi) = \cos x$$ for
every $$x$$. So $$2\pi$$ is a period of sine and of cosine. On the graph, sliding by $$2\pi$$ to the
right sends each point $$(x, \sin x)$$ to $$(x + 2\pi, \sin x)$$, which is again a point of the
graph.

A function with one period has many: if $$2\pi$$ is a period, so are $$4\pi$$ and $$6\pi$$, since
two full turns bring the point back too. When people say "the period of sine is $$2\pi$$", they mean
the smallest one. Nothing smaller than $$2\pi$$ works, because sine reaches its top, the height
$$1$$, once in each full turn.

:::proposition[The smallest period of sine and cosine]{#graphs-least-period}

Every period of sine is a whole multiple of $$2\pi$$, so $$2\pi$$ is the smallest one. The same
holds for cosine.

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Sine_and_Cosine_are_Periodic_on_Reals"}

Let $$p$$ be a period of sine. Put $$x = \frac{\pi}{2}$$ into the definition, and use
:ref{#quarter-values} for $$\sin\frac{\pi}{2}$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
\sin\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2} + p\right) &= \sin\tfrac{\pi}{2} && \text{since } p \text{ is a period} \\
&= 1 && \text{the value at a quarter turn}
\end{aligned}
```

Let $$m$$ be the largest whole number with $$2\pi m \le \frac{\pi}{2} + p$$, and write
$$\frac{\pi}{2} + p = y + 2\pi m$$, so that $$0 \le y < 2\pi$$. By :ref{#periodic}, $$\sin y = 1$$.
By :ref{#pythagorean-identity}, $$\cos^2 y = 1 - 1 = 0$$, so $$\cos y = 0$$ and the point
$$(\cos y, \sin y)$$ is $$E_2 = (0, 1)$$.

By :ref{#unit-circle-point}, exactly one angle $$y$$ with $$0 \le y < 2\pi$$ gives $$E_2$$, and
:ref{#quarter-values} says it is $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$. So $$y = \frac{\pi}{2}$$ and $$p = 2\pi m$$.
Since $$p > 0$$, $$m$$ is at least $$1$$, so $$p \ge 2\pi$$.

For cosine, the same argument with $$x = 0$$ works: a period $$p$$ gives $$\cos p = \cos 0 = 1$$, so
$$\sin p = 0$$, the point is $$E_1$$, and $$p$$ is a whole multiple of $$2\pi$$.

:::

## Cosine is sine slid sideways

Put the two waves on the same axes and they look alike. Cosine reaches its top at $$0$$ and sine at
$$\frac{\pi}{2}$$; cosine reaches its bottom at $$\pi$$ and sine at $$\frac{3\pi}{2}$$. Everything
sine does, cosine does a quarter turn earlier.

<CosineShift caption="Sine in blue and cosine in orange. Sliding the sine wave left by π/2 lays it exactly on the cosine wave." />

The circle says why. Turn the point a further quarter turn: the quarter turn sends $$(a, b)$$ to
$$(-b, a)$$, so the across distance $$a$$ of the point now becomes the height of the point a quarter
turn on. That is :ref{#quarter-turn-identities} read from right to left.

:::proposition[Cosine is a shifted sine]{#cosine-shift}

For every real number $$x$$,

```math
\cos x = \sin\left(x + \frac{\pi}{2}\right)
```

So the slide by $$\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, 0\right)$$, which moves every point $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$ to the
left, sends the graph of sine onto the graph of cosine.

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Sine_of_Angle_plus_Right_Angle"}

The equation is the second part of :ref{#quarter-turn-identities}, which says
$$\sin\left(x + \frac{\pi}{2}\right) = \cos x$$.

For the graphs, use part 1 of :ref{#functions/function-graph-moves} with $$h = -\frac{\pi}{2}$$ and
$$k = 0$$. The slide by $$\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, 0\right)$$ sends the graph of sine onto the graph of
$$x \mapsto \sin\left(x + \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$$, and by the equation above that is the function
$$x \mapsto \cos x$$.

:::

Check it with $$x = \frac{\pi}{6}$$. The left side is $$\cos\frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$. The
right side is $$\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{6} + \frac{\pi}{2}\right) = \sin\frac{2\pi}{3}$$, which the
table gives as $$\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$ too.

## Symmetry of the two graphs

Fold the cosine graph along the $$y$$-axis and the two halves match: the wave at $$-x$$ is as high
as the wave at $$x$$. The sine graph has a different symmetry. Turn it half a turn about the origin
and it lands on itself: the wave at $$-x$$ is as far below the axis as the wave at $$x$$ is above
it.

<WaveSymmetry caption="Top: the cosine graph is its own mirror image in the y-axis; the points at π/3 and −π/3 are both at height 1/2. Bottom: the sine graph is its own image under a half turn about the origin; the points at π/3 and −π/3 are at heights √3/2 and −√3/2." />

Both come from flipping the point on the unit circle in the $$x$$-axis. The flip turns the point for
$$x$$ into the point for $$-x$$, keeping its across distance and changing the sign of its height:
$$\cos(-x) = \cos x$$ and $$\sin(-x) = -\sin x$$ (:ref{#negative-angle}).

That makes cosine an even function and sine an odd one (:ref{#functions/even-odd-function}), like
$$x^2$$ and $$x^3$$.

:::proposition[Symmetries of the graphs]{#graphs-symmetry}

1. The flip $$R_2$$ in the $$y$$-axis is a symmetry (:ref{#isometries/symmetry}) of the graph of
   cosine.
2. The half turn $$H_O$$ about the origin is a symmetry of the graph of sine.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Even_and_odd_functions"}

Cosine is even and sine is odd, by :ref{#negative-angle}. For an even function the flip $$R_2$$ in
the $$y$$-axis is a symmetry of the graph, and for an odd function the half turn $$H_O$$ about the
origin is (:ref{#functions/even-odd-graphs}).

:::

Check with $$x = \frac{\pi}{3}$$: $$\cos\left(-\frac{\pi}{3}\right) = \cos\frac{5\pi}{3}$$ by
:ref{#periodic}, and the table gives $$\frac12$$, the same as $$\cos\frac{\pi}{3}$$. Likewise
$$\sin\left(-\frac{\pi}{3}\right) = \sin\frac{5\pi}{3} = -\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$, the opposite of
$$\sin\frac{\pi}{3}$$.

## Taller, squeezed and slid waves

A bigger Ferris wheel gives a taller wave, and a faster one gives more waves in the same time. Each
change to the wheel changes the formula in its own way. Multiplying the sine by a number makes the
wave taller, and multiplying $$x$$ inside the sine squeezes the wave sideways.

### Taller waves

On the Ferris wheel of radius $$20$$ m, the car after a turn by $$x$$ is at
$$(20\cos x, 20\sin x)$$, measured from the centre (:ref{#circle-point-radius}). So its height is
$$20\sin x$$: every height on the sine wave times $$20$$. The wave keeps its period, $$2\pi$$, and
crosses zero at the same places, but it now reaches up to $$20$$ and down to $$-20$$.

In the same way, for a number $$a > 0$$ the graph of $$y = a\sin x$$ rises to $$a$$ and falls to
$$-a$$. The number $$a$$ is the **amplitude** of the wave. Its graph is the graph of sine stretched
by $$a$$ away from the $$x$$-axis, which moves each point $$(x, y)$$ to $$(x, ay)$$ (part 2 of
:ref{#functions/function-graph-moves}).

<WaveTaller caption="The graph of y = 3 sin x is the sine wave stretched upwards by 3: amplitude 3, the same period 2π, the same crossings at 0, π and 2π." />

### Squeezed waves

Now imagine the wheel turning twice as fast. After time $$x$$ it has turned through $$2x$$, so the
height is $$\sin 2x$$. At $$x = \frac{\pi}{4}$$ the turn is already $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$ and the car is
at the top; at $$x = \frac{\pi}{2}$$ the turn is $$\pi$$ and the car is level with the centre; at
$$x = \pi$$ the car has been all the way round. So one whole wave fits between $$0$$ and $$\pi$$,
and two fit between $$0$$ and $$2\pi$$. In the words of :ref{#functions/function-graph-moves}, part
3 with $$a = \frac12$$, the graph of $$\sin 2x$$ is the sine wave squeezed towards the $$y$$-axis to
half its width.

<WaveSqueezed caption="The graph of y = sin 2x fits two whole waves between 0 and 2π, each π long. Its tops are at π/4 and 5π/4." />

One slip to watch for: $$\sin 2x$$ and $$2\sin x$$ are different waves. The first is squeezed and
the second is stretched. At $$x = \frac{\pi}{2}$$, $$\sin 2x = \sin\pi = 0$$, but $$2\sin x = 2$$.

:::proposition[Period of a squeezed wave]{#squeezed-period}

Let $$b > 0$$. Then $$\frac{2\pi}{b}$$ is a period of $$x \mapsto \sin bx$$ and of
$$x \mapsto \cos bx$$, and every period of either is at least $$\frac{2\pi}{b}$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_function#Properties"}

**A period.** For every $$x$$, using :ref{#periodic},

```math
\begin{aligned}
\sin\left(b\left(x + \tfrac{2\pi}{b}\right)\right) &= \sin(bx + 2\pi) && \text{multiplying out the bracket} \\
&= \sin bx && \text{a full turn more}
\end{aligned}
```

The same lines with $$\cos$$ in place of $$\sin$$ work for cosine.

**No smaller period.** Let $$p$$ be a period of $$x \mapsto \sin bx$$. For every number $$t$$, put
$$x = \frac{t}{b}$$ into the definition of a period:

```math
\begin{aligned}
\sin(t + bp) &= \sin\left(b\left(\tfrac{t}{b} + p\right)\right) && \text{multiplying out the bracket} \\
&= \sin\left(b \cdot \tfrac{t}{b}\right) && \text{since } p \text{ is a period} \\
&= \sin t
\end{aligned}
```

So $$bp > 0$$ is a period of sine, and by :ref{#graphs-least-period}, $$bp \ge 2\pi$$. Dividing by
$$b > 0$$ gives $$p \ge \frac{2\pi}{b}$$. The same argument works for cosine.

:::

So the number $$b$$ counts how many whole waves fit into a stretch of length $$2\pi$$. A number
$$b$$ between $$0$$ and $$1$$ fits less than one wave, so the wave is longer than $$2\pi$$.

:::example[Three waves]

- **$$y = \sin 2x$$**: amplitude $$1$$ and period $$\frac{2\pi}{2} = \pi$$. It is $$0$$ at $$0$$,
  $$1$$ at $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$, $$0$$ at $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$, $$-1$$ at $$\frac{3\pi}{4}$$ and $$0$$
  again at $$\pi$$.
- **$$y = 3\sin x$$**: amplitude $$3$$ and period $$2\pi$$. It is $$3$$ at $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$ and
  $$-3$$ at $$\frac{3\pi}{2}$$. At $$\frac{\pi}{6}$$ it is $$3 \cdot \frac12 = 1.5$$.
- **$$y = \cos\frac{x}{2}$$**: here $$b = \frac12$$, so the period is $$\frac{2\pi}{1/2} = 4\pi$$.
  It starts at $$\cos 0 = 1$$, is $$\cos\frac{\pi}{2} = 0$$ at $$x = \pi$$, reaches $$\cos\pi = -1$$
  at $$x = 2\pi$$, and climbs back to $$1$$ at $$x = 4\pi$$.

:::

<WaveWidened caption="The graph of y = cos(x/2) takes 4π to make one whole wave, twice as long as cosine." />

### Slid waves

A wheel that starts a little late reaches every height a little later. The wave
$$y = \sin\left(x - \frac{\pi}{3}\right)$$ reaches at $$x = \frac{5\pi}{6}$$ the height that sine
reaches at $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$, since $$\frac{5\pi}{6} - \frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{\pi}{2}$$. So its graph
is the sine wave slid right by $$\frac{\pi}{3}$$.

<WaveSlid caption="The graph of y = sin(x − π/3) is the sine wave slid right by π/3. Its top moves from π/2 to 5π/6." />

In general, for a number $$c$$ the graph of $$y = \sin(x - c)$$ is the graph of sine slid right by
$$c$$ (part 1 of :ref{#functions/function-graph-moves}). The minus sign inside means a slide to the
right, which can feel backwards at first. :ref{#cosine-shift} is the case $$c = -\frac{\pi}{2}$$:
$$\sin\left(x + \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$$ is the sine wave slid left by $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$.

## Where sine and cosine are zero

The sine wave crosses the $$x$$-axis at $$0$$, $$\pi$$, $$2\pi$$, and on in both directions, once
every half turn. On the circle, the height is $$0$$ exactly when the point is on the $$x$$-axis, and
the unit circle meets the $$x$$-axis at two points: $$(1, 0)$$ on the right and $$(-1, 0)$$ on the
left. The point passes one of them every half turn.

<TrigZeros caption="Sine (blue) is zero at every whole multiple of π. Cosine (orange) is zero halfway between, at π/2 plus a whole multiple of π." />

```plane
scale 2.5
point O 0 0 sw
point E1 1 0 e
point -E1 -1 0 w
point E2 0 1 n
point -E2 0 -1 s
circle O 1 color=muted
segment -E1 E1 color=blue
segment -E2 E2 color=orange
caption "The points of the unit circle at height 0 are E1 and −E1, on the blue axis. The points with across distance 0 are E2 and −E2, on the orange axis."
```

For cosine the picture is the same with across and up swapped: the across distance is $$0$$ exactly
when the point is at the top, $$(0, 1)$$, or the bottom, $$(0, -1)$$. The point reaches one of these
every half turn too, starting a quarter turn in.

:::proposition[Where sine and cosine are zero]{#trig-zeros}

1. $$\sin x = 0$$ exactly when $$x = n\pi$$ for some integer $$n$$.
2. $$\cos x = 0$$ exactly when $$x = \frac{\pi}{2} + n\pi$$ for some integer $$n$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Zeroes_of_Sine_and_Cosine"}

**Part 1.** Suppose $$\sin x = 0$$. Let $$m$$ be the largest whole number with $$2\pi m \le x$$, and
write $$x = y + 2\pi m$$, so that $$0 \le y < 2\pi$$. By :ref{#periodic} and
:ref{#pythagorean-identity},

```math
\begin{aligned}
\sin y &= \sin x = 0 && \text{whole turns repeat} \\
\cos^2 y &= 1 - \sin^2 y = 1 && \text{sine squared plus cosine squared}
\end{aligned}
```

So $$\cos y$$ is $$1$$ or $$-1$$, and the point $$(\cos y, \sin y)$$ is $$E_1 = (1, 0)$$ or
$$-E_1 = (-1, 0)$$. By :ref{#quarter-values}, these are the points for $$y = 0$$ and $$y = \pi$$,
and by :ref{#unit-circle-point} no other $$y$$ with $$0 \le y < 2\pi$$ gives them. So $$y = 0$$ and
$$x = 2m \cdot \pi$$, or $$y = \pi$$ and $$x = (2m + 1)\pi$$. Either way $$x$$ is a whole multiple
of $$\pi$$.

Conversely, every integer $$n$$ is $$2m$$ or $$2m + 1$$ for some integer $$m$$
(:ref{#numbers/even-or-odd}). By :ref{#periodic} and :ref{#quarter-values},

```math
\sin(2m\pi) = \sin 0 = 0 \qquad \sin\big((2m + 1)\pi\big) = \sin(\pi + 2m\pi) = \sin\pi = 0
```

**Part 2.** By :ref{#cosine-shift}, $$\cos x = \sin\left(x + \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$$. By part 1, that
is $$0$$ exactly when $$x + \frac{\pi}{2} = k\pi$$ for some integer $$k$$, that is, when

```math
x = k\pi - \frac{\pi}{2} = \frac{\pi}{2} + (k - 1)\pi
```

Writing $$n = k - 1$$, which runs through all the integers as $$k$$ does, gives
$$x = \frac{\pi}{2} + n\pi$$.

:::

For example, $$\sin 5\pi = 0$$ and
$$\cos\frac{7\pi}{2} = \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2} + 3\pi\right) = 0$$. But $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$ is not a
whole multiple of $$\pi$$, so $$\sin\frac{\pi}{2}$$ is not $$0$$: it is $$1$$.

## Waves in the world

A sine wave also turns up, at least roughly, in things that repeat on a steady cycle without any
wheel in sight. The length of the day is one example. In London the shortest day, in late December,
has about $$7.8$$ hours of daylight and the longest, in late June, about $$16.6$$ hours. Over a year
the hours of daylight rise and fall roughly like a sine wave of period $$365$$ days.

The middle of the two extremes is $$12.2$$ hours and half the gap is $$4.4$$ hours. Counting $$t$$
in days from the start of the year, with day $$80$$ about 21 March, a good rough model is

```math
\text{hours of daylight} \approx 12.2 + 4.4 \sin\left(\frac{2\pi}{365}(t - 80)\right)
```

By :ref{#squeezed-period} with $$b = \frac{2\pi}{365}$$, its period is
$$\frac{2\pi}{2\pi/365} = 365$$ days. On 21 June, day $$172$$, the angle inside is about $$1.58$$,
close to $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$, and the model gives about $$12.2 + 4.4 = 16.6$$ hours. The real figures
wobble a little around the curve, but the shape is right.

Sound is another. A tuning fork for the note A shakes $$440$$ times a second, and the air pressure
next to it rises and falls close to a sine wave of period $$\frac{1}{440}$$ of a second. In the
formula $$\sin(880\pi t)$$, with $$t$$ in seconds, :ref{#squeezed-period} gives that period:
$$\frac{2\pi}{880\pi} = \frac{1}{440}$$.

   $$\sin\left(-\frac{3\pi}{2}\right) = \sin\frac{\pi}{2} = 1$$. On the circle, turning three

::::exercise{#ex-graphs-zeros-squeezed}

Find every $$x$$ with $$0 \le x \le 2\pi$$ and $$\sin 2x = 0$$.

:::solution

By :ref{#trig-zeros}, $$\sin 2x = 0$$ exactly when $$2x = n\pi$$ for an integer $$n$$, that is,
$$x = \frac{n\pi}{2}$$. Between $$0$$ and $$2\pi$$ that gives $$n = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4$$:

```math
x = 0, \quad \frac{\pi}{2}, \quad \pi, \quad \frac{3\pi}{2}, \quad 2\pi
```

That is twice as many as for $$\sin x$$, which fits with the squeezed wave making two whole waves in
the same stretch.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-graphs-cos-half-zeros}

Find every $$x$$ with $$\cos\frac{x}{2} = 0$$. Check one of your answers with the table.

:::solution

By part 2 of :ref{#trig-zeros}, $$\cos\frac{x}{2} = 0$$ exactly when
$$\frac{x}{2} = \frac{\pi}{2} + n\pi$$ for an integer $$n$$. Multiplying both sides by $$2$$ gives

```math
x = \pi + 2n\pi
```

so the answers are the odd multiples of $$\pi$$: $$\dots, -3\pi, -\pi, \pi, 3\pi, \dots$$. As a
check, $$x = \pi$$ gives $$\cos\frac{\pi}{2} = 0$$, which is in the table.

::::exercise{#ex-graphs-sine-from-cosine}

Show that $$\sin x = \cos\left(x - \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$$ for every $$x$$, so the graph of sine is
the graph of cosine slid right by $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$. Check it with $$x = \frac{\pi}{6}$$.

:::solution

:ref{#cosine-shift} holds for every number, so put $$x - \frac{\pi}{2}$$ in place of $$x$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
\cos\left(x - \tfrac{\pi}{2}\right) &= \sin\left(\left(x - \tfrac{\pi}{2}\right) + \tfrac{\pi}{2}\right) && \text{cosine is a shifted sine} \\
&= \sin x && \text{since } -\tfrac{\pi}{2} + \tfrac{\pi}{2} = 0
\end{aligned}
```

By part 1 of :ref{#functions/function-graph-moves} with $$h = \frac{\pi}{2}$$ and $$k = 0$$, the
graph of $$x \mapsto \cos\left(x - \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$$ is the cosine wave slid right by
$$\frac{\pi}{2}$$. As a check, with $$x = \frac{\pi}{6}$$ the sine is
$$\sin\frac{\pi}{6} = \frac12$$, and the cosine is
$$\cos\left(-\frac{\pi}{3}\right) = \cos\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac12$$ by :ref{#negative-angle}.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-graphs-not-a-period}

Show with one value of $$x$$ that $$2\pi$$ is not a period of $$x \mapsto \sin\frac{x}{2}$$. What is
its smallest period?

:::solution

A period must work for every $$x$$, so one $$x$$ where it fails is enough. Take $$x = \pi$$:

```math
\sin\frac{\pi}{2} = 1 \qquad \sin\frac{\pi + 2\pi}{2} = \sin\frac{3\pi}{2} = -1
```

These differ, so $$2\pi$$ is not a period. By :ref{#squeezed-period} with $$b = \frac12$$, the
smallest period is $$\frac{2\pi}{1/2} = 4\pi$$: the wave is twice as long as sine.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-graphs-ferris}

A Ferris wheel of radius $$15$$ m has its centre $$17$$ m above the ground, and turns anticlockwise
once every $$10$$ minutes. Your car starts level with the centre, on the right. After $$t$$ minutes
its height above the ground is

```math
h = 17 + 15\sin\frac{\pi t}{5}
```

1. Explain where each number in the formula comes from.
2. Find $$h$$ after $$2.5$$, $$5$$ and $$7.5$$ minutes, and after $$50$$ seconds.

:::solution

1. The wheel turns $$2\pi$$ in $$10$$ minutes, so after $$t$$ minutes it has turned
   $$\frac{2\pi t}{10} = \frac{\pi t}{5}$$. By :ref{#circle-point-radius}, the car is then
   $$15\sin\frac{\pi t}{5}$$ above the centre, and the centre is $$17$$ m up. The period is
   $$\frac{2\pi}{\pi/5} = 10$$ minutes (:ref{#squeezed-period}), one turn of the wheel.
2. At $$t = 2.5$$: $$\sin\frac{\pi}{2} = 1$$, so $$h = 32$$ m, the top. At $$t = 5$$:
   $$\sin\pi = 0$$, so $$h = 17$$ m. At $$t = 7.5$$: $$\sin\frac{3\pi}{2} = -1$$, so $$h = 2$$ m,
   the bottom. Fifty seconds is $$\frac56$$ of a minute, and
   $$\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{5} \cdot \frac{5}{6}\right) = \sin\frac{\pi}{6} = \frac12$$, so
   $$h = 17 + 7.5 = 24.5$$ m.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-graphs-sine-squared}

Show that $$\pi$$ is a period of $$x \mapsto \sin^2 x$$, even though it is not a period of sine.
Check with $$x = \frac{\pi}{6}$$.

:::solution

Recall that $$\sin^2 x$$ means $$(\sin x)^2$$. For every $$x$$, using :ref{#half-turn-identities},

```math
\begin{aligned}
\sin^2(x + \pi) &= \big(\sin(x + \pi)\big)^2 && \text{what the notation means} \\
&= (-\sin x)^2 && \text{a half turn more} \\
&= \sin^2 x && \text{a square doesn't change when the sign does}
\end{aligned}
```

So $$\pi$$ is a period. It is not a period of sine: at $$x = \frac{\pi}{2}$$,
$$\sin\frac{3\pi}{2} = -1$$ but $$\sin\frac{\pi}{2} = 1$$. Squaring removes the sign, so the dips
below the axis flip up and match the humps above. As a check,
$$\sin^2\frac{7\pi}{6} = \left(-\frac12\right)^2 = \frac14 = \sin^2\frac{\pi}{6}$$.

:::
