title: Lines and rays
description: Lines, rays, parallel and perpendicular lines, and rectangles.

Look at a tiled floor. The lines between the tiles run dead straight, and some of them run side by
side without ever meeting, while others cross them square on. Straight lines are the other basic
material of plane geometry, next to distance.

In this guide we'll take a few facts about lines as given: how lines pass through points, and how
parallel and perpendicular lines behave. From them we'll prove when two lines meet, define rays and
rectangles, and show that opposite sides of a rectangle are equal. We'll also set out the rules for
area that later guides use to measure angles and prove the Pythagoras theorem.

## Lines through points

Hammer two nails into a board and stretch a string tight between them. However you do it, the string
takes the same path, so two points fix a straight line. Two lines drawn on a page either cross once
or run side by side forever without meeting.

Two different lines are **parallel** if they never meet. It is convenient to count a line as
parallel to itself too, so that the rules below have no exceptions. You write $$L_1 \parallel L_2$$
when lines $$L_1$$ and $$L_2$$ are parallel.

```plane
point A 0 0 hidden
point B 3 2 hidden
point C 0 2 hidden
point D 3 0 hidden
point X 1.5 1 s hidden
line A B extend=0.4
line C D extend=0.4
text 3.6 2.75 "L_1"
text 3.6 -0.75 "L_2"
point E 5.5 0.2 hidden
point F 8.5 1.2 hidden
point G 5.5 1.6 hidden
point H 8.5 2.6 hidden
line E F extend=0.4
line G H extend=0.4
text 9.3 1.35 "L_2"
text 9.3 2.75 "L_1"
text 1.5 -1.2 "cross at one point"
text 7 -1.2 "parallel: never meet"
caption "Two different lines either cross at exactly one point or are parallel"
```

Two facts about lines are taken as given. The first says that two points fix a line. The second says
that a line has exactly one parallel through each point. The
[Segments, rays and lines](../segments-rays-and-lines/rules-proved.mdx) chapter proves both from
coordinates.

:::remark[Rules for lines]{#line-rules}

1. **Two points, one line**: two different points $$P$$ and $$Q$$ lie on exactly one line, called
   the line $$PQ$$
2. **One parallel through a point**: for any line $$L$$ and point $$P$$, exactly one line through
   $$P$$ is parallel to $$L$$

These are taken as given.

::see-also{src="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_postulate#Equivalent_properties"}

:::

```plane
point P 0 0 sw
point Q 4 1.5 se
line P Q extend=1
caption "Rule 1: the points P and Q lie on exactly one line, the line PQ"
```

In the next figure, $$P$$ is a point off the line $$L$$. Tilt a line through $$P$$ even a little,
and it crosses $$L$$ somewhere, perhaps far off the page. Exactly one position never meets $$L$$:
the dashed line, which is the parallel that rule 2 promises.

```plane
point A 0 0 hidden
point B 6 0 hidden
point P 3 2 n
point C 0 2 hidden
point D 6 2 hidden
point X 0.8 0 hidden
point Y 5.6 0 hidden
line A B extend=0.6
line X P extend=0.15 color=muted
line Y P extend=0.15 color=muted
line C D extend=0.6 dashed color=blue
text 7 0.35 "L"
caption "Rule 2: every line through P crosses L except one, the dashed parallel"
```

## Lines on a globe

Rule 2 is what makes this geometry flat. The surface of a globe shows what happens without it. On a
globe, the shortest route between two points runs along a **great circle**: a circle that goes all
the way round the globe, with its centre at the centre of the globe. The equator is one, and so is
each line of longitude together with the one opposite it. Long flights follow routes close to great
circles, so on a globe great circles play the part of straight lines.

Any two great circles cross. A great circle goes all the way round, so with each point it also
passes through the opposite point of the globe. A great circle that never crossed the equator would
stay in one half, the northern or the southern. But opposite points always lie in different halves,
so it can't. Tilt the blue great circle below as close to the equator as you like: it still crosses
at two opposite points.

<GreatCircles />

So on a globe, no line through a point off the equator is parallel to the equator, and rule 2 fails.
The circles of latitude never meet the equator, but they are not great circles. Someone walking
along one has to keep turning towards the nearer pole to stay on it, so it is not straight. Rule 1
fails too: the north and south poles lie on every line of longitude, so they lie on many lines.

Results built on these rules fail with them. The [Angles in triangles](./triangle-angles.mdx) page
proves that the angles of a flat triangle add up to $$180^\circ$$. On a globe, the triangle below
has a right angle at every corner, so its angles add up to $$270^\circ$$.

<GlobeTriangle />

## Crossing and parallel lines

Two facts about lines follow from the rules, and each proof is a short argument by contradiction
(:ref{#basic-proofs/contradiction}). The first says when two lines meet.

:::proposition[When two lines meet]{#crossing-lines}

Two different lines meet in at most one point. So two lines that are not parallel meet in exactly
one point.

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Two_Lines_Meet_at_Unique_Point"}

Suppose two different lines met at two different points $$P$$ and $$Q$$. Then both lines pass
through $$P$$ and $$Q$$. By rule 1 of :ref{#line-rules}, only one line does, so the two lines would
be the same. This contradicts the lines being different, so they meet in at most one point.

Now take two lines that are not parallel. A line counts as parallel to itself, so the two lines are
different. Different lines that never meet are parallel by definition, so these two must meet
somewhere. By the first part, they meet in exactly one point.

:::

The second fact says that being parallel passes along a chain of lines. If one line is parallel to a
second, and the second is parallel to a third, then the first is parallel to the third.

:::proposition[Parallel to a parallel]{#parallel-transitive}

If $$L_1 \parallel L_2$$ and $$L_2 \parallel L_3$$, then $$L_1 \parallel L_3$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Parallelism_is_Transitive_Relation"}

If $$L_1$$ and $$L_3$$ are the same line, they are parallel, since a line counts as parallel to
itself. Otherwise, suppose they met at a point $$P$$. Then $$L_1$$ and $$L_3$$ would be two
different lines through $$P$$, both parallel to $$L_2$$. By rule 2 of :ref{#line-rules}, only one
line through $$P$$ is parallel to $$L_2$$, which is a contradiction. So $$L_1$$ and $$L_3$$ never
meet, and they are parallel.

:::

## Rays

A torch beam starts at the torch and runs on in one direction without end. That is a ray: half of a
line, cut off at a starting point. The definition uses the idea of one point lying between two
others, from the rules for segments on the [Distance](./distance.mdx) page.

:::definition[Ray]{#ray}

Let $$P$$ and $$Q$$ be different points. The **ray** $$PQ$$ is made up of $$P$$ together with every
point $$X$$ of the line $$PQ$$ for which $$P$$ does not lie between $$X$$ and $$Q$$. The point $$P$$
is its **starting point**.

:::

In words, the ray $$PQ$$ starts at $$P$$, passes through $$Q$$ and carries on past it. The points of
the line on the other side of $$P$$ are left out. The starting point always comes first in the name,
so the ray $$PQ$$ and the ray $$QP$$ are different: they start at different points and run in
opposite directions.

```plane
point P 0 0 s
point Q 2.5 0 s
point R 4.5 0 s
ray P Q extend=2.5
caption "The ray PQ starts at P and runs on through Q, so it is also the ray PR"
```

A ray is fixed by its starting point and any one other point on it. If $$R$$ is another point of the
ray $$PQ$$, then the ray $$PR$$ is the same ray.

## Perpendicular lines

Fold a sheet of paper in half, then fold it in half again across the first crease. The two creases
meet square on, like the corner of the page. Lines that cross this way are perpendicular.

:::definition[Perpendicular lines]{#perpendicular}

Two lines are **perpendicular** if they cross and the four angles they make at the crossing are all
the same size. Angles are defined precisely on the [Angles](./angles.mdx) page. You write
$$L_1 \perp L_2$$. Each of these four angles is a **right angle**, which the
[Measuring angles](./measuring-angles.mdx) page shows is $$90^\circ$$.

:::

Given a line $$L$$ and a point $$P$$, the line through $$P$$ perpendicular to $$L$$ meets $$L$$ at a
point called the **foot** of the perpendicular. Two facts about perpendicular lines are taken as
given.

:::remark[Rules for perpendicular lines]{#perpendicular-rules}

1. **One perpendicular through a point**: for any line $$L$$ and point $$P$$, exactly one line
   through $$P$$ is perpendicular to $$L$$
2. **Perpendicular to parallels**: if $$L_1 \perp L_2$$ and $$L_2 \parallel L_3$$, then
   $$L_1 \perp L_3$$

These are taken as given.

::see-also{src="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perpendicular#In_relationship_to_parallel_lines"}

:::

```plane
point A -2.5 0 hidden
point B 2.5 0 hidden
line A B
point F 0 0 se
point P 0 2.5 ne
point G 0 -1 hidden
line G P color=blue
right B F P
caption "The perpendicular from P meets the line at its foot F"
```

Perpendicular lines give a practical way to draw parallel lines. A carpenter cutting a board into
strips needs the cuts to be parallel, or each strip would get narrower towards one end. To mark
them, the carpenter uses a set square: a flat triangle with one right angle. With one short side
held flat against the straight edge of the board, the other short side stands at a right angle to
the edge.

Drawing along that upright side marks a line perpendicular to the edge. The carpenter then slides
the square along the edge and draws again, which marks a second line, also perpendicular to the
edge. In the figure, $$L_1$$ is the first line, and the square is drawing $$L_3$$.

<CarpenterBoard caption="Slide the set square along the edge, and each line it draws is perpendicular to the edge" />

Here is why the two lines never meet. Both point straight up from the edge, so neither leans towards
the other, and they stay the same distance apart all the way up. For two lines standing on the edge
to meet, one of them has to lean towards the other. Tilt one by any amount, as in the next figure,
and the two lines cross somewhere above the edge, even if that is far off the board.

```plane
point A -0.5 0 hidden
point B 6.5 0 hidden
point P 1.5 0 hidden
point P' 1.5 5.4 hidden
point Q 5 0 hidden
point M 1.5 5 nw
line A B extend=0.3
segment P P' color=blue
segment Q M color=orange
right Q P P'
angle M Q A label="55°" color=orange r=0.7
text 7.1 0.35 "L_2"
text 1.05 3 "L_1"
text 3.75 2.5 "L_3"
caption "A line that leans towards the other one meets it, here at M"
```

The carpenter is relying on a fact: two lines perpendicular to the same line never meet. The next
proposition proves it from rule 1 of :ref{#perpendicular-rules}.

:::proposition[Two perpendiculars are parallel]{#perpendiculars-parallel}

If $$L_1 \perp L_2$$ and $$L_2 \perp L_3$$, then $$L_1 \parallel L_3$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perpendicular#In_relationship_to_parallel_lines"}

If $$L_1$$ and $$L_3$$ are the same line, they are parallel. Otherwise, suppose they met at a point
$$P$$, as in the figure below. Then $$L_1$$ and $$L_3$$ would be two different lines through $$P$$,
both perpendicular to $$L_2$$. By rule 1 of :ref{#perpendicular-rules}, only one line through $$P$$
is perpendicular to $$L_2$$, which is a contradiction. So $$L_1$$ and $$L_3$$ never meet, and they
are parallel.

```plane
point A -0.5 0 hidden
point B 6.5 0 hidden
point P 3 3 n
point X 1.6 0 hidden
point Y 4.4 0 hidden
line A B extend=0.3
ray P X extend=0.4 color=blue
ray P Y extend=0.4 color=blue
text 7.3 0.35 "L_2"
text 1.75 1.9 "L_1"
text 4.25 1.9 "L_3"
text 1.6 0.55 "90°?"
text 4.4 0.55 "90°?"
caption "If the two lines met at P, there would be two perpendiculars to the edge through P, which rule 1 rules out"
```

:::

## Distance between parallel lines

Look down on a railway track from above. The two steel rails are parallel, and the wooden sleepers
laid square across them hold the rails a fixed distance apart. Measure the gap between the rails at
any sleeper and you get the same answer. If the rails crept closer together at some point, the
wheels of a train, which are a fixed distance apart on their axle, would come off there.

<RailwayTrack caption="Seen from above: the sleepers hold the rails the same distance apart all along the track" />

Parallel lines in the plane behave like the rails, and this is the last fact about lines taken as
given. To measure the gap, go straight across: start at a point of one line and follow the
perpendicular until it reaches the other, like walking along a sleeper. A slanted route across is
longer, so the perpendicular is the fair way to measure. The
[Perpendicular bisectors](./perpendicular-bisectors.mdx) guide proves this.

:::remark[Distance between parallel lines]{#parallel-distance}

Let $$L$$ and $$L'$$ be parallel lines, and let $$P$$ and $$Q$$ be points of $$L$$. Let the lines
through $$P$$ and $$Q$$ perpendicular to $$L$$ meet $$L'$$ at $$P'$$ and $$Q'$$. Then

```math
d(P, P') = d(Q, Q')
```

This common length is the **distance between the lines**. It is taken as given.

::see-also{src="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_(geometry)#Euclidean_parallelism"}

:::

```plane
point A -1 0 hidden
point B 6 0 hidden
line A B
point C -1 2.5 hidden
point D 6 2.5 hidden
line C D
point P 0.5 0 s
point P' 0.5 2.5 n
point Q 4.5 0 s
point Q' 4.5 2.5 n
segment P P' color=blue
segment Q Q' color=blue
right B P P'
right B Q Q'
text 7 0.35 "L"
text 7 2.85 "L'"
caption "Perpendicular segments between two parallel lines all have the same length"
```

The rule takes for granted that the perpendicular lines meet $$L'$$, and they do. Each one is
perpendicular to $$L'$$ as well as to $$L$$, by rule 2 of :ref{#perpendicular-rules}, and
perpendicular lines cross by definition.

## Rectangles

A rectangle is the shape of a door, a page or a phone screen: four straight sides that meet at four
square corners. Its opposite sides look the same length, and the rules so far are enough to prove
it.

:::definition[Rectangle and square]{#rectangle}

Let $$A$$, $$B$$, $$C$$ and $$D$$ be four points. The figure made of the segments $$AB$$, $$BC$$,
$$CD$$ and $$DA$$ is a **rectangle** if each side is perpendicular to the two sides next to it. A
**square** is a rectangle whose four sides all have the same length.

:::

```plane
point A 0 0 sw
point B 5 0 se
point C 5 3 ne
point D 0 3 nw
segment A B label="a" side=right
segment B C label="b" side=right
segment C D
segment D A
right B A D
right C B A
right D C B
right A D C
caption "A rectangle with sides of lengths a and b"
```

The corners are listed in order around the figure, so $$AB$$ and $$CD$$ are opposite sides, and so
are $$BC$$ and $$DA$$. Opposite sides are parallel: $$AB$$ and $$CD$$ are both perpendicular to
$$BC$$, so they are parallel by :ref{#perpendiculars-parallel}. In the same way, $$BC$$ and $$DA$$
are both perpendicular to $$AB$$.

:::corollary[Opposite sides of a rectangle are equal]{#rectangle-sides}

In a rectangle $$ABCD$$, opposite sides have the same length:

```math
d(A, B) = d(D, C) \quad \text{and} \quad d(A, D) = d(B, C)
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectangle#Properties"}

The lines $$AB$$ and $$DC$$ are parallel, since both are perpendicular to $$BC$$. The line through
$$A$$ perpendicular to $$AB$$ is the line $$AD$$, which meets the line $$DC$$ at $$D$$. The line
through $$B$$ perpendicular to $$AB$$ is the line $$BC$$, which meets the line $$DC$$ at $$C$$. So
by :ref{#parallel-distance}, $$d(A, D) = d(B, C)$$.

The lines $$AD$$ and $$BC$$ are parallel too. The lines through $$A$$ and $$D$$ perpendicular to
$$AD$$ are the lines $$AB$$ and $$DC$$, which meet the line $$BC$$ at $$B$$ and $$C$$. So
:ref{#parallel-distance} gives $$d(A, B) = d(D, C)$$.

:::

So a rectangle has two side lengths, usually called $$a$$ and $$b$$, each used twice. A square has
one side length, used four times.

## Area

Area measures how much flat space a shape covers. With the unit of length fixed, the unit of area is
a square whose sides have length $$1$$. A rectangle $$3$$ by $$2$$ can be cut into $$3 \cdot 2$$
such squares, so its area is $$6$$.

When this chapter speaks of the area of a rectangle, a triangle or a disc, it means the area of the
region inside it. A rectangle is four segments, and the segments themselves cover no area. So two
pieces that share only an edge, like two tiles side by side, don't overlap.

:::remark[Rules for area]{#area-rules}

1. **Rectangles**: a rectangle with sides of lengths $$a$$ and $$b$$ has area $$ab$$, so a square
   with sides of length $$a$$ has area $$a^2$$
2. **Areas add**: if a region is cut into pieces that don't overlap, its area is the sum of the
   areas of the pieces

These are taken as given.

::see-also{src="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Area#Formal_definition"}

:::

```plane
point A 0 0 hidden
point B 5 0 hidden
point C 5 2 hidden
point D 2 2 hidden
point E 2 5 hidden
point F 0 5 hidden
point G 0 2 hidden
polygon A B C D E F
segment A B
segment B C
segment C D
segment D E
segment E F
segment F A
segment G D dashed
text 2.5 -0.45 "5"
text 5.4 1 "2"
text 1 5.45 "2"
text 2.4 3.5 "3"
text 2.5 1 "10"
text 1 3.5 "6"
caption "An L shape cut into two rectangles, of areas 10 and 6"
```

:::example[An L-shaped room]

A room has the L shape in the last figure. A dashed cut splits it into two rectangles that don't
overlap: one $$5$$ by $$2$$, and one $$2$$ by $$3$$. By rule 1 their areas are $$10$$ and $$6$$, so
by rule 2 the room has area $$10 + 6 = 16$$.

Cutting it another way gives the same answer. An upright cut through the inner corner gives a
rectangle $$2$$ by $$5$$ and a rectangle $$3$$ by $$2$$, with areas $$10$$ and $$6$$ again. Rule 2
promises that every cut agrees, since each total is the area of the same room.

:::

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on lines and rays, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-lines-same-ray}

Four different points $$A$$, $$B$$, $$C$$ and $$D$$ lie on one line in that order: $$B$$ lies
between $$A$$ and $$C$$, and $$C$$ lies between $$B$$ and $$D$$. Which of the rays $$BC$$, $$BD$$,
$$CB$$ and $$BA$$ are the same ray?

:::solution

Only the rays $$BC$$ and $$BD$$ are the same. The reasons use :ref{#ray} and rule 3 of
:ref{#segment-rules}, which says that of three points on a line, exactly one lies between the other
two:

- **$$BD$$ and $$BC$$**: of $$B$$, $$C$$ and $$D$$, the point between the others is $$C$$, so $$B$$
  does not lie between $$D$$ and $$C$$. So $$D$$ is a point of the ray $$BC$$, and a ray is fixed by
  its starting point and any other of its points. So the ray $$BD$$ is the ray $$BC$$.
- **$$BA$$**: $$B$$ lies between $$A$$ and $$C$$, so $$A$$ is not on the ray $$BC$$. But $$A$$ is on
  the ray $$BA$$. So these rays are different.
- **$$CB$$**: of $$A$$, $$B$$ and $$C$$, the point between the others is $$B$$, so $$C$$ does not
  lie between $$A$$ and $$B$$. So $$A$$ is on the ray $$CB$$ but not on the ray $$BC$$, and these
  rays are different too. The ray $$CB$$ also differs from $$BA$$, since it contains $$C$$ and the
  ray $$BA$$ does not.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-lines-third-line}

The lines $$L_1$$ and $$L_2$$ are different and parallel. A third line $$L_3$$ crosses $$L_1$$ at a
point $$P$$. Prove that $$L_3$$ meets $$L_2$$ in exactly one point.

:::solution

The line $$L_3$$ crosses $$L_1$$, so the two are different lines that meet. That means they are not
parallel.

Suppose $$L_3$$ were parallel to $$L_2$$ (:ref{#basic-proofs/contradiction}). Since
$$L_2 \parallel L_1$$, :ref{#parallel-transitive} gives $$L_3 \parallel L_1$$. This contradicts the
first paragraph, so $$L_3$$ is not parallel to $$L_2$$.

Two lines that are not parallel meet in exactly one point (:ref{#crossing-lines}). So $$L_3$$ meets
$$L_2$$ in exactly one point.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-lines-segments-dont-meet}

Two segments drawn on a page don't meet. Must the lines through them be parallel?

:::solution

No. A segment is only a piece of its line, and the lines can cross at a point that neither piece
reaches. In the figure, the two segments don't meet, but their lines, drawn dashed, cross at $$X$$.

```plane
point X 0 0 sw
point A 1.5 0 s
point B 4 0 s
point C 1.2 1.2 nw
point D 2.6 2.6 nw
segment A B
segment C D
segment X A dashed
segment X C dashed
caption "The segments AB and CD don't meet, but the lines through them cross at X"
```

So parallel lines are a fact about whole lines, which run on without end. Checking a short stretch
of each never settles it, which is why the carpenter relies on :ref{#perpendiculars-parallel} rather
than on how the marks look.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-lines-rectangle-sides}

A rectangle $$ABCD$$ has $$d(A, B) = 7$$ and $$d(B, C) = 3$$. Find $$d(C, D)$$ and $$d(D, A)$$, the
total length of the four sides, and the area of the rectangle.

:::solution

Opposite sides of a rectangle have the same length (:ref{#rectangle-sides}). So
$$d(D, C) = d(A, B) = 7$$ and $$d(A, D) = d(B, C) = 3$$.

The four sides add up to $$7 + 3 + 7 + 3 = 20$$. By rule 1 of :ref{#area-rules}, the area is
$$7 \cdot 3 = 21$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-lines-strip}

The lines $$L$$ and $$L'$$ are parallel, and the distance between them is $$4$$
(:ref{#parallel-distance}). The points $$P$$ and $$Q$$ of $$L$$ have $$d(P, Q) = 3$$. The lines
through $$P$$ and $$Q$$ perpendicular to $$L$$ meet $$L'$$ at $$P'$$ and $$Q'$$.

1. Show that $$PQQ'P'$$ is a rectangle.
2. Find $$d(P', Q')$$ and the area of the rectangle.

:::solution

1. The lines $$PP'$$ and $$QQ'$$ are perpendicular to $$L$$. Since $$L \parallel L'$$, they are
   perpendicular to $$L'$$ too (rule 2 of :ref{#perpendicular-rules}). Now check each corner:

   - at $$P$$, the side $$PQ$$ lies on $$L$$, which is perpendicular to $$PP'$$
   - at $$Q$$, the side $$QP$$ lies on $$L$$, which is perpendicular to $$QQ'$$
   - at $$Q'$$ and $$P'$$, the side $$P'Q'$$ lies on $$L'$$, which is perpendicular to $$QQ'$$ and
     to $$PP'$$

   So each side is perpendicular to the two sides next to it, and $$PQQ'P'$$ is a rectangle
   (:ref{#rectangle}).

2. Opposite sides are equal (:ref{#rectangle-sides}), so $$d(P', Q') = d(P, Q) = 3$$. The sides
   $$PP'$$ and $$QQ'$$ both have the length $$4$$, the distance between the lines. So the area is
   $$3 \cdot 4 = 12$$ (:ref{#area-rules}).

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-lines-notched-square}

A square of side $$10$$ has a square of side $$4$$ cut out of one corner. Find the area of what is
left in two different ways.

:::solution

**Taking away.** The big square is made of two pieces that don't overlap: the small square and what
is left. By :ref{#area-rules}, $$100 = 16 + (\text{area left})$$, so the area left is $$84$$.

**Cutting up.** Say the small square was cut from the top right corner. Cut what is left along the
line where the notch begins: this gives a strip $$10$$ wide and $$6$$ high along the bottom, and a
rectangle $$6$$ wide and $$4$$ high above its left part. These don't overlap, so the area is
$$60 + 24 = 84$$.

Both ways give $$84$$, as rule 2 promises: every way of cutting up the same region gives the same
total.

:::
