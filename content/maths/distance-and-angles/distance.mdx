Stick two pins in a map and stretch a thread tight between them. The length of the thread is the
distance between the pins, and no path between them is shorter. Distance is the most basic idea in
the geometry of the plane, and the rest of the chapter builds on it.

In this guide we'll take three facts about distance as given, and three more about the straight path
between two points. Then we'll use distance alone to describe circles and discs. Later guides add a
few more facts, starting with lines in the [next guide](./lines.mdx).

## Distance between two points

Think of laying a ruler from one point to another and reading off the length. The answer depends on
the ruler's unit, such as centimetres or inches. So these notes fix one unit of length at the start
and keep it throughout. With the unit fixed, every distance is a number, and you can leave the unit
out.

The distance between points $$P$$ and $$Q$$ is written $$d(P, Q)$$. Three facts about it match what
any ruler tells you. They aren't proved here, and every later result about distance rests on them.
The [Coordinates](../coordinates/geometry-from-numbers.mdx) chapter proves all three from a formula
for distance.

:::remark[Rules for distance]{#distance-rules}

For any points $$P$$, $$Q$$ and $$M$$ in the plane:

1. **Never negative**: $$d(P, Q) \ge 0$$, and $$d(P, Q) = 0$$ exactly when $$P$$ and $$Q$$ are the
   same point
2. **The same both ways**: $$d(P, Q) = d(Q, P)$$
3. **The triangle inequality**: $$d(P, M) \le d(P, Q) + d(Q, M)$$

These are taken as given. Every other fact about distance in this chapter is proved from them and
the rules for segments below.

::see-also{src="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_space#Definition"}

:::

The first two rules say that a distance is a size, with no direction. Going from $$P$$ to $$Q$$
covers the same ground as going back. The same rules hold for distance on the number line
(:ref{#real-numbers/distance}), where the distance between $$a$$ and $$b$$ is
$$\lvert a - b \rvert$$.

The triangle inequality says that a detour can't be shorter than the direct route. Getting from
$$P$$ to $$M$$ by way of a third point $$Q$$ covers at least as much ground as going straight there.
When $$P$$, $$Q$$ and $$M$$ are the corners of a triangle, it says that one side is never longer
than the other two put together, which is where the name comes from. The number line has its own
version, :ref{#real-numbers/triangle-inequality}.

```plane
point P 0 0 sw
point M 6 0 se
point Q 2.2 2.4 n
segment P Q label="d(P, Q)" color=blue
segment Q M label="d(Q, M)" color=orange
segment P M label="d(P, M)" side=right dashed
caption "The route through Q is longer than the direct route from P to M"
```

## Segments and points between

The straight path from $$P$$ to $$M$$ is the shortest route between them. A detour through a point
on that path costs nothing extra, since you never leave the direct route. Lower $$Q$$ onto the
segment below and watch the two distances through $$Q$$ add up to exactly $$d(P, M)$$.

<SegmentDistances />

:::definition[Segment]{#segment}

Let $$P$$ and $$M$$ be different points. The **segment** $$PM$$ is the straight piece of the line
through $$P$$ and $$M$$ that runs from $$P$$ to $$M$$, including $$P$$ and $$M$$ themselves. Its
**length** is $$d(P, M)$$. A point of the segment other than $$P$$ and $$M$$ lies **between** $$P$$
and $$M$$.

:::

Three facts about segments are taken as given, like the rules for distance. The first is what the
diagram of $$Q$$ and the segment shows: a stop on the segment adds no distance. The second says that
a segment has no gaps, so you can stop at any distance along it. The third says that three points on
one line always come in an order, with one of them in the middle. The
[Segments, rays and lines](../segments-rays-and-lines/rules-proved.mdx) chapter proves all three
from coordinates.

:::remark[Rules for segments]{#segment-rules}

Let $$P$$ and $$M$$ be different points.

1. **Points on the segment**: for any point $$Q$$, $$d(P, M) = d(P, Q) + d(Q, M)$$ exactly when
   $$Q$$ lies on the segment $$PM$$
2. **Every length is reached**: for every number $$c$$ with $$0 \le c \le d(P, M)$$, exactly one
   point $$Q$$ of the segment $$PM$$ has $$d(P, Q) = c$$
3. **One point in the middle**: of three different points on one line, exactly one lies between the
   other two

These are taken as given.

::see-also{src="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_segment"}

:::

```plane
point P 0 0 s
point Q 2.4 0 s
point M 6 0 s
segment P M
text 1.2 0.5 "c"
text 4.2 0.5 "d(P, M) − c"
caption "The point of the segment at distance c from P"
```

:::example[The rest of the way]

Let $$Q$$ be the point of the segment $$PM$$ with $$d(P, Q) = c$$, from rule 2. Then $$Q$$ is on the
segment, so rule 1 gives $$d(P, M) = c + d(Q, M)$$. Taking $$c$$ from both sides,

```math
d(Q, M) = d(P, M) - c
```

So the point that is $$c$$ along from $$P$$ is the rest of the way, $$d(P, M) - c$$, from $$M$$. For
example, on a segment of length $$10$$, the point $$3$$ from one end is $$7$$ from the other.

:::

:::example[A point on a segment is no further from P than M is]

If $$Q$$ is on the segment $$PM$$, then $$d(P, Q) \le d(P, M)$$. Rule 1 gives
$$d(P, M) = d(P, Q) + d(Q, M)$$, and $$d(Q, M) \ge 0$$ by :ref{#distance-rules}. So $$d(P, M)$$ is
$$d(P, Q)$$ plus a number that isn't negative, and it can't be smaller than $$d(P, Q)$$.

:::

## Circles and discs

Tie a goat to a post with a rope of length $$r$$. The furthest points the goat can reach form a
circle around the post, and all the grass it can eat forms a disc. Both are described by distance
from the post alone.

:::definition[Circle and disc]{#circle}

Let $$P$$ be a point and $$r$$ a positive number.

- The **circle** with centre $$P$$ and radius $$r$$ is the set of all points $$Q$$ with
  $$d(P, Q) = r$$.
- The **disc** with centre $$P$$ and radius $$r$$ is the set of all points $$Q$$ with
  $$d(P, Q) \le r$$.

:::

```plane
point P 0 0 s
circle P 1.6 color=blue
point Q 5 0 s
circle Q 1.6 fill
text 0 2.1 "circle"
text 5 2.1 "disc"
caption "A circle is only the edge; a disc is the edge and everything inside it"
```

The circle is the edge of the disc. Everyday speech uses "circle" for both, but they are different
sets, and this chapter keeps the two words apart. A circle is a curve, so it has a length but no
area. A disc is a region, so it has an area. Later pages use this difference: an angle is measured
by the part of a disc it covers, and the disc's area is what gets shared out.

:::example[Inside, on or outside]

Take the circle and disc with centre $$P$$ and radius $$5$$, and three points with $$d(P, A) = 3$$,
$$d(P, B) = 5$$ and $$d(P, C) = 8$$:

- **$$A$$**: $$3 \le 5$$, so $$A$$ is in the disc, but $$3 \ne 5$$, so it isn't on the circle
- **$$B$$**: $$5 = 5$$, so $$B$$ is on the circle, and so also in the disc
- **$$C$$**: $$8 > 5$$, so $$C$$ is in neither

Every point of the circle belongs to the disc, but the points inside the disc, like $$A$$, aren't on
the circle.

:::

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on distance, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-distance-in-the-disc}

Take the circle and the disc with centre $$P$$ and radius $$4$$. For each point below, say whether
it lies on the circle, in the disc, in both or in neither:

1. A point $$A$$ with $$d(P, A) = 2$$
2. A point $$B$$ with $$d(P, B) = 4$$
3. A point $$C$$ with $$d(P, C) = 4.5$$
4. The centre $$P$$ itself

:::solution

Compare each distance from $$P$$ with the radius $$4$$, using :ref{#circle}:

1. $$2 \le 4$$, so $$A$$ is in the disc. Since $$2 \ne 4$$, it is not on the circle.
2. $$d(P, B) = 4$$, so $$B$$ is on the circle. Since $$4 \le 4$$, it is in the disc as well.
3. $$4.5 > 4$$, so $$C$$ is in neither.
4. $$d(P, P) = 0$$ by :ref{#distance-rules}. Since $$0 \le 4$$, the centre is in the disc, but
   $$0 \ne 4$$, so it is not on the circle.

The centre always belongs to the disc and never to the circle, because the radius is positive.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-distance-rest-of-segment}

The segment $$PM$$ has length $$9$$. The point $$Q$$ of the segment has $$d(P, Q) = 4$$, and the
point $$R$$ of the segment has $$d(R, M) = 2$$.

1. Find $$d(Q, M)$$.
2. Find $$d(P, R)$$.

:::solution

Both points lie on the segment, so rule 1 of :ref{#segment-rules} applies to each of them.

1. $$d(P, M) = d(P, Q) + d(Q, M)$$ gives $$9 = 4 + d(Q, M)$$, so $$d(Q, M) = 5$$.
2. $$d(P, M) = d(P, R) + d(R, M)$$ gives $$9 = d(P, R) + 2$$, so $$d(P, R) = 7$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-distance-off-the-segment}

Three points have $$d(P, Q) = 3$$, $$d(Q, M) = 4$$ and $$d(P, M) = 6$$. Does $$Q$$ lie on the
segment $$PM$$? Do these distances break the triangle inequality?

:::solution

By rule 1 of :ref{#segment-rules}, $$Q$$ lies on the segment $$PM$$ exactly when
$$d(P, M) = d(P, Q) + d(Q, M)$$. Here $$d(P, Q) + d(Q, M) = 7$$, which is not $$6$$, so $$Q$$ is not
on the segment.

The triangle inequality (:ref{#distance-rules}) asks for $$d(P, M) \le d(P, Q) + d(Q, M)$$, that is
$$6 \le 7$$, which holds. So nothing is broken: the route through $$Q$$ is $$1$$ longer than the
direct route, which is what you expect from a point off the segment.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-distance-impossible}

Can three points $$A$$, $$B$$ and $$C$$ have $$d(A, B) = 2$$, $$d(B, C) = 3$$ and $$d(A, C) = 6$$?

:::solution

No. The triangle inequality (:ref{#distance-rules}) gives

```math
d(A, C) \le d(A, B) + d(B, C) = 2 + 3 = 5
```

But $$d(A, C) = 6$$, and $$6 > 5$$. So no three points have these distances: the direct route from
$$A$$ to $$C$$ would be longer than the detour through $$B$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-distance-strictly-closer}

The point $$Q$$ lies on the segment $$PM$$. Someone claims that $$d(P, Q) < d(P, M)$$. When is the
claim true, and when does it fail?

:::solution

Since $$Q$$ is on the segment, rule 1 of :ref{#segment-rules} gives

```math
d(P, M) = d(P, Q) + d(Q, M)
```

So $$d(P, Q) < d(P, M)$$ exactly when $$d(Q, M) > 0$$. By :ref{#distance-rules}, $$d(Q, M) = 0$$
exactly when $$Q$$ is $$M$$. So the claim is true for every point of the segment except $$M$$
itself, where $$d(P, Q) = d(P, M)$$.

The segment includes its two ends, so the safe statement is $$d(P, Q) \le d(P, M)$$, with equality
exactly when $$Q = M$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-distance-separate-discs}

Two discs both have radius $$3$$. Their centres $$P$$ and $$Q$$ have $$d(P, Q) = 10$$. Prove that no
point lies in both discs.

:::solution

Suppose some point $$X$$ lay in both discs (:ref{#basic-proofs/contradiction}). By :ref{#circle},
$$d(P, X) \le 3$$ and $$d(Q, X) \le 3$$. Distance is the same both ways, so $$d(X, Q) \le 3$$ as
well. The triangle inequality (:ref{#distance-rules}) then gives

```math
d(P, Q) \le d(P, X) + d(X, Q) \le 3 + 3 = 6
```

But $$d(P, Q) = 10$$, which is more than $$6$$. This is impossible, so no point lies in both discs.
The same argument shows that two discs of radii $$r$$ and $$s$$ never meet when their centres are
more than $$r + s$$ apart.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-distance-disc-inside-disc}

The points $$P$$ and $$Q$$ have $$d(P, Q) = 1$$. Prove that every point of the disc with centre
$$Q$$ and radius $$1$$ lies in the disc with centre $$P$$ and radius $$2$$.

:::solution

Let $$X$$ be a point of the disc with centre $$Q$$ and radius $$1$$, so $$d(Q, X) \le 1$$
(:ref{#circle}). By the triangle inequality (:ref{#distance-rules}),

```math
d(P, X) \le d(P, Q) + d(Q, X) \le 1 + 1 = 2
```

So $$X$$ is in the disc with centre $$P$$ and radius $$2$$, and the small disc sits inside the large
one.

:::
