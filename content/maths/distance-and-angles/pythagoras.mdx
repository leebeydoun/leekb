title: The Pythagoras theorem

Knowing two sides of a right triangle fixes the third. The Pythagoras theorem says exactly how: the
square of the hypotenuse is the sum of the squares of the legs. This page proves the theorem by
fitting four copies of the triangle inside a square, and then uses it to find missing lengths, from
the diagonal of a square to the diagonal of a box.

## The theorem

Draw a square on each side of a right triangle. The theorem says that the two squares on the legs
have the same total area as the square on the hypotenuse. For legs $$3$$ and $$4$$ the hypotenuse is
$$5$$, and indeed $$9 + 16 = 25$$.

:::theorem[Pythagoras theorem]{#pythagoras}

In a right triangle with legs of lengths $$a$$ and $$b$$ and hypotenuse of length $$c$$,

```math
a^2 + b^2 = c^2
```

:::

The proof compares areas. Take a square with sides of length $$a + b$$, and fit four copies of the
triangle into its corners. They leave a tilted square uncovered in the middle, with sides of length
$$c$$. The big square's area can then be added up two ways: once as $$(a + b)^2$$, and once as four
triangles plus the square in the middle.

<PythagorasSquares />

The same four triangles can also be slid, without turning, so that they leave a square of side $$a$$
and a square of side $$b$$ uncovered instead. Move the slider to watch this. The big square and the
four triangles stay the same, so once the triangles fit together again they leave the same area
uncovered: $$c^2$$ in the first layout equals $$a^2 + b^2$$ in the second. The written proof below
uses only the first layout.

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_theorem#Rearrangement_proofs"}

**Build the square.** Draw a square with sides of length $$a + b$$. On each side, mark the point
that splits it into a piece of length $$a$$ and a piece of length $$b$$ (:ref{#segment-rules}). Go
round the square in one direction, so that each side starts with its $$a$$ piece. Call the marked
points $$K$$, $$L$$, $$S$$ and $$T$$.

```plane

point L 5 2 e

angle X K L color=green

caption "Four copies of the triangle in a square of side a + b"
```

**The four corner triangles.** Each corner of the big square is a right angle, with an $$a$$ piece
along one side and a $$b$$ piece along the other. So each corner, with the two marked points next to
it, makes a right triangle with legs $$a$$ and $$b$$. By the rules for right triangles
(:ref{#right-triangle-rule}), each one has a hypotenuse of length $$c$$, the same angles as the
original triangle, and the same area, $$\frac{ab}{2}$$ (:ref{#right-triangle-area}).

**The middle is a square.** Its four sides $$KL$$, $$LS$$, $$ST$$ and $$TK$$ are hypotenuses of the
corner triangles, so each has length $$c$$. Look at its corner $$K$$, on the bottom side. As the
figure shows, three angles sit side by side there and together fill the straight angle along the
bottom side:

- **Orange**: the angle at $$K$$ of the bottom left triangle, which faces its leg of length $$b$$
- **Middle**: the corner $$\angle LKT$$ of the middle square
- **Green**: the angle at $$K$$ of the bottom right triangle, which faces its leg of length $$a$$

The orange angle faces a leg of length $$b$$ and the green angle faces a leg of length $$a$$. So
they match the two angles of the original triangle other than its right angle
(:ref{#right-triangle-rule}), and they add up to $$90^\circ$$ (:ref{#right-triangle-angles}). The
three angles together make a straight angle, $$180^\circ$$ (:ref{#angle-addition},
:ref{#straight-angle}). So

```math
\angle LKT = 180^\circ - 90^\circ = 90^\circ
```

The same argument works at $$L$$, $$S$$ and $$T$$. So each side of the middle shape meets the next
at a right angle. The other angles where their lines cross are then
$$180^\circ - 90^\circ = 90^\circ$$ too (:ref{#angles-on-line}), so all four are equal and the two
sides are perpendicular (:ref{#perpendicular}). A shape whose sides are each perpendicular to the
two next to it is a rectangle, and with four sides of length $$c$$ it is a square of side $$c$$
(:ref{#rectangle}). Its area is $$c^2$$ (:ref{#area-rules}).

**Add up the areas.** The big square has area $$(a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$$, by :ref{#area-rules}
and :ref{#numbers/square-formulas}. It is cut into the middle square and the four triangles, which
don't overlap, so its area is also

```math
c^2 + 4 \cdot \frac{ab}{2} = c^2 + 2ab
```

Setting the two equal gives $$a^2 + 2ab + b^2 = c^2 + 2ab$$. Taking $$2ab$$ from both sides leaves
$$a^2 + b^2 = c^2$$.

:::

## Finding a missing side

The theorem links three lengths, so any two of them give the third. Lengths are never negative, so
each one is the square root of its square (:ref{#real-numbers/square-root}).

:::corollary[Finding the third side]{#pythagoras-sides}

In a right triangle with legs $$a$$ and $$b$$ and hypotenuse $$c$$,

```math
c = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}, \qquad a = \sqrt{c^2 - b^2}, \qquad b = \sqrt{c^2 - a^2}
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://www.mathsisfun.com/pythagoras.html"}

By :ref{#pythagoras}, $$c^2 = a^2 + b^2$$. The length $$c$$ is a number $$c \ge 0$$ whose square is
$$a^2 + b^2$$, and the square root is the only such number (:ref{#real-numbers/square-root}). So
$$c = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$$.

Taking $$b^2$$ from both sides of the theorem gives $$a^2 = c^2 - b^2$$. Since $$a \ge 0$$, the same
reasoning gives $$a = \sqrt{c^2 - b^2}$$. The formula for $$b$$ follows in the same way.

:::

:::example[A leaning ladder]

A ladder $$5$$ m long leans against a wall, with its foot $$1.4$$ m from the wall. The wall meets
the ground at a right angle, so the ladder is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with one leg
$$1.4$$. The height $$h$$ the ladder reaches up the wall is the other leg:

```math
h = \sqrt{5^2 - 1.4^2} = \sqrt{25 - 1.96} = \sqrt{23.04} = 4.8
```

So the ladder reaches $$4.8$$ m up the wall. Check: $$1.4^2 + 4.8^2 = 1.96 + 23.04 = 25 = 5^2$$.

:::

:::example[Whole number sides]

A right triangle with legs $$5$$ and $$12$$ has hypotenuse $$\sqrt{25 + 144} = \sqrt{169} = 13$$.
Going the other way, a right triangle with hypotenuse $$13$$ and one leg $$12$$ has other leg
$$\sqrt{169 - 144} = \sqrt{25} = 5$$. Sets of whole numbers like $$3, 4, 5$$ and $$5, 12, 13$$,
which fit the theorem exactly, are called **Pythagorean triples**.

:::

## The diagonal of a square

A diagonal cuts a square into two right triangles, with the diagonal as the hypotenuse of each. So
the theorem gives the length of the diagonal from the length of the side.

```plane
point P 0 0 sw
point Q 3 0 se
point R 3 3 ne
point S 0 3 nw
segment P Q label="1" side=right
segment Q R label="1" side=right
segment R S
segment S P
segment P R label="d" color=blue
right Q P S
right R Q P
caption "The diagonal d of a square with sides of length 1"
```

:::example[A diagonal of length √2]

The diagonal $$PR$$ of a square with sides of length $$1$$ is the hypotenuse of the right triangle
$$\triangle PQR$$, with legs $$1$$ and $$1$$. So its length is

```math
d = \sqrt{1^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{2}
```

No fraction squares to $$2$$ (:ref{#numbers/sqrt-two}), so this length can't be written as a
fraction. A square with sides of length $$s$$ has diagonal $$\sqrt{s^2 + s^2} = s\sqrt{2}$$, by
:ref{#real-numbers/root-product}.

:::

## Using the theorem twice

Picture a box $$6$$ cm long, $$3$$ cm wide and $$2$$ cm high, and a pencil that has to fit inside
it. Laid flat on the floor of the box, the longest pencil runs along the floor's diagonal. Tilted up
from a bottom corner to the opposite top corner, a longer one fits. That line from corner to corner
is the diagonal through the box.

<BoxDiagonal
  step="pencil"
  caption="The longest pencil runs from a bottom corner to the opposite top corner"
/>

The diagonal through the box doesn't lie in any one face, so it isn't a side of any triangle drawn
on the outside of the box. Instead, two right triangles inside the box reach it, one after the
other. The first lies on the floor and finds the floor's diagonal. The second stands upright on that
diagonal, like a card stood on its edge, and finds the diagonal through the box.

:::example[The diagonal of a box]

The box is $$6$$ long, $$3$$ wide and $$2$$ high. The first right triangle lies on the floor. Its
legs are the length $$6$$ and the width $$3$$, which meet at a corner of the floor at a right angle,
and its hypotenuse is the floor diagonal $$f$$:

```math
f = \sqrt{6^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{45}
```

<BoxDiagonal
  step="floor"
  caption="The first triangle lies on the floor, with legs 6 and 3 and hypotenuse f"
/>

The second right triangle stands upright inside the box. Its legs are the floor diagonal $$f$$ and
the upright edge of height $$2$$ at the far corner, and its hypotenuse is the diagonal $$D$$ through
the box.

Its right angle is where the upright edge meets the floor. The edge points straight up, so it is
square to every line on the floor through its foot, whichever way that line runs. A flagpole is the
same: it is square to the ground whichever direction you walk away from it. This is a fact about
space rather than the plane, and it is taken here from the picture of a box rather than proved. So
the second triangle has a right angle between $$f$$ and the edge, and:

```math
D = \sqrt{f^2 + 2^2} = \sqrt{45 + 4} = \sqrt{49} = 7
```

<BoxDiagonal
  step="upright"
  caption="The second triangle stands on the floor diagonal, with legs f and 2 and hypotenuse D"
/>

So a pencil $$7$$ cm long fits from corner to corner, while flat on the floor the longest that fits
is $$\sqrt{45}$$, about $$6.7$$ cm. There was no need to work out $$\sqrt{45}$$ for $$D$$, since the
second triangle only used $$f^2 = 45$$.

The same two steps work for any box. With sides $$x$$, $$y$$ and $$z$$, the floor gives
$$f^2 = x^2 + y^2$$, and the second triangle adds $$z^2$$, so the diagonal through the box is
$$\sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}$$.

:::
