An angle describes how far apart two directions are. The hands of a clock, the blades of open
scissors and the two edges of a slice of pizza all make one. This page says exactly what an angle
is, how to name one, which angles have special names, and how an angle cuts a slice from a disc. The
[next page](./measuring-angles.mdx) uses that slice to measure angles in degrees.

## Two rays make two angles

Picture two rays starting from the same point, like two hands of a clock fixed at the centre. They
split the flat plane into two pieces: a narrow wedge between the hands, and everything else. Both
pieces have the same two rays as their edges, so both count as angles. When you mean one of them,
you need a way to say which.

:::definition[Angle]{#angle}

Let $$PQ$$ and $$PM$$ be two rays (:ref{#ray}) starting from the same point $$P$$. The two rays
split the plane into two regions. Each region, together with the two rays along its edge, is an
**angle** made by the rays. The point $$P$$ is the **vertex** of the angle, and the two rays are its
**arms**.

:::

In the figure, the blue arc marks the narrow angle between the rays, and the orange arc marks the
wide angle that goes the other way round. Both have vertex $$P$$ and arms $$PQ$$ and $$PM$$.

```plane
point P 0 0 sw
point Q 3.2 0 s
point M 1.6 2.8 nw
ray P Q extend=0.6
ray P M extend=0.4
angle Q P M color=blue r=0.6
angle M P Q color=orange r=1
caption "Two rays from P make two angles"
```

## Zero, straight and full angles

Two positions of the arms give angles with special names. If the two rays point the same way, they
lie on top of each other. If they point in opposite directions, together they make a straight line.
Each case still gives two angles, as the definition sets out.

:::definition[Zero, straight and full angles]{#special-angles}

Let $$PQ$$ and $$PM$$ be rays starting from $$P$$:

- **Same ray**: if the two rays are the same ray, one of the angles has nothing between its arms,
  and is called the **zero angle**. The other is the whole plane, and is called the **full angle**.
- **Opposite rays**: if the two rays point in opposite directions along one line, each angle is the
  half of the plane on one side of that line. Each is called a **straight angle**.

:::

The full angle is one complete turn: an arm that turns all the way round comes back to where it
started. A straight angle is half of that, since the line splits the plane into two halves.

```plane
point P 0 0 s
point Q 2.5 0 s
point M -2.5 0 s
line M Q extend=0.3
angle Q P M color=blue r=0.7 arrow
caption "A straight angle: the arms PQ and PM point in opposite directions"
```

## Naming an angle

To talk about an angle you need a name for it. The usual name lists three points: a point on one
arm, then the vertex, then a point on the other arm. You can read it as a path that starts at $$Q$$,
turns the corner at $$P$$ and ends at $$M$$. So $$\angle QPM$$ is an angle at $$P$$, and its arms
are the rays $$PQ$$ and $$PM$$.

```plane
point P 0 0 sw
point Q 3.5 0 s
point M 2 2.6 n
ray P Q extend=0.6
ray P M extend=0.6
angle Q P M color=blue r=0.8
caption "The angle QPM: from Q to the vertex P, then out to M"
```

Two rays from $$P$$ make two angles, though, as the first section showed. So the name also needs a
rule for which of the two it means. Everyday speech already has one. The angle between the hands of
a clock at two o'clock is the narrow one between them, and nobody means the wide way round.

<ClockHands caption="At two o'clock, the angle between the hands means the narrow one" />

"Narrow" isn't precise yet, because angles are only measured on the next page. A line gives a
precise version of it. Extend the arm $$PQ$$ backwards past $$P$$, so that it becomes the whole line
$$PQ$$. The narrow angle fits entirely on one side of that line. The wide angle doesn't: it crosses
the line and covers the whole of the other side.

```plane
point A -3 0 hidden
point B 5.5 0 hidden
point C 5.5 3.2 hidden
point D -3 3.2 hidden
polygon A B C D color=green
point P 0 0 sw
point Q 3.5 0 s
point M 2 2.6 n
line P Q dashed color=muted extend=2.5
ray P Q extend=0.6
ray P M extend=0.6
angle Q P M color=blue r=1.1 fill
angle M P Q color=orange r=0.55 label="reflex"
text -1.4 2.7 "one side of the line PQ"
caption "The angle QPM lies on one side of the line PQ. The reflex angle crosses to the other side."
```

:::definition[Naming an angle]{#angle-name}

Let $$PQ$$ and $$PM$$ be rays starting from $$P$$. Of the two angles they make, $$\angle QPM$$ is
the one that lies on one side of the line $$PQ$$. The other angle is the **reflex angle** at $$P$$,
and is named in words.

:::

A straight angle is exactly one side of a line, so $$\angle QPM$$ is never larger than a straight
angle. A reflex angle is always larger.

Using the line $$PM$$ instead of the line $$PQ$$ picks out the same angle. So the order of the outer
letters doesn't matter, and $$\angle QPM$$ and $$\angle MPQ$$ are the same angle. The middle letter
does matter, because it names the vertex. In the figure below, $$\angle QPM$$ is the corner at
$$P$$, while $$\angle PQM$$ is the corner at $$Q$$.

```plane
point P 0 0 sw
point Q 4 0 se
point M 1.5 2.4 n
segment P Q
segment P M
segment Q M
angle Q P M color=blue r=0.6
angle M Q P color=orange r=0.6
text 1.25 0.45 "∠QPM"
text 2.95 0.4 "∠PQM"
caption "The middle letter names the vertex: the angle QPM is at P, and the angle PQM is at Q"
```

The two special positions from the previous section need a note:

- **Opposite rays**: the two angles are the two halves of the plane, one on each side of the line.
  Both fit the rule, and both are straight angles, so $$\angle QPM$$ can mean either.
- **The same ray**: $$\angle QPM$$ is the zero angle, and the full angle is named in words.

:::example[Angles in a triangle]

Take three points $$A$$, $$B$$ and $$C$$ that don't lie on one line. Then $$\angle BAC$$ is the
corner of the triangle at $$A$$, $$\angle ABC$$ is the corner at $$B$$, and $$\angle ACB$$ is the
corner at $$C$$. Each is the narrow angle between two sides, which is the one the rule picks.

```plane
point A 0 0 sw
point B 4.5 0 se
point C 1.5 2.6 n
segment A B
segment B C
segment A C
angle B A C color=blue r=0.55
angle C B A color=orange r=0.55
angle A C B color=green r=0.45
caption "The three angles of a triangle: at A in blue, at B in orange and at C in green"
```

:::

## Sectors

An angle with its vertex at the centre of a round pizza cuts out a slice. The slice is the part of
the pizza that lies inside the angle. A wider angle cuts a bigger slice, and that link between the
size of the angle and the size of the slice is what the next page uses to measure angles.

:::definition[Sector]{#sector}

Let $$A$$ be an angle with vertex $$P$$, and let $$D$$ be a disc with centre $$P$$ (:ref{#circle}).
The part of the disc $$D$$ that lies inside $$A$$ is the **sector** of $$D$$ cut out by $$A$$. The
curved edge of a sector, the part of the circle inside $$A$$, is called an **arc**.

:::

```plane
point P 0 0 sw
point Q 2 0 hidden
point M 1 1.73 hidden
circle P 2 color=muted dashed
angle Q P M r=2 fill color=blue
segment P Q color=blue
segment P M color=blue
caption "The shaded sector is the part of the disc inside the angle"
```

The special angles give the sectors you would expect:

- **Zero angle**: the sector is a single radius, with no area
- **Straight angle**: the sector is half of the disc, cut along a line through the centre
- **Full angle**: the sector is the whole disc
