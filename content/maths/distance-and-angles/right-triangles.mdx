title: Right triangles
description: Triangles, right triangles, their angles and their area.
---

Cut a sheet of paper along its diagonal and you get two right triangles, each with one square
corner. The rest of the chapter depends on these triangles. The Pythagoras theorem is about them,
and you can find the angles and area of any other triangle by splitting it into right triangles.

In this guide we'll say what a triangle is and take one fact about right triangles as given. From it
we'll prove that the two other angles add up to $$90^\circ$$, and that the area is half the product
of the legs.

## Triangles

Pick three points that don't lie on one line, and join each pair with a straight segment. The three
segments close up into a triangle. If the three points were on one line, the segments would lie on
top of each other and enclose nothing, which is why that case is ruled out.

:::definition[Triangle]{#triangle}

Let $$P$$, $$Q$$ and $$M$$ be three points that don't all lie on one line. The **triangle**
$$\triangle PQM$$ is made of the three segments $$PQ$$, $$QM$$ and $$MP$$ (:ref{#segment}):

- **Vertices**: the points $$P$$, $$Q$$ and $$M$$, also called its corners
- **Sides**: the three segments
- **Angles**: at each vertex, the angle between the two sides that meet there, such as
  $$\angle QPM$$ at $$P$$

:::

```plane
point P 0 0 sw
point Q 5 0 se
point M 1.5 3 n
segment P Q
segment Q M
segment M P
angle Q P M color=blue
angle M Q P color=orange
angle P M Q color=green
caption "The triangle PQM, with its three angles marked"
```

A triangle is its three sides, the way a circle is its outline (:ref{#circle}). The region inside
has no separate short name, so "the area of a triangle" means the area of the region its sides
enclose.

## Right triangles

A right triangle is a triangle with a square corner. The two sides that make that corner are its
legs, and the side facing it is the hypotenuse. The hypotenuse is always the longest side. The
[Perpendicular bisectors](./perpendicular-bisectors.mdx) guide proves this.

:::definition[Right triangle]{#right-triangle}

A **right triangle** is a triangle with a right angle (:ref{#right-angle}), so two of its sides are
perpendicular (:ref{#perpendicular}).

- **Legs**: the two sides that meet at the right angle
- **Hypotenuse**: the third side, opposite the right angle

In these notes the legs usually have lengths $$a$$ and $$b$$, and the hypotenuse has length $$c$$.

:::

```plane
point P 0 0 sw
point Q 4 0 se
point M 0 3 nw
segment P Q label="b" side=right
segment P M label="a"
segment Q M label="c" side=right
right Q P M
caption "A right triangle with its right angle at P: legs a and b, hypotenuse c"
```

Each of the two other angles sits at the far end of one leg, facing the other leg. In the figure,
the angle at $$Q$$ faces the leg $$PM$$ of length $$a$$, and the angle at $$M$$ faces the leg $$PQ$$
of length $$b$$.

## Right triangles with the same legs

Cut a right triangle with legs of $$3$$ cm and $$4$$ cm out of card. Any other right triangle with
legs of $$3$$ cm and $$4$$ cm can be laid exactly on top of it, perhaps after turning it over. Once
the two legs and the square corner between them are fixed, there is nothing left to choose, so the
hypotenuse, the angles and the area all come out the same.

<CardTriangles caption="Two right triangles with legs of 3 cm and 4 cm: turned over, the second covers the first exactly" />

This fact is taken as given here, as the starting point for right triangles. It is a special case of
a wider idea, **congruence**, which says when two shapes are copies of each other. The
[Congruence](../isometries/congruence.mdx) guide, in the Isometries chapter, proves the hypotenuse
and angle parts by moving one triangle onto the other.

:::remark[Rules for right triangles]{#right-triangle-rule}

Suppose two right triangles have legs of the same lengths: a leg of length $$a$$ and a leg of length
$$b$$ in each. Then they match completely:

1. **Hypotenuse**: their hypotenuses have the same length
2. **Angles**: the angles facing legs of the same length are equal
3. **Area**: they have the same area

These are taken as given. The rest of this page is proved from them.

::see-also{src="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congruence_(geometry)#Determining_congruence"}

:::

```plane
point P 0 0 sw
point Q 4 0 se
point M 0 3 nw
segment P Q
segment P M
segment Q M
right Q P M
ticks P Q 1
ticks P M 2
angle M Q P color=orange
point P' 9 3.5 ne
point Q' 9 -0.5 se
point M' 6 3.5 nw
segment P' Q'
segment P' M'
segment Q' M'
right M' P' Q'
ticks P' Q' 1
ticks P' M' 2
angle P' Q' M' color=orange
caption "Legs with the same marks have the same length, so the marked angles are equal"
```

The rule matches the angle facing one leg with the angle facing the leg of the same length in the
other triangle. In the figure, both orange angles face the leg with two marks, so they are equal,
even though the second triangle is turned round.

## The two other angles add up to 90°

Put two copies of a right triangle together along their hypotenuses, and they make a rectangle. Each
corner of the rectangle is a right angle. At one of the corners where the hypotenuse ends, the right
angle is split into two pieces, one from each copy, and those are the two angles of the original
triangle. So together they make $$90^\circ$$.

:::theorem[Angles of a right triangle]{#right-triangle-angles}

In a right triangle, the two angles other than the right angle add up to $$90^\circ$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_triangle#Angles"}

Let the triangle be $$\triangle PQM$$ with its right angle at $$P$$, legs $$d(P, M) = a$$ and
$$d(P, Q) = b$$. The aim is to show $$\angle PQM + \angle PMQ = 90^\circ$$.

```plane
point P 0 0 sw
point Q 4 0 se
point M 0 3 nw
point N 4 3 ne
segment P Q label="b" side=right
segment P M label="a"
segment Q N label="a" side=right
segment M N label="b"
segment Q M dashed
right Q P M
right M N Q
angle M Q P color=orange
angle N Q M color=green
angle P M Q color=green
caption "Completing the rectangle PQNM: the two green angles are equal"
```

**Complete the rectangle.** Draw the line through $$Q$$ perpendicular to $$PQ$$
(:ref{#perpendicular-rules}), and the line through $$M$$ parallel to $$PQ$$ (:ref{#line-rules}). The
first line is perpendicular to $$PQ$$, and $$PQ$$ is parallel to the second line, so the first line
is perpendicular to the second as well (rule 2 of :ref{#perpendicular-rules}). Perpendicular lines
cross, so the two lines meet at one point $$N$$ (:ref{#crossing-lines}).

Each corner of $$PQNM$$ is now a right angle. At $$P$$ the legs are perpendicular, and at $$Q$$ and
$$N$$ the lines were drawn that way. At $$M$$, the line $$MN$$ is parallel to $$PQ$$, which is
perpendicular to $$PM$$, so $$MN$$ is perpendicular to $$PM$$ too (rule 2 of
:ref{#perpendicular-rules}). So each side of $$PQNM$$ is perpendicular to the two sides next to it,
and it is a rectangle (:ref{#rectangle}). Its opposite sides are equal (:ref{#rectangle-sides}), so
$$d(Q, N) = a$$ and $$d(M, N) = b$$.

**Compare the two halves.** The diagonal $$QM$$ splits the rectangle into two right triangles:
$$\triangle PQM$$, with its right angle at $$P$$, and $$\triangle NMQ$$, with its right angle at
$$N$$. Both have a leg of length $$a$$ and a leg of length $$b$$. In $$\triangle PQM$$, the angle at
$$M$$ faces the leg $$PQ$$ of length $$b$$. In $$\triangle NMQ$$, the angle at $$Q$$ faces the leg
$$NM$$ of length $$b$$. So by :ref{#right-triangle-rule},

```math
\angle MQN = \angle PMQ
```

**Add up at the corner $$Q$$.** The figure shows the diagonal $$QM$$ running into the rectangle, so
it splits the right angle $$\angle PQN$$ into the two angles $$\angle PQM$$ and $$\angle MQN$$, side
by side. Their measures add (:ref{#angle-addition}):

```math
\angle PQM + \angle MQN = \angle PQN = 90^\circ
```

Replacing $$\angle MQN$$ by the equal angle $$\angle PMQ$$ gives
$$\angle PQM + \angle PMQ = 90^\circ$$.

:::

So a right triangle has only one right angle, and its other two angles are acute. Neither of them is
a zero angle, because the three corners are not on one line. Two angles bigger than $$0^\circ$$ that
add up to $$90^\circ$$ are each less than $$90^\circ$$.

:::example[Finding the third angle]

A ramp meets the ground at an angle of $$12^\circ$$, and its back edge stands upright, at a right
angle to the ground. The ramp, the ground and the back edge make a right triangle, so the angle at
the top of the ramp is $$90^\circ - 12^\circ = 78^\circ$$.

:::

## The area of a right triangle

The same rectangle gives the area. The diagonal cuts the rectangle into two right triangles with the
same legs, so the two halves have the same area. The rectangle's area is $$ab$$, so each half is
$$\frac{ab}{2}$$.

:::proposition[Area of a right triangle]{#right-triangle-area}

A right triangle with legs of lengths $$a$$ and $$b$$ has area $$\frac{ab}{2}$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_triangle#Area"}

Complete the rectangle $$PQNM$$ as in the proof of :ref{#right-triangle-angles}. Its sides have
lengths $$a$$ and $$b$$, so its area is $$ab$$ (:ref{#area-rules}).

The diagonal $$QM$$ cuts the rectangle into $$\triangle PQM$$ and $$\triangle NMQ$$, which don't
overlap. So their areas add up to $$ab$$ (:ref{#area-rules}). Both are right triangles with legs of
lengths $$a$$ and $$b$$, so they have the same area (:ref{#right-triangle-rule}). Call it $$T$$.
Then $$T + T = ab$$, so $$T = \frac{ab}{2}$$.

:::

:::example[A corner of a garden]

A flower bed fills the corner of a garden between two walls that meet at a right angle. Its edges
along the walls are $$6$$ m and $$5$$ m long, and a straight path closes it off. The bed is a right
triangle with legs $$6$$ and $$5$$, so its area is $$\frac{6 \cdot 5}{2} = 15$$ square metres.

:::

Every triangle can be cut into two right triangles, by a line from one corner that meets the
opposite side square on. The [Angles in triangles](./triangle-angles.mdx) page uses this to find the
area of any triangle, and to show that the three angles of any triangle add up to $$180^\circ$$.

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on right triangles, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-right-triangles-which}

For each set of three points, say whether they are the corners of a triangle:

1. Three corners $$A$$, $$B$$ and $$C$$ of a rectangle $$ABCD$$
2. Two points $$P$$ and $$Q$$, and a point between them on the segment $$PQ$$
3. Two points $$P$$ and $$Q$$, and a point of the ray $$PQ$$ beyond $$Q$$

:::solution

A triangle needs three points that don't all lie on one line (:ref{#triangle}).

1. **Yes.** The lines $$AB$$ and $$BC$$ are perpendicular (:ref{#rectangle}), so they are different
   lines. If $$A$$, $$B$$ and $$C$$ lay on one line, that line would pass through $$A$$ and $$B$$
   and through $$B$$ and $$C$$, so it would be both the line $$AB$$ and the line $$BC$$
   (:ref{#line-rules}). So they don't, and $$\triangle ABC$$ is a right triangle with its right
   angle at $$B$$.
2. **No.** All three points lie on the line $$PQ$$.
3. **No.** A ray is part of a line, so again all three points lie on the line $$PQ$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-right-triangles-parts}

The triangle $$\triangle PQM$$ has its right angle at $$M$$. Name its legs and its hypotenuse. Which
angle faces the leg $$PM$$?

:::solution

The legs are the two sides that meet at the right angle, $$MP$$ and $$MQ$$. The hypotenuse is the
side opposite the right angle, $$PQ$$ (:ref{#right-triangle}).

Each of the other two angles sits at the far end of one leg and faces the other leg. The angle at
$$Q$$, $$\angle PQM$$, sits at the far end of the leg $$MQ$$, so it faces the leg $$PM$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-right-triangles-angle-difference}

In a right triangle, one of the two other angles is $$24^\circ$$ larger than the other. Find both.

:::solution

Write the smaller angle as $$x^\circ$$, so the larger is $$(x + 24)^\circ$$. By
:ref{#right-triangle-angles}, $$x + (x + 24) = 90$$. So $$2x = 66$$ and $$x = 33$$.

The angles are $$33^\circ$$ and $$57^\circ$$. Check: $$33 + 57 = 90$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-right-triangles-matching-angles}

A right triangle has legs $$5$$ and $$12$$, and its angle facing the leg of length $$5$$ is about
$$22.6^\circ$$. A second right triangle, drawn turned round and upside down, has legs $$12$$ and
$$5$$. Which angle of the second triangle is about $$22.6^\circ$$? Find the remaining angle of each
triangle.

:::solution

Both triangles have a leg of length $$5$$ and a leg of length $$12$$. By rule 2 of
:ref{#right-triangle-rule}, the angles facing legs of the same length are equal. So in the second
triangle, the angle facing its leg of length $$5$$ is about $$22.6^\circ$$, however the triangle is
turned.

In each triangle, the remaining angle is about $$90^\circ - 22.6^\circ = 67.4^\circ$$, by
:ref{#right-triangle-angles}. It faces the leg of length $$12$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-right-triangles-leg-and-hypotenuse}

Two right triangles each have one leg of length $$3$$ and a hypotenuse of length $$5$$. Does
:ref{#right-triangle-rule} say that they match? (This exercise looks ahead to the next guide,
[The Pythagoras theorem](./pythagoras.mdx).)

:::solution

Not directly. The rule starts from two legs of the same lengths, and here only one leg is known, so
the rule can't be applied yet.

The next guide fills the gap. By the Pythagoras theorem, the other leg of each triangle is
$$\sqrt{5^2 - 3^2} = \sqrt{16} = 4$$ (:ref{#pythagoras-sides}). Now both triangles have legs $$3$$
and $$4$$, and the rule says they match.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-right-triangles-composite}

A shape is made of a rectangle $$6$$ by $$4$$ and a right triangle with legs $$4$$ and $$3$$. The
triangle's leg of length $$4$$ lies along one side of length $$4$$ of the rectangle, and the two
pieces don't overlap. Find the area of the shape.

:::solution

By rule 1 of :ref{#area-rules}, the rectangle has area $$6 \cdot 4 = 24$$. By
:ref{#right-triangle-area}, the triangle has area $$\frac{4 \cdot 3}{2} = 6$$. The pieces don't
overlap, so by rule 2 of :ref{#area-rules} the shape has area $$24 + 6 = 30$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-right-triangles-doubled-legs}

Prove that doubling both legs of a right triangle multiplies its area by $$4$$.

:::solution

Let the legs be $$a$$ and $$b$$, so the area is $$\frac{ab}{2}$$ (:ref{#right-triangle-area}). A
right triangle with legs $$2a$$ and $$2b$$ has area

```math
\frac{2a \cdot 2b}{2} = 2ab = 4 \cdot \frac{ab}{2}
```

So its area is $$4$$ times the first. Doubling only one leg would double the area instead.

:::
