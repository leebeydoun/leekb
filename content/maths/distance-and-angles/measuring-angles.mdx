Lengths are measured by comparing them with a fixed unit, and angles are measured the same way. The
unit is a fraction of one full turn, called a degree. This page defines degrees in two ways, first
as a fraction of a turn and then more precisely by area, and explains how the two fit together. It
then proves that angles side by side add up, shows that a straight angle is $$180^\circ$$, defines
right angles, and finds the area of a sector.

## Degrees as a fraction of a full turn

Stand facing one way and turn on the spot until you face the same way again. That is one **full
turn**. Split it into $$360$$ equal steps, and each step is one **degree**, written $$1^\circ$$. An
angle measures how far one arm has to turn to reach the other. A quarter turn is $$90^\circ$$, a
half turn is $$180^\circ$$, and a third of a turn is $$120^\circ$$.

The number $$360$$ is used because it divides evenly by small numbers. It is a multiple of $$2$$,
$$3$$, $$4$$, $$5$$, $$6$$, $$8$$, $$9$$, $$10$$ and $$12$$, so halves, thirds, quarters, fifths and
eighths of a turn are all whole numbers of degrees.

"A third of a turn" is clear enough in a picture, but a definition needs a way to check it, and area
gives one. Draw a disc with its centre at the vertex. An angle that is a third of a turn cuts out a
sector that is a third of the disc, and the same holds for any fraction. Turn the arm below and
watch the two fractions stay equal.

<AngleTurn />

:::definition[Angles in degrees]{#degree}

Let $$A$$ be an angle with vertex $$P$$, and let $$x$$ be a number from $$0$$ to $$360$$. The angle
$$A$$ has **$$x$$ degrees**, written $$x^\circ$$, when it is $$\frac{x}{360}$$ of a full turn.
Precisely, this means that for a disc $$D$$ with centre $$P$$, the sector $$S$$ that $$A$$ cuts out
(:ref{#sector}) satisfies

```math
\frac{\text{area of } S}{\text{area of } D} = \frac{x}{360}
```

The size of an angle is written with an equals sign, as in $$\angle QPM = 40^\circ$$.

:::

The zero angle has $$0^\circ$$, since its sector has no area. The full angle has $$360^\circ$$,
since its sector is the whole disc. An angle $$A$$ is **smaller** than an angle $$B$$ when $$A$$ has
fewer degrees than $$B$$.

The definition doesn't say how big the disc is, and it doesn't need to. A bigger disc gives a bigger
sector, but the sector stays the same fraction of the disc. That fact is taken as given, along with
two more facts about discs:

:::remark[Rules for disc area]{#disc-area}

1. **Area of a disc**: a disc of radius $$r$$ has area $$\pi r^2$$, where $$\pi$$ is a fixed number
   a little more than $$3.14$$. A later guide proves this.
2. **Any disc gives the same fraction**: the fraction of a disc that an angle's sector takes up is
   the same for every disc centred at the angle's vertex.
3. **A line through the centre halves a disc**: a line through the centre of a disc cuts it into two
   pieces of equal area.

These are taken as given.

::see-also{src="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Area_of_a_circle"}

:::

From here on, the guide thinks of degrees as a fraction of a full turn. The area version is what
makes "fraction of a turn" precise, and the proofs on the rest of this page use it: for angles side
by side, for straight angles and for the area of a sector.

## Angles side by side add up

Turning $$30^\circ$$ and then $$40^\circ$$ more is the same as turning $$70^\circ$$ in one go. In
the same way, two angles that sit side by side, sharing an arm and not overlapping, make a bigger
angle whose size is the sum of theirs. School courses usually take this as given. Here it follows
from the definition of degrees, because the two sectors side by side make up the bigger sector, and
areas add.

The proof uses the fact that one ray lies inside an angle. The guide has no rules for deciding which
side of a line a point is on, so facts of that kind are read from the figure, and each proof says
when it uses one.

:::proposition[Adding angles]{#angle-addition}

Let $$PQ$$, $$PR$$ and $$PM$$ be rays from $$P$$, with the ray $$PR$$ inside the angle
$$\angle QPM$$. Then

```math
\angle QPR + \angle RPM = \angle QPM
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle#Angle_addition_postulate"}

Write $$\angle QPR = y^\circ$$, $$\angle RPM = z^\circ$$ and $$\angle QPM = x^\circ$$. Take a disc
$$D$$ with centre $$P$$, and let $$S_1$$, $$S_2$$ and $$S$$ be the sectors of $$D$$ cut out by
$$\angle QPR$$, $$\angle RPM$$ and $$\angle QPM$$.

The ray $$PR$$ lies inside $$\angle QPM$$, so it cuts $$S$$ into the two pieces $$S_1$$ and $$S_2$$,
as the figure below shows. The two pieces share only the radius along $$PR$$, and a segment covers
no area, so they don't overlap. By rule 2 of :ref{#area-rules},

```math
\text{area of } S = \text{area of } S_1 + \text{area of } S_2
```

Divide both sides by the area of $$D$$. By :ref{#degree}, each sector's share of the disc is its
angle's number of degrees divided by $$360$$, so

```math
\frac{x}{360} = \frac{y}{360} + \frac{z}{360}
```

Multiplying both sides by $$360$$ gives $$x = y + z$$.

:::

```plane
point P 0 0 sw
point Q 2.6 0 se
point R 2.25 1.3 e
point M 0.89 2.44 n
circle P 2.6 color=muted dashed
angle Q P R r=2.6 fill color=blue
angle R P M r=2.6 fill color=orange
segment P Q
segment P R
segment P M
angle Q P R color=blue r=0.8 label="30°"
angle R P M color=orange r=0.8 label="40°"
caption "Sectors of 30° and 40° side by side make the sector of 70°, and their areas add"
```

## Straight angles and right angles

A straight angle is half a turn, so it should be $$180^\circ$$. The precise reason uses the third
rule for discs: the line that forms a straight angle passes through the centre of any disc at its
vertex, so it cuts the disc into two equal halves.

:::proposition[A straight angle is 180°]{#straight-angle}

Every straight angle has $$180^\circ$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://www.mathsisfun.com/geometry/straight-angle.html"}

Let the straight angle have vertex $$P$$, and take a disc $$D$$ with centre $$P$$. The arms of the
angle lie along one line through $$P$$, and the angle is the half of the plane on one side of that
line (:ref{#special-angles}). So its sector is one of the two pieces the line cuts $$D$$ into. By
rule 3 of :ref{#disc-area}, the two pieces have equal area, so the sector is half of $$D$$. By
:ref{#degree}, the angle has $$x^\circ$$ with $$\frac{x}{360} = \frac{1}{2}$$, so $$x = 180$$.

:::

Half of a straight angle is the angle at the corner of a square or a sheet of paper. It turns up in
every rectangle and wherever two perpendicular lines meet, so it has its own name.

:::definition[Right angle]{#right-angle}

A **right angle** is an angle of $$90^\circ$$, a quarter of a full turn. The four equal angles where
two perpendicular lines cross (:ref{#perpendicular}) are right angles in this sense, as the end of
this section shows. Other angles are named by how they compare with a right angle:

- **Acute angle**: more than $$0^\circ$$ and less than $$90^\circ$$
- **Obtuse angle**: more than $$90^\circ$$ and less than $$180^\circ$$
- **Reflex angle**: more than $$180^\circ$$ and less than $$360^\circ$$

:::

In a figure, a right angle is marked with a small square in its corner instead of an arc.

```plane
point P 0 0 sw
point Q 2.8 0 s
point M 0 2.4 e
ray P Q extend=0.3
ray P M extend=0.3
right Q P M
caption "A right angle, marked with a small square"
```

When a ray stands on a straight line, it splits the straight angle into two angles side by side.
Those two always add up to $$180^\circ$$, so knowing one tells you the other.

:::corollary[Angles on a straight line]{#angles-on-line}

Let $$P$$ be a point between $$Q$$ and $$R$$ on a line, and let $$M$$ be a point that is not on the
line. Then

```math
\angle QPM + \angle MPR = 180^\circ
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://www.mathsisfun.com/angle180.html"}

The rays $$PQ$$ and $$PR$$ point in opposite directions, so the angle on the side of the line where
$$M$$ lies is a straight angle, and the ray $$PM$$ lies inside it. By :ref{#angle-addition}, the two
angles $$\angle QPM$$ and $$\angle MPR$$ add up to that straight angle, which is $$180^\circ$$ by
:ref{#straight-angle}.

:::

```plane
point Q -2.8 0 s
point P 0 0 s
point R 2.8 0 s
point M 1.4 2.4 nw
line Q R extend=0.3
ray P M extend=0.3
angle R P M color=blue r=0.7 label="60°"
angle M P Q color=orange r=0.7 label="120°"
caption "The two angles on a straight line add up to 180°"
```

So if $$\angle QPM = 35^\circ$$, then $$\angle MPR = 180^\circ - 35^\circ = 145^\circ$$. Knowing one
of the two angles always tells you the other.

The corollary also shows that perpendicular lines meet at right angles. Two neighbouring angles at
the crossing are equal and add up to $$180^\circ$$, so each is $$90^\circ$$. It works the other way
round too: when two lines cross and one of the four angles is a right angle, its neighbours are
$$180^\circ - 90^\circ = 90^\circ$$, and so all four angles are right angles.

## The area of a sector

The definition of degrees links the size of an angle to the share of the disc its sector takes. Once
you know the area of the whole disc, that share gives the area of the sector.

:::proposition[Area of a sector]{#sector-area}

The sector cut out by an angle of $$x^\circ$$ from a disc of radius $$r$$ centred at its vertex has
area

```math
\frac{x}{360} \cdot \pi r^2
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circular_sector#Area"}

Let $$S$$ be the sector and $$D$$ the disc. By :ref{#degree},
$$\frac{\text{area of } S}{\text{area of } D} = \frac{x}{360}$$, so the area of $$S$$ is
$$\frac{x}{360}$$ times the area of $$D$$. The area of $$D$$ is $$\pi r^2$$ by rule 1 of
:ref{#disc-area}.

:::

:::example[Slices and rings]

- **A slice of pizza**: a pizza of radius $$15$$ cm is cut into six equal slices, so each slice is
  $$\frac{360}{6} = 60^\circ$$. Its area is
  $$\frac{60}{360} \cdot \pi \cdot 15^2 = \frac{225\pi}{6} = 37.5\pi$$, about $$118$$ square
  centimetres.
- **Part of a ring**: two circles share a centre, with radii $$3$$ and $$5$$. A right angle at the
  centre cuts a piece from the ring between them. That piece is the sector of the large disc with
  the sector of the small disc taken away, so its area is
  $$\frac{90}{360}(25\pi - 9\pi) = \frac{16\pi}{4} = 4\pi$$.
- **Finding the angle**: a sector of a disc of radius $$3$$ has area $$3\pi$$. The whole disc has
  area $$9\pi$$, so the sector is $$\frac{3\pi}{9\pi} = \frac{1}{3}$$ of it, and the angle is
  $$\frac{1}{3} \cdot 360^\circ = 120^\circ$$.

:::

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on measuring angles, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-measuring-fractions}

Write each fraction of a full turn in degrees. Then say whether the angle is acute, a right angle,
obtuse or reflex.

1. $$\frac{5}{8}$$ of a turn
2. $$\frac{1}{12}$$ of a turn
3. $$\frac{3}{10}$$ of a turn
4. $$\frac{1}{4}$$ of a turn

:::solution

By :ref{#degree}, $$\frac{x}{360}$$ of a turn is $$x^\circ$$, so multiply each fraction by $$360$$
and compare with :ref{#right-angle}:

1. $$\frac{5}{8} \cdot 360 = 225$$, so $$225^\circ$$, which is reflex
2. $$\frac{1}{12} \cdot 360 = 30$$, so $$30^\circ$$, which is acute
3. $$\frac{3}{10} \cdot 360 = 108$$, so $$108^\circ$$, which is obtuse
4. $$\frac{1}{4} \cdot 360 = 90$$, so $$90^\circ$$, a right angle

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-measuring-line-ratio}

The point $$P$$ lies between $$Q$$ and $$R$$ on a line, and $$M$$ is not on the line. The angle
$$\angle QPM$$ is four times the angle $$\angle MPR$$. Find both angles.

:::solution

Write $$\angle MPR = x^\circ$$, so $$\angle QPM = 4x^\circ$$. By :ref{#angles-on-line},

```math
4x + x = 180
```

So $$5x = 180$$ and $$x = 36$$. The angles are $$\angle MPR = 36^\circ$$ and
$$\angle QPM = 144^\circ$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-measuring-three-rays}

The point $$P$$ lies between $$Q$$ and $$R$$ on a line. The rays $$PM$$ and $$PN$$ lie on the same
side of the line, with the ray $$PM$$ inside the angle $$\angle QPN$$, as in the figure. If
$$\angle QPM = 50^\circ$$ and $$\angle NPR = 60^\circ$$, find $$\angle MPN$$.

```plane
point Q -3 0 s
point P 0 0 s
point R 3 0 s
point M -1.67 1.99 nw
point N 1.3 2.25 ne
line Q R
ray P M
ray P N
angle M P Q label="50°" color=blue
angle R P N label="60°" color=orange
angle N P M label="?" color=green r=0.6
caption "Two rays on the same side of the line QR"
```

:::solution

The rays $$PQ$$ and $$PR$$ point in opposite directions, and $$N$$ is not on the line. So by
:ref{#angles-on-line},

```math
\angle QPN = 180^\circ - \angle NPR = 180^\circ - 60^\circ = 120^\circ
```

The ray $$PM$$ lies inside $$\angle QPN$$, so by :ref{#angle-addition},
$$\angle QPM + \angle MPN = \angle QPN$$. This gives $$50^\circ + \angle MPN = 120^\circ$$, so
$$\angle MPN = 70^\circ$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-measuring-inside-matters}

Three rays $$PQ$$, $$PR$$ and $$PM$$ start from $$P$$, with $$\angle QPR = 50^\circ$$ and
$$\angle RPM = 80^\circ$$. Someone concludes that $$\angle QPM = 130^\circ$$.

1. What does :ref{#angle-addition} need before it gives this answer?
2. Find $$\angle QPM$$ if instead the ray $$PQ$$ lies inside the angle $$\angle RPM$$.

:::solution

1. It needs the ray $$PR$$ to lie inside the angle $$\angle QPM$$. Only then do the two smaller
   angles sit side by side and make up $$\angle QPM$$.
2. If the ray $$PQ$$ lies inside $$\angle RPM$$, the same proposition gives
   $$\angle RPQ + \angle QPM = \angle RPM$$. So $$50^\circ + \angle QPM = 80^\circ$$, and
   $$\angle QPM = 30^\circ$$.

The same two measurements give different answers depending on which ray lies inside which angle.
That is why each proof says when it reads such a fact from the figure.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-measuring-sector-radius}

A sector cut out by an angle of $$40^\circ$$ has area $$4\pi$$. Find the radius of the disc.

:::solution

Let the radius be $$r$$. By :ref{#sector-area},

```math
\frac{40}{360} \cdot \pi r^2 = 4\pi
```

Dividing both sides by $$\pi$$ gives $$\frac{r^2}{9} = 4$$, so $$r^2 = 36$$. A radius is positive,
so $$r = 6$$. Check: $$\frac{40}{360} \cdot 36\pi = 4\pi$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-measuring-quarter-area}

A disc has radius $$4$$. What angle at its centre cuts out a sector with the same area as a whole
disc of radius $$2$$?

:::solution

By rule 1 of :ref{#disc-area}, the disc of radius $$2$$ has area $$4\pi$$, and the disc of radius
$$4$$ has area $$16\pi$$. An angle of $$x^\circ$$ cuts out a sector of area
$$\frac{x}{360} \cdot 16\pi$$ (:ref{#sector-area}). Setting this equal to $$4\pi$$ gives
$$\frac{x}{360} = \frac{1}{4}$$, so $$x = 90$$.

So a right angle does it. Halving the radius divides the area by $$4$$, so the small disc has the
same area as a quarter of the large one.

:::
