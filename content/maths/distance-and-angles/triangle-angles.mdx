---
title: Angles in triangles
description: The angles of any triangle, angles at parallel lines, and the area of a triangle.
---

Right triangles have been the main tool so far, and this page shows how far that tool reaches. Any
triangle can be cut into two right triangles by one perpendicular line. That cut proves that the
angles of every triangle add up to $$180^\circ$$, and it gives the area of every triangle. Along the
way it proves two facts about the angles where lines cross, which the exercises and later geometry
rely on.

## Opposite angles

Cross two sticks and look at the four angles around the crossing. The angles facing each other look
the same size, and they are. Both of them sit beside the same neighbouring angle on a straight line,
so each one is $$180^\circ$$ minus that neighbour.

```plane
point O 0 0 s
point X 3 1 n
point Y -2.5 2 ne
point X' -3 -1 s
point Y' 2.5 -2 sw
line X' X
line Y' Y
angle X O Y label="α" color=blue
angle X' O Y' label="α′" color=blue
angle Y O X' label="β" color=orange
caption "Where two lines cross, the opposite angles α and α′ are equal"
```

:::proposition[Opposite angles are equal]{#opposite-angles}

When two lines cross, the two angles opposite each other at the crossing are equal.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertical_angles"}

Label the angles as in the figure, with $$\alpha$$ and $$\alpha'$$ opposite each other and $$\beta$$
between them. The angles $$\alpha$$ and $$\beta$$ sit side by side on the line $$X'X$$, and
$$\beta$$ and $$\alpha'$$ sit side by side on the line $$Y'Y$$. By :ref{#angles-on-line},

```math
\alpha + \beta = 180^\circ \qquad \text{and} \qquad \beta + \alpha' = 180^\circ
```

Taking $$\beta$$ from both sides of each gives $$\alpha = 180^\circ - \beta = \alpha'$$.

:::

## The angles of a triangle

Draw any triangle, cut off its three corners and lay them side by side. They always fit together to
make a straight line. The proof below explains why, by dropping a perpendicular from one corner and
using what you already know about right triangles.

<TornCorners caption="Tear the three corners off a paper triangle and put their points together: they always fill a straight line" />

For a triangle $$\triangle ABC$$, write $$\angle A$$ for its angle at the corner $$A$$, and the same
for $$B$$ and $$C$$.

:::theorem[Angle sum of a triangle]{#triangle-angle-sum}

In every triangle $$\triangle ABC$$,

```math
\angle A + \angle B + \angle C = 180^\circ
```

:::

The perpendicular from $$C$$ to the line $$AB$$ meets it at a point $$F$$. There are three ways this
can fall: on one of the corners $$A$$ or $$B$$, between them, or outside the side $$AB$$. The two
main cases look like this:

```plane
point A 0 0 sw
point B 5 0 se
point C 1.8 3 n
point F 1.8 0 s
segment A B
segment B C
segment C A
segment C F color=purple dashed
right B F C color=purple
caption "Case 1: the foot F lies between A and B"
```

```plane
point A 0 0 sw
point B 3 0 s
point C 4.5 2.6 n
point F 4.5 0 se
segment A B
segment B C
segment C A
segment B F color=muted dashed
segment C F color=purple dashed
right C F A color=purple
caption "Case 2: the foot F lies outside the side AB, beyond B"
```

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sum_of_angles_of_a_triangle"}

Let $$F$$ be the point where the line through $$C$$ perpendicular to the line $$AB$$ meets it
(:ref{#perpendicular-rules}).

**Case 0: $$F$$ is a corner.** If $$F = A$$, the line $$CA$$ is perpendicular to $$AB$$, so the
triangle has a right angle at $$A$$. By :ref{#right-triangle-angles},
$$\angle B + \angle C = 90^\circ$$, so all three add to $$90^\circ + 90^\circ = 180^\circ$$. The
case $$F = B$$ is the same.

Otherwise $$A$$, $$B$$ and $$F$$ are three different points on one line, and by :ref{#segment-rules}
exactly one lies between the other two.

**Case 1: $$F$$ lies between $$A$$ and $$B$$.** The segment $$CF$$ cuts the triangle into two right
triangles, $$\triangle AFC$$ and $$\triangle BFC$$, each with its right angle at $$F$$. By
:ref{#right-triangle-angles},

```math
\angle A + \angle ACF = 90^\circ \qquad \text{and} \qquad \angle B + \angle BCF = 90^\circ
```

As the figure shows, the ray $$CF$$ splits $$\angle C$$ into $$\angle ACF$$ and $$\angle BCF$$, so
$$\angle C = \angle ACF + \angle BCF$$ (:ref{#angle-addition}). Adding the two equations gives
$$\angle A + \angle B + \angle C = 180^\circ$$.

**Case 2: $$F$$ lies outside the side $$AB$$.** Then $$A$$ or $$B$$ lies between the other corner
and $$F$$. Name the corners so that $$B$$ lies between $$A$$ and $$F$$. There are two right
triangles with the right angle at $$F$$: the large one $$\triangle AFC$$ and the small one
$$\triangle BFC$$. By :ref{#right-triangle-angles},

```math
\angle A + \angle ACF = 90^\circ \qquad \text{and} \qquad \angle CBF + \angle BCF = 90^\circ
```

Next, write the triangle's angles at $$B$$ and $$C$$ in terms of these:

- As the figure shows, the ray $$CB$$ splits $$\angle ACF$$ into $$\angle C$$ and $$\angle BCF$$, so
  $$\angle C = \angle ACF - \angle BCF$$ (:ref{#angle-addition})
- The angles $$\angle B$$ and $$\angle CBF$$ sit side by side on the line $$AF$$, so
  $$\angle B = 180^\circ - \angle CBF$$ (:ref{#angles-on-line})

So the sum is

```math
\begin{aligned}
\angle A + \angle B + \angle C &= \angle A + (180^\circ - \angle CBF) + (\angle ACF - \angle BCF) \\
&= (\angle A + \angle ACF) + 180^\circ - (\angle CBF + \angle BCF) \\
&= 90^\circ + 180^\circ - 90^\circ = 180^\circ
\end{aligned}
```

:::

:::example[A missing angle]

A triangle has angles of $$47^\circ$$ and $$68^\circ$$. Its third angle is
$$180^\circ - 47^\circ - 68^\circ = 65^\circ$$. You never need to measure the last angle of a
triangle: the other two decide it.

:::

The theorem also limits what a triangle can look like. Two angles of $$90^\circ$$ or more would
already add up to $$180^\circ$$ or more, leaving nothing for the third. So a triangle has at most
one right angle or obtuse angle.

## Angles at parallel lines

A line crossing two parallel lines meets them at the same slant. So the angles it makes at the first
line repeat at the second. The two kinds of matching pairs have names:

- **Corresponding angles**: in the same position at each crossing, for example both above their
  parallel line and to the right of the crossing line
- **Alternate angles**: on opposite sides of the crossing line and between the two parallels,
  forming a Z shape

```plane
point P' 0 0 s
point P 2 2.5 n
point F 2 0 s
point R 4.5 0 hidden
point L -2.5 2.5 hidden
point K 2.8 3.5 hidden
point E 4.5 2.5 hidden
point W -2.5 0 hidden
line W R
line L E
line P' K
segment P F color=purple dashed
right R F P color=purple
angle F P' P label="B" color=blue
angle L P P' label="B′" color=orange
caption "The alternate angles B and B′, with the perpendicular PF used in the proof"
```

:::theorem[Angles where a line crosses parallels]{#parallel-angles}

Let a line $$K$$ cross two parallel lines $$L_1$$ and $$L_2$$. Then its alternate angles are equal,
and its corresponding angles are equal.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transversal_(geometry)"}

Let $$K$$ meet $$L_1$$ at $$P$$ and $$L_2$$ at $$P'$$.

**If $$K$$ is perpendicular to the parallels.** A line perpendicular to one of two parallel lines is
perpendicular to the other (rule 2 of :ref{#perpendicular-rules}). So every angle at both crossings
is a right angle, and all of them are equal.

**Otherwise,** let $$F$$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $$P$$ to $$L_2$$, as in the figure.
The line $$PF$$ is perpendicular to $$L_2$$, so it is also perpendicular to $$L_1$$ (rule 2 of
:ref{#perpendicular-rules}). Let $$B$$ be the angle at $$P'$$ between $$P'F$$ and $$P'P$$. Let
$$B'$$ be the alternate angle at $$P$$: the angle between $$PP'$$ and $$L_1$$ on the other side of
$$K$$ from $$B$$.

The triangle $$\triangle PFP'$$ has a right angle at $$F$$, so by :ref{#right-triangle-angles},

```math
B + \angle FPP' = 90^\circ
```

At $$P$$, the line $$PF$$ meets $$L_1$$ at a right angle, and the figure shows the ray $$PP'$$
splitting that right angle into $$\angle FPP'$$ and $$B'$$. So $$B' + \angle FPP' = 90^\circ$$
(:ref{#angle-addition}). Comparing the two equations gives $$B = B'$$. The other pair of alternate
angles are the angles beside these on a straight line, $$180^\circ - B$$ and $$180^\circ - B'$$
(:ref{#angles-on-line}), so they are equal too.

**Corresponding angles.** Each corresponding angle at $$P$$ is opposite one of the alternate angles
at $$P$$, so it equals that angle (:ref{#opposite-angles}). That alternate angle equals its partner
at $$P'$$, which is the corresponding angle there.

:::

:::example[Filling in angles]

A road crosses two parallel railway lines, making an angle of $$70^\circ$$ with the first. By
:ref{#parallel-angles}, the matching angle at the second line is also $$70^\circ$$. The angle beside
it on the line is $$180^\circ - 70^\circ = 110^\circ$$ (:ref{#angles-on-line}), and the angle
opposite each of these is the same size (:ref{#opposite-angles}). So the eight angles at the two
crossings take only two values: $$70^\circ$$ and $$110^\circ$$.

:::

## The area of a triangle

A right triangle is half a rectangle, so its area is half the product of its legs. Any other
triangle is two right triangles put together, or one right triangle with another cut away. Either
way, adding or subtracting the two right triangles gives the same rule: half the base times the
height.

:::definition[Base and height]{#height}

Choose one side of a triangle as its **base**. The **height** is the distance from the opposite
corner to the line containing the base (:ref{#point-line-distance}). It is measured along the
perpendicular from that corner, which may meet the line outside the base.

:::

:::theorem[Area of a triangle]{#triangle-area}

A triangle with base $$b$$ and height $$h$$ has area $$\frac{1}{2}bh$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Area_of_Triangle_in_Terms_of_Side_and_Altitude"}

Let the triangle be $$\triangle ABC$$ with base $$AB$$, so $$b = d(A, B)$$. Let $$F$$ be the foot of
the perpendicular from $$C$$ to the line $$AB$$, so $$h = d(C, F)$$. The cases are the same as for
:ref{#triangle-angle-sum}.

**Case 0: $$F$$ is a corner.** If $$F = B$$, the triangle is a right triangle with legs $$b$$ and
$$h$$, and its area is $$\frac{1}{2}bh$$ by :ref{#right-triangle-area}. The case $$F = A$$ is the
same.

**Case 1: $$F$$ lies between $$A$$ and $$B$$.** The segment $$CF$$ cuts the triangle into the right
triangles $$\triangle AFC$$ and $$\triangle BFC$$, which don't overlap. By rule 2 of
:ref{#area-rules}, the area of the triangle is the sum of their areas, so by
:ref{#right-triangle-area},

```math
\text{area} = \tfrac{1}{2} d(A, F)\,h + \tfrac{1}{2} d(F, B)\,h = \tfrac{1}{2}\big(d(A, F) + d(F, B)\big)h
```

Since $$F$$ is between $$A$$ and $$B$$, $$d(A, F) + d(F, B) = b$$ (:ref{#segment-rules}). So the
area is $$\frac{1}{2}bh$$.

**Case 2: $$F$$ lies outside the side $$AB$$.** As before, name the corners so that $$B$$ lies
between $$A$$ and $$F$$. The large right triangle $$\triangle AFC$$ is cut by $$CB$$ into
$$\triangle ABC$$ and the small right triangle $$\triangle BFC$$, which don't overlap. By rule 2 of
:ref{#area-rules}, the area of $$\triangle AFC$$ is the sum of the other two areas. So, taking away
the area of $$\triangle BFC$$ and using :ref{#right-triangle-area},

```math
\text{area of } \triangle ABC = \tfrac{1}{2} d(A, F)\,h - \tfrac{1}{2} d(B, F)\,h = \tfrac{1}{2}\big(d(A, F) - d(B, F)\big)h
```

Since $$B$$ is between $$A$$ and $$F$$, $$d(A, F) = d(A, B) + d(B, F)$$ (:ref{#segment-rules}), so
$$d(A, F) - d(B, F) = b$$. Again the area is $$\frac{1}{2}bh$$.

:::

```plane
point A 0 0 sw
point B 3 0 s
point C 4.5 2.6 n
point F 4.5 0 se
polygon A B C color=blue
segment A B label="b" side=right
segment B C
segment C A
segment B F color=muted dashed
segment C F color=purple dashed label="h" side=right
right C F A color=purple
caption "When the foot F falls outside the base, the height is still measured to the line AB"
```

:::example[Any side can be the base]

A triangle has corners $$A$$, $$B$$ and $$C$$, with $$d(A, B) = 6$$ and the height from $$C$$ equal
to $$4$$. Its area is $$\frac{1}{2} \cdot 6 \cdot 4 = 12$$. If $$d(B, C) = 5$$, the height from
$$A$$ to the line $$BC$$ must satisfy $$\frac{1}{2} \cdot 5 \cdot h = 12$$, so $$h = 4.8$$. The area
is the same whichever side you choose as the base, so one height tells you the others.

:::

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on angles in triangles, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-triangle-angles-crossing}

Two lines cross, and one of the four angles at the crossing is $$70^\circ$$. Find the other three.

:::solution

Each angle next to the $$70^\circ$$ angle sits beside it on a straight line, so it is
$$180^\circ - 70^\circ = 110^\circ$$ (:ref{#angles-on-line}). The angle opposite the $$70^\circ$$
angle is $$70^\circ$$ (:ref{#opposite-angles}).

So the four angles are $$70^\circ$$, $$110^\circ$$, $$70^\circ$$ and $$110^\circ$$ in turn. Check:
they add up to $$360^\circ$$, a full turn.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-triangle-angles-missing}

1. In $$\triangle ABC$$, $$\angle A = 48^\circ$$ and $$\angle B$$ is $$20^\circ$$ larger than
   $$\angle C$$. Find $$\angle B$$ and $$\angle C$$.
2. In $$\triangle PQM$$, $$\angle P = \angle Q$$ and $$\angle M = 40^\circ$$. Find $$\angle P$$.

:::solution

Both parts use :ref{#triangle-angle-sum}.

1. $$\angle B + \angle C = 180^\circ - 48^\circ = 132^\circ$$. Writing $$\angle C = x^\circ$$ gives
   $$x + (x + 20) = 132$$, so $$x = 56$$. So $$\angle C = 56^\circ$$ and $$\angle B = 76^\circ$$.
2. $$\angle P + \angle Q = 180^\circ - 40^\circ = 140^\circ$$, and the two are equal, so each is
   $$70^\circ$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-triangle-angles-impossible}

1. Can a triangle have angles of $$100^\circ$$ and $$85^\circ$$?
2. Show that a triangle has at most one angle of $$90^\circ$$ or more.

:::solution

Each angle of a triangle is more than $$0^\circ$$, since its corners don't lie on one line.

1. No. The third angle would be $$180^\circ - 100^\circ - 85^\circ = -5^\circ$$
   (:ref{#triangle-angle-sum}), and no angle is negative.
2. If two angles were each $$90^\circ$$ or more, they would add up to at least $$180^\circ$$. The
   third angle would then be $$0^\circ$$ or less, which is impossible.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-triangle-angles-zigzag}

The lines $$L_1$$ and $$L_2$$ are parallel. The point $$A$$ is on $$L_1$$, the point $$B$$ is on
$$L_2$$, and $$C$$ lies between the two lines, as in the figure. The angle at $$A$$ between $$L_1$$
and $$AC$$ is $$30^\circ$$, and the angle at $$B$$ between $$L_2$$ and $$BC$$ is $$45^\circ$$. Find
$$\angle ACB$$.

```plane
point U -6 2 hidden
point V 2 2 hidden
point S -6 -2 hidden
point T 2 -2 hidden
point A -3.46 2 n
point B -2 -2 s
point C 0 0 e
point X 0 2 hidden
point Y 0 -2 hidden
line U V
line S T
segment A C
segment C B
angle C A X label="30°" color=blue
angle Y B C label="45°" color=orange
angle A C B label="?" color=green
text 1.8 2.4 "L_1"
text 1.8 -1.6 "L_2"
caption "A zigzag between two parallel lines"
```

:::solution

Draw the line $$K$$ through $$C$$ parallel to $$L_1$$ (:ref{#line-rules}). Since
$$L_2 \parallel L_1$$, the line $$K$$ is parallel to $$L_2$$ as well (:ref{#parallel-transitive}).
Let $$W$$ be a point of $$K$$ on the same side of $$C$$ as $$A$$ and $$B$$.

- The line $$AC$$ crosses the parallels $$L_1$$ and $$K$$. The $$30^\circ$$ angle at $$A$$ and the
  angle $$\angle ACW$$ are alternate angles, forming a Z, so $$\angle ACW = 30^\circ$$
  (:ref{#parallel-angles}).
- In the same way, the line $$BC$$ crosses the parallels $$L_2$$ and $$K$$, and
  $$\angle WCB = 45^\circ$$.

The figure shows the ray $$CW$$ inside the angle $$\angle ACB$$, so by :ref{#angle-addition},
$$\angle ACB = 30^\circ + 45^\circ = 75^\circ$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-triangle-angles-same-area}

The points $$A$$ and $$B$$ are different, and $$L$$ is a line parallel to the line $$AB$$ but
different from it. Prove that for any two points $$C$$ and $$D$$ of $$L$$, the triangles
$$\triangle ABC$$ and $$\triangle ABD$$ have the same area.

:::solution

The line $$L$$ is different from the line $$AB$$ and parallel to it, so they never meet. So $$C$$
and $$D$$ are not on the line $$AB$$, and both triangles exist. Take $$AB$$ as the base of each, so
both have base $$d(A, B)$$ (:ref{#height}).

The line through $$C$$ perpendicular to $$L$$ is perpendicular to the line $$AB$$ too (rule 2 of
:ref{#perpendicular-rules}). So it meets the line $$AB$$ at the foot $$F$$ of the perpendicular from
$$C$$, and the height from $$C$$ is $$d(C, F)$$. This is the distance between the two parallel lines
(:ref{#parallel-distance}). The same holds for $$D$$, so both triangles have the same height $$h$$.

By :ref{#triangle-area}, both have area $$\frac{1}{2}\,d(A, B)\,h$$. Sliding the top corner along a
line parallel to the base never changes the area.

:::
