The Pythagoras theorem turns questions about distance into questions about squares of numbers. This
page uses it to find the points that are the same distance from two given points, and the point of a
line that is closest to a given point. The first answer leads to a circle through the three corners
of any triangle.

## The midpoint and the perpendicular bisector

Fold a sheet of paper so that a point $$P$$ lands exactly on a point $$Q$$. The crease crosses the
segment $$PQ$$ at its middle, and it meets the segment square on. Every point on the crease is the
same distance from $$P$$ as from $$Q$$, because folding carries one distance onto the other. This
section names the crease, and the rest of the page proves what the fold suggests.

<PaperFold caption="Fold P onto Q and open the sheet out: the crease meets PQ square on at its midpoint O, and any point X on it is as far from P as from Q" />

:::definition[Midpoint]{#midpoint}

The **midpoint** of a segment $$PQ$$ is the point $$O$$ on the segment with $$d(P, O) = d(O, Q)$$.

:::

Every segment has exactly one midpoint. Any point $$O$$ of the segment $$PQ$$ splits its length into
two parts, $$d(P, O) + d(O, Q) = d(P, Q)$$ (:ref{#segment-rules}). So $$O$$ is the midpoint exactly
when both parts are $$\frac{1}{2} d(P, Q)$$, that is, exactly when
$$d(P, O) = \frac{1}{2} d(P, Q)$$. By the same rules, exactly one point of the segment is at that
distance from $$P$$.

:::definition[Perpendicular bisector]{#perpendicular-bisector}

The **perpendicular bisector** of a segment $$PQ$$ is the line through its midpoint that is
perpendicular to $$PQ$$. There is exactly one such line, by rule 1 of :ref{#perpendicular-rules}.

:::

```plane
point P -2.5 0 sw
point Q 2.5 0 se
point O 0 0 sw
point T 0 3 hidden
point B 0 -1 hidden
segment P Q
line B T color=purple
right Q O T color=purple
ticks P O 1
ticks O Q 1
caption "The perpendicular bisector of PQ passes through its midpoint O, square on to PQ"
```

Before the main theorem comes a fact about the line $$PQ$$ itself. On that line, the only point the
same distance from $$P$$ and $$Q$$ is the midpoint. A point of the line outside the segment is
always nearer one end than the other.

:::lemma[Equal distances on the line]{#equidistant-on-line}

Let $$P$$ and $$Q$$ be different points, and let $$O$$ be a point on the line $$PQ$$ with
$$d(O, P) = d(O, Q)$$. Then $$O$$ is the midpoint of $$PQ$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midpoint"}

First, $$O$$ is not $$P$$. If it were, then $$d(P, Q) = d(O, Q) = d(O, P) = 0$$, so $$P = Q$$ by
:ref{#distance-rules}, but $$P$$ and $$Q$$ are different. In the same way, $$O$$ is not $$Q$$.

So $$O$$, $$P$$ and $$Q$$ are three different points on one line, and by :ref{#segment-rules}
exactly one of them lies between the other two. Suppose $$P$$ lies between $$O$$ and $$Q$$. Then

```math
d(O, Q) = d(O, P) + d(P, Q)
```

Since $$d(O, Q) = d(O, P)$$, this leaves $$d(P, Q) = 0$$, which is impossible
(:ref{#basic-proofs/contradiction}). The same argument rules out $$Q$$ lying between $$O$$ and
$$P$$.

So $$O$$ lies between $$P$$ and $$Q$$, on the segment, with $$d(P, O) = d(O, Q)$$. That makes it the
midpoint.

:::

## Points the same distance from two points

The folding picture says that the points the same distance from $$P$$ and $$Q$$ are exactly the
points of the perpendicular bisector. Try it below: slide $$M$$ along its line and compare its two
distances. They agree at one place only, where $$M$$ crosses the bisector.

<BisectorDistances />

The proof drops a perpendicular from $$M$$ to the line $$PQ$$. Its foot $$F$$ makes two right
triangles, $$PFM$$ and $$QFM$$, which share the leg $$FM$$. By the Pythagoras theorem, the distances
from $$M$$ match exactly when the other legs, $$PF$$ and $$QF$$, match too.

```plane
point P -2.5 0 sw
point Q 2.5 0 se
point F 0.8 0 s
point M 0.8 3 n
segment P Q
segment F M color=purple
segment P M color=blue dashed
segment Q M color=orange dashed
right Q F M color=purple
caption "The foot F of the perpendicular from M splits the picture into two right triangles"
```

:::theorem[Points on the perpendicular bisector]{#perpendicular-bisector-rule}

Let $$P$$ and $$Q$$ be different points. A point $$M$$ satisfies $$d(P, M) = d(Q, M)$$ exactly when
$$M$$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $$PQ$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://www.cuemath.com/geometry/perpendicular-bisector-theorem/"}

Let $$K$$ be the line through $$M$$ perpendicular to $$PQ$$ (rule 1 of :ref{#perpendicular-rules}),
and let $$F$$ be the point where $$K$$ meets the line $$PQ$$. If $$M$$ is on the line $$PQ$$, then
$$K$$ meets it at $$M$$ itself, so $$F = M$$.

**Step 1: the squared distances.** When $$F$$, $$P$$ and $$M$$ are three different points, they form
a right triangle with its right angle at $$F$$, so by :ref{#pythagoras},

```math
d(P, M)^2 = d(P, F)^2 + d(F, M)^2
```

This also holds when two of the points are the same. If $$F = P$$, then $$d(P, F) = 0$$ and both
sides equal $$d(F, M)^2$$. If $$F = M$$, then $$d(F, M) = 0$$ and both sides equal $$d(P, F)^2$$.
The same reasoning gives $$d(Q, M)^2 = d(Q, F)^2 + d(F, M)^2$$.

**Step 2: a chain of equivalent statements.** Distances are never negative, so two distances are
equal exactly when their squares are (:ref{#real-numbers/equal-squares}). Using step 1, and taking
$$d(F, M)^2$$ from both sides, each line below is true exactly when the next one is
(:ref{#basic-proofs/exactly-when}):

1. $$d(P, M) = d(Q, M)$$
2. $$d(P, M)^2 = d(Q, M)^2$$
3. $$d(P, F)^2 = d(Q, F)^2$$
4. $$d(P, F) = d(Q, F)$$
5. $$F$$ is the midpoint of $$PQ$$

Statement 4 leads to 5 by :ref{#equidistant-on-line}, and 5 leads back to 4 by the definition of the
midpoint.

**Step 3: the foot and the bisector.** It remains to show that $$F$$ is the midpoint exactly when
$$M$$ is on the perpendicular bisector.

- If $$F$$ is the midpoint, then $$K$$ is perpendicular to $$PQ$$ and passes through the midpoint.
  So $$K$$ is the perpendicular bisector, and $$M$$ is on it.
- If $$M$$ is on the perpendicular bisector, then the bisector is a line through $$M$$ perpendicular
  to $$PQ$$. There is only one such line (rule 1 of :ref{#perpendicular-rules}), so the bisector is
  $$K$$. The bisector meets the line $$PQ$$ only at the midpoint (:ref{#crossing-lines}), so $$F$$
  is the midpoint.

:::

:::example[Finding where to meet]

Two friends live at points $$P$$ and $$Q$$, $$8$$ km apart along a straight road. They want a café
that is the same distance from both homes, and $$5$$ km from each. So the café must be on the
perpendicular bisector of $$PQ$$ (:ref{#perpendicular-bisector-rule}).

The bisector meets the road at the midpoint $$O$$, $$4$$ km from each home. A café at $$M$$ on the
bisector makes a right triangle $$POM$$, with its right angle at $$O$$, hypotenuse $$5$$ and one leg
$$4$$. So $$d(O, M)^2 = 5^2 - 4^2 = 9$$, and the café is $$3$$ km from the road, on either side of
it.

:::

## The hypotenuse is the longest side

In a right triangle, the side opposite the right angle is the longest. Pythagoras makes this
precise: $$c^2$$ is $$a^2$$ with the positive number $$b^2$$ added on, so $$c^2$$ is the larger
square.

:::corollary[The hypotenuse is longest]{#hypotenuse-longest}

In a right triangle with legs $$a$$ and $$b$$ and hypotenuse $$c$$, the hypotenuse is longer than
each leg: $$c > a$$ and $$c > b$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypotenuse"}

The corners of a triangle are different points, so $$a$$ and $$b$$ are positive
(:ref{#distance-rules}). By :ref{#pythagoras}, $$c^2 = a^2 + b^2$$, and $$b^2 > 0$$, so
$$c^2 > a^2$$. Both $$a$$ and $$c$$ are positive, so $$c > a$$ by
:ref{#real-numbers/power-order-converse}. Swapping the roles of $$a$$ and $$b$$ gives $$c > b$$.

:::

## The shortest distance to a line

Stand in a field next to a long straight fence and walk to it by the shortest route. You walk
straight at it, square on. Any slanted path ends further along the fence, and it is longer. The
reason is :ref{#hypotenuse-longest}: a slanted path is the hypotenuse of a right triangle, and the
straight path is one of its legs.

<FenceWalk caption="Seen from above: the path that meets the fence square on is the shortest, and every slanted path is longer" />

<ShortestDistance />

:::theorem[The shortest distance to a line]{#shortest-distance}

Let $$L$$ be a line and $$P$$ a point not on it, and let $$F$$ be the foot of the perpendicular from
$$P$$ to $$L$$. Then for every point $$M$$ of $$L$$ other than $$F$$,

```math
d(P, M) > d(P, F)
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Perpendicular_is_Shortest_Straight_Line_from_Point_to_Straight_Line"}

The points $$P$$, $$F$$ and $$M$$ are all different: $$P$$ is not on $$L$$, and $$M \neq F$$. The
segment $$PF$$ is perpendicular to $$L$$, and $$M$$ is on $$L$$, so $$PFM$$ is a right triangle with
its right angle at $$F$$. Its hypotenuse is $$PM$$ and $$PF$$ is a leg, so $$d(P, M) > d(P, F)$$ by
:ref{#hypotenuse-longest}.

:::

```plane
point P 1 3 n
point F 1 0 s
point M 4 0 s
point A -2 0 hidden
line A M
segment P F color=purple
segment P M color=blue dashed
right M F P color=purple
caption "PF is a leg and PM is the hypotenuse, so PM is longer"
```

The theorem gives a single number for how far a point is from a line.

:::definition[Distance from a point to a line]{#point-line-distance}

The **distance from a point $$P$$ to a line $$L$$** is $$d(P, F)$$, where $$F$$ is the foot of the
perpendicular from $$P$$ to $$L$$. It is the smallest distance from $$P$$ to any point of $$L$$. If
$$P$$ is on $$L$$, the distance is $$0$$.

:::

## A circle through three points

A circle is every point at one distance from its centre. So a circle through three points $$P$$,
$$Q$$ and $$M$$ needs a centre that is the same distance from all three.
:ref{#perpendicular-bisector-rule} says where to look:

- the points the same distance from $$P$$ as from $$Q$$ make up the perpendicular bisector of $$PQ$$
- the points the same distance from $$Q$$ as from $$M$$ make up the perpendicular bisector of $$QM$$

A point on both bisectors is the same distance from all three corners, so it is the centre you want.
Two things need checking. The two bisectors must cross somewhere, since two parallel lines never
meet. And once they do, the third bisector, that of $$PM$$, should pass through the same point. Move
the corner $$M$$ below and watch both happen.

<Circumcircle />

:::corollary[The circle through a triangle's corners]{#circumcentre}

Let $$\triangle PQM$$ be a triangle. The perpendicular bisectors of its three sides meet at one
point $$O$$, and $$d(O, P) = d(O, Q) = d(O, M)$$. So the circle with centre $$O$$ and radius
$$d(O, P)$$ passes through $$P$$, $$Q$$ and $$M$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://www.cuemath.com/geometry/circumcenter/"}

Let $$L_1$$ be the perpendicular bisector of $$PQ$$, and $$L_2$$ that of $$QM$$.

**The two bisectors meet.** Suppose $$L_1$$ and $$L_2$$ were parallel. The line $$PQ$$ is
perpendicular to $$L_1$$, so it is perpendicular to $$L_2$$ as well (rule 2 of
:ref{#perpendicular-rules}). The line $$QM$$ is also perpendicular to $$L_2$$, so the lines $$PQ$$
and $$QM$$ would be parallel (:ref{#perpendiculars-parallel}).

But the lines $$PQ$$ and $$QM$$ both pass through $$Q$$. A line counts as parallel to itself, so
both would be lines through $$Q$$ parallel to $$QM$$, and rule 2 of :ref{#line-rules} allows only
one. So $$PQ$$ and $$QM$$ would be the same line, and $$P$$, $$Q$$ and $$M$$ would lie on it. A
triangle's corners never lie on one line, so this is impossible. So $$L_1$$ and $$L_2$$ are not
parallel, and they meet in exactly one point $$O$$ (:ref{#crossing-lines}).

**The distances.** Since $$O$$ is on $$L_1$$, $$d(O, P) = d(O, Q)$$, and since $$O$$ is on $$L_2$$,
$$d(O, Q) = d(O, M)$$ (:ref{#perpendicular-bisector-rule}). So all three distances are equal. In
particular $$d(O, P) = d(O, M)$$, so by the same theorem $$O$$ is on the perpendicular bisector of
$$PM$$ too.

:::

The point $$O$$ is called the **circumcentre** of the triangle, and the circle is its
**circumcircle**.

The circumcentre need not lie inside the triangle. Move $$M$$ in the diagram above to see three
cases:

- when every angle is smaller than a right angle, $$O$$ lies inside the triangle
- when one angle is a right angle, $$O$$ is the midpoint of the side opposite it
- when one angle is wider than a right angle, $$O$$ lies outside, beyond the side opposite it

The same idea finds the centre of a round plate from a broken piece of it. Mark three points on the
rim and draw the perpendicular bisectors of two of the segments between them. The bisectors cross at
the centre of the plate.

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on perpendicular bisectors, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-perpendicular-bisectors-which-point}

The points $$P$$ and $$Q$$ have $$d(P, Q) = 6$$, and $$O$$ is the midpoint of $$PQ$$. A point $$M$$
has $$d(P, M) = d(Q, M) = 5$$, and a point $$N$$ has $$d(P, N) = 4$$ and $$d(Q, N) = 5$$.

1. Which of $$M$$ and $$N$$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $$PQ$$?
2. Find $$d(O, M)$$.

:::solution

1. By :ref{#perpendicular-bisector-rule}, a point lies on the bisector exactly when it is the same
   distance from $$P$$ and $$Q$$. So $$M$$ is on it, and $$N$$ is not.
2. $$M$$ is not $$O$$, since $$d(P, O) = 3$$ and $$d(P, M) = 5$$. So $$\triangle POM$$ is a right
   triangle with its right angle at $$O$$, legs $$d(P, O) = 3$$ and $$d(O, M)$$, and hypotenuse
   $$5$$. By :ref{#pythagoras-sides}, $$d(O, M) = \sqrt{25 - 9} = 4$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-perpendicular-bisectors-far-point}

The segment $$PQ$$ has length $$14$$, with midpoint $$O$$. A point $$M$$ on the perpendicular
bisector has $$d(P, M) = 25$$. Find $$d(Q, M)$$ and $$d(O, M)$$.

:::solution

$$M$$ is on the bisector, so $$d(Q, M) = d(P, M) = 25$$ (:ref{#perpendicular-bisector-rule}).

The triangle $$\triangle POM$$ has a right angle at $$O$$, with $$d(P, O) = 7$$ and hypotenuse
$$25$$. So by :ref{#pythagoras-sides},

```math
d(O, M) = \sqrt{25^2 - 7^2} = \sqrt{625 - 49} = \sqrt{576} = 24
```

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-perpendicular-bisectors-circle-centre}

The points $$P$$ and $$Q$$ have $$d(P, Q) = 10$$. A circle of radius $$13$$ passes through both.
Where can its centre be?

:::solution

Let the centre be $$X$$. The circle passes through $$P$$ and $$Q$$, so $$d(X, P) = d(X, Q) = 13$$
(:ref{#circle}). By :ref{#perpendicular-bisector-rule}, $$X$$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of
$$PQ$$.

Let $$O$$ be the midpoint, so $$d(P, O) = 5$$. The triangle $$\triangle POX$$ has a right angle at
$$O$$ and hypotenuse $$13$$, so $$d(O, X) = \sqrt{169 - 25} = \sqrt{144} = 12$$
(:ref{#pythagoras-sides}). So the centre is one of the two points of the bisector at distance $$12$$
from $$O$$, one on each side of $$PQ$$. Both work, so two different circles of radius $$13$$ pass
through $$P$$ and $$Q$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-perpendicular-bisectors-two-points}

Let $$P$$ and $$Q$$ be different points. Two different points $$M$$ and $$N$$ are each the same
distance from $$P$$ as from $$Q$$. Prove that the line $$MN$$ is the perpendicular bisector of
$$PQ$$.

:::solution

By :ref{#perpendicular-bisector-rule}, both $$M$$ and $$N$$ lie on the perpendicular bisector of
$$PQ$$. So the bisector is a line through the two different points $$M$$ and $$N$$. By rule 1 of
:ref{#line-rules}, only one line passes through them, the line $$MN$$. So the bisector is the line
$$MN$$.

This is how the bisector is drawn with compasses: two arcs of the same radius around $$P$$ and $$Q$$
cross at two points, and the line through them is the bisector.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-perpendicular-bisectors-equal-squares}

Step 2 of the proof of :ref{#perpendicular-bisector-rule} goes from $$d(P, F)^2 = d(Q, F)^2$$ to
$$d(P, F) = d(Q, F)$$. Give two numbers with equal squares that are not equal. Why is the step safe
here?

:::solution

For example, $$(-3)^2 = 9 = 3^2$$, but $$-3 \ne 3$$. Equal squares only say that two numbers have
the same size, and one may be the negative of the other.

The step is safe because distances are never negative (:ref{#distance-rules}). Two numbers that are
not negative and have equal squares are equal (:ref{#real-numbers/equal-squares}).

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-perpendicular-bisectors-parallel-lines}

The lines $$L$$ and $$L'$$ are different and parallel, and the distance between them is $$4$$
(:ref{#parallel-distance}). Let $$P$$ be a point of $$L$$. Show that the distance from $$P$$ to the
line $$L'$$ is $$4$$, and that every point of $$L'$$ is at least $$4$$ from $$P$$.

:::solution

Different parallel lines never meet, so $$P$$ is not on $$L'$$. The line through $$P$$ perpendicular
to $$L$$ meets $$L'$$ at a point $$P'$$, with $$d(P, P') = 4$$ by :ref{#parallel-distance}. This
line is perpendicular to $$L'$$ as well (rule 2 of :ref{#perpendicular-rules}), so $$P'$$ is the
foot of the perpendicular from $$P$$ to $$L'$$.

So the distance from $$P$$ to $$L'$$ is $$d(P, P') = 4$$ (:ref{#point-line-distance}). By
:ref{#shortest-distance}, every other point $$M$$ of $$L'$$ has $$d(P, M) > 4$$.

:::
