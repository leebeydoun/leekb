  Long division of polynomials, why it always gives one quotient and one remainder, the remainder
  theorem, and rational functions written as a polynomial plus a proper part.
---

Imagine sharing a bag of $$47$$ sweets among $$6$$ children. You hand out a round of $$6$$, then
another, and after $$7$$ rounds $$42$$ sweets are gone and $$5$$ are still in the bag. Five is too
few for another round, so each child gets $$7$$ and $$5$$ are left over. The pile left over is
always smaller than the number of children, because otherwise you could hand out one more round.

Polynomials can be shared out in the same way. Dividing one polynomial by another gives a
**quotient** and a **remainder**, and the remainder is smaller in the sense that its degree is lower
than the degree of the polynomial you divide by. In this guide we'll learn the long division that
finds them, prove that it always works, and then use the remainder to find roots and to read the
graphs of fractions of polynomials.

## Whole numbers first

You have met division with a remainder for whole numbers in :ref{#numbers/division-remainder}:
dividing $$n$$ by $$d > 0$$ gives $$n = dq + r$$ with $$0 \le r < d$$. The sweets are the case
$$47 = 6 \cdot 7 + 5$$. Long division at school finds $$q$$ one digit at a time, and each digit
stands for a batch of $$d$$s taken away.

Take $$452 \div 13$$. Thirteen hundreds would be $$1300$$, more than $$452$$, so the first batch is
tens of thirteens: $$13$$ goes into $$45$$ three times, so $$30$$ thirteens, which is $$390$$, fit
into $$452$$. Taking them away leaves $$62$$, and $$4$$ more thirteens, which is $$52$$, leave
$$10$$:

```math
\begin{array}{lr}
 & 452 \\
\text{take away } 30 \times 13 & 390 \\
\hline
\text{leaves} & 62 \\
\text{take away } 4 \times 13 & 52 \\
\hline
\text{leaves} & 10
\end{array}
```

Since $$10 < 13$$, we stop. We took away $$30 + 4 = 34$$ thirteens in all, so
$$452 = 34 \cdot 13 + 10$$. As a check, $$34 \cdot 13 = 442$$ and $$442 + 10 = 452$$. Each batch was
chosen to clear the leading digits of what was left, and that is the idea to carry over to
polynomials.

## Long division of polynomials

For polynomials the batches are multiples of the divisor $$g$$ by a single term, such as $$2x$$ or
$$3$$. Each one is chosen so that its top term matches the top term of what is left, so subtracting
it makes that term cancel. The degree drops every time, and we stop once the degree of what is left
is below the degree of $$g$$.

Here is a worked case. Divide

```math
f(x) = 2x^3 + x^2 - 5x + 4 \quad\text{by}\quad g(x) = x^2 - x + 2
```

**Round 1.** The top term of $$f$$ is $$2x^3$$, and the top term of $$g$$ is $$x^2$$. To turn
$$x^2$$ into $$2x^3$$ you multiply it by $$2x$$, so $$2x$$ is the first term of the quotient.
Multiplying the whole of $$g$$ by $$2x$$ gives $$2x^3 - 2x^2 + 4x$$, and we take that away from
$$f$$. The $$2x^3$$ terms cancel, because $$2x$$ was chosen to make them match.

**Round 2.** What is left is $$3x^2 - 9x + 4$$, with top term $$3x^2$$. To turn $$x^2$$ into
$$3x^2$$ you multiply by $$3$$, so $$3$$ is the next term of the quotient. Taking away
$$3g(x) = 3x^2 - 3x + 6$$ leaves $$-6x - 2$$.

**Stop.** The degree of $$-6x - 2$$ is $$1$$, less than $$2$$, the degree of $$g$$. Laid out in
columns, one for each power, the two rounds look like the ladder for $$452 \div 13$$:

```math
\begin{array}{lrrrr}
 & 2x^3 & {} + x^2 & {} - 5x & {} + 4 \\
\text{take away } 2x \cdot g(x) & 2x^3 & {} - 2x^2 & {} + 4x & \\
\hline
\text{leaves} & & 3x^2 & {} - 9x & {} + 4 \\
\text{take away } 3 \cdot g(x) & & 3x^2 & {} - 3x & {} + 6 \\
\hline
\text{leaves} & & & -6x & {} - 2
\end{array}
```

We took away $$2x \cdot g(x)$$ and then $$3 \cdot g(x)$$, so $$(2x + 3)\,g(x)$$ in all. So the
quotient is $$q(x) = 2x + 3$$, the remainder is $$r(x) = -6x - 2$$, and

```math
2x^3 + x^2 - 5x + 4 = (2x + 3)(x^2 - x + 2) + (-6x - 2)
```

Check it at $$x = 1$$. The left side is $$2 + 1 - 5 + 4 = 2$$. On the right, $$q(1) = 5$$,
$$g(1) = 2$$ and $$r(1) = -8$$, and $$5 \cdot 2 - 8 = 2$$. The two calculations match the whole
numbers line by line: $$452$$ plays $$f$$, $$13$$ plays $$g$$, and the batches $$30$$ and $$4$$ play
$$2x$$ and $$3$$.

One habit saves a lot of slips: give every power its own column, and write a missing power with a
$$0$$. So $$x^4 - 3x + 1$$ goes down as $$x^4 + 0x^3 + 0x^2 - 3x + 1$$, and the terms you take away
then land under the right powers. Step through three divisions below, and $$452 \div 13$$ beside
them.

## Division always works

Each round of long division lowers the degree of what is left by at least $$1$$. So after at most as
many rounds as the degree of $$f$$, what is left has degree below the degree of $$g$$, or is the
zero polynomial, and the division stops. The theorem says this, and adds that the answer can't come
out two different ways.

Degrees and leading coefficients are as in :ref{#degree}: the **leading coefficient** of a nonzero
polynomial is the number in front of its highest power.

:::theorem[Dividing polynomials with remainder]{#polynomial-division}

Let $$f$$ and $$g$$ be polynomials, with $$g$$ not the zero polynomial. Then there are polynomials
$$q$$ and $$r$$ with

```math
f = qg + r
```

where $$r$$ is the zero polynomial or $$\deg r < \deg g$$. There is only one such pair. The
polynomial $$q$$ is the **quotient** and $$r$$ the **remainder** on dividing $$f$$ by $$g$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Division_Theorem_for_Polynomial_Forms_over_Field#Proof_3"}

Write $$m = \deg g$$, and $$b$$ for the leading coefficient of $$g$$, so $$b \ne 0$$. Two facts do
the work.

- **Small $$f$$.** If $$f$$ is the zero polynomial or $$\deg f < m$$, take $$q = 0$$ and $$r = f$$.
  This is the bag with too few sweets for another round.
- **One round.** Suppose $$f$$ has degree $$k \ge m$$ and leading coefficient $$a$$. Then
  $$f_1(x) = f(x) - \frac{a}{b}x^{k - m}g(x)$$ is the zero polynomial or has degree less than $$k$$.
  Indeed, $$\frac{a}{b}x^{k - m}g(x)$$ has degree $$(k - m) + m = k$$ and leading coefficient
  $$\frac{a}{b} \cdot b = a$$ (:ref{#degree-product}), and all its other terms have powers below
  $$k$$. So in $$f_1$$ the $$x^k$$ terms cancel, and every term left has a power below $$k$$.

**There is such a pair.** For each positive integer $$n$$, let $$P(n)$$ be the statement "for every
polynomial $$f$$ that is the zero polynomial or has degree less than $$n$$, there are $$q$$ and
$$r$$ as in the theorem". We prove every $$P(n)$$ by induction (:ref{#basic-proofs/induction}). That
covers every $$f$$, since a nonzero $$f$$ has degree less than $$n = \deg f + 1$$.

**Base case.** For $$P(1)$$, $$f$$ is the zero polynomial or a nonzero constant $$a$$. If
$$m \ge 1$$, this is a small $$f$$. If $$m = 0$$, then $$g$$ is the constant $$b$$, and
$$f = \frac{f}{b} \cdot g + 0$$, where $$\frac{f}{b}$$ is the constant $$\frac{a}{b}$$, or $$0$$
when $$f$$ is zero.

**Inductive step.** Suppose $$P(k)$$ is true, and let $$f$$ be zero or have degree less than
$$k + 1$$. If $$f$$ is zero or $$\deg f < k$$, then $$P(k)$$ gives $$q$$ and $$r$$. If
$$\deg f = k < m$$, $$f$$ is small. That leaves $$\deg f = k \ge m$$, with leading coefficient
$$a$$. One round gives $$f_1$$, which is zero or has degree less than $$k$$, so by $$P(k)$$,
$$f_1 = q_1 g + r$$ with $$r$$ zero or $$\deg r < m$$. Then

```math
\begin{aligned}
f(x) &= f_1(x) + \tfrac{a}{b}x^{k - m}g(x) && \text{by the definition of } f_1 \\
&= q_1(x)g(x) + r(x) + \tfrac{a}{b}x^{k - m}g(x) && \text{by } P(k) \\
&= \left(q_1(x) + \tfrac{a}{b}x^{k - m}\right)g(x) + r(x) && \text{taking out the factor } g(x)
\end{aligned}
```

which is $$f = qg + r$$ with $$q(x) = q_1(x) + \frac{a}{b}x^{k - m}$$. So $$P(k + 1)$$ is true.

**There is only one pair.** Suppose $$f = qg + r$$ and $$f = q'g + r'$$, where $$r$$ and $$r'$$ are
each zero or of degree less than $$m$$. Subtracting one equation from the other,

```math
(q - q')\,g = r' - r
```

Every term of $$r$$ and of $$r'$$ has a power below $$m$$, so $$r' - r$$ is zero or has degree less
than $$m$$. Now suppose $$q - q'$$ were not the zero polynomial. Then by :ref{#degree-product} the
left side would have degree $$\deg(q - q') + m$$, so it would have a nonzero coefficient at a power
of at least $$m$$. On the right side every coefficient at a power of $$m$$ or more is $$0$$. But the
two sides are the same function, so they have the same coefficients (:ref{#coefficients-unique}). So
$$q - q'$$ is the zero polynomial, which makes $$r' - r = 0$$ too: $$q = q'$$ and $$r = r'$$.

:::

The proof is long division written out once and for all: each round in the worked case was one use
of the "one round" fact, with $$\frac{a}{b}x^{k - m}$$ equal to $$2x$$ and then $$3$$. Because the
pair is unique, any way you find a $$q$$ and an $$r$$ with $$f = qg + r$$ and $$r$$ small enough
gives the same answer as long division.

## The remainder theorem

Dividing by $$x - c$$, a polynomial of degree $$1$$, leaves a remainder of degree $$0$$ or the zero
polynomial. Either way the remainder is a constant. Putting $$x = c$$ into
$$f = q \cdot (x - c) + r$$ makes the first part $$0$$, so the remainder is the value $$f(c)$$.

Try it with numbers. Dividing $$x^4 - 3x + 1$$ by $$x - 2$$, the second choice in the long division
steps under [Long division of polynomials](#long-division-of-polynomials), gives the quotient
$$x^3 + 2x^2 + 4x + 5$$ and the remainder $$11$$. And $$f(2) = 16 - 6 + 1 = 11$$, the same number.
We say that $$g$$ **divides** $$f$$ when $$f = qg$$ for some polynomial $$q$$, that is, when the
remainder is $$0$$.

:::corollary[The remainder theorem]{#remainder-theorem}

Let $$f$$ be a polynomial and $$c$$ a number. The remainder on dividing $$f$$ by $$x - c$$ is the
constant $$f(c)$$:

```math
f(x) = q(x)(x - c) + f(c)
```

for a polynomial $$q$$. So $$c$$ is a root of $$f$$ exactly when $$x - c$$ divides $$f$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_remainder_theorem#Using_Euclidean_division"}

By :ref{#polynomial-division} with $$g(x) = x - c$$, which has degree $$1$$, there are $$q$$ and
$$r$$ with $$f(x) = q(x)(x - c) + r(x)$$, where $$r$$ is zero or has degree $$0$$. Either way $$r$$
is a constant, say $$r(x) = r$$ for every $$x$$. Putting in $$x = c$$,

```math
f(c) = q(c)(c - c) + r = q(c) \cdot 0 + r = r
```

For the second part, if $$f(c) = 0$$, then $$f(x) = q(x)(x - c)$$, so $$x - c$$ divides $$f$$. If
$$f(x) = q(x)(x - c)$$ for some $$q$$, then $$f(c) = q(c) \cdot 0 = 0$$, so $$c$$ is a root.

:::

The second part is :ref{#factor-theorem} again, reached a second way: a root $$c$$ gives the factor
$$x - c$$. When $$f$$ is not the zero polynomial, :ref{#degree-product} shows that the other factor
$$q$$ has degree one less than $$f$$.

The theorem also finds remainders without any dividing. The remainder of $$x^{10} + 1$$ on division
by $$x + 1 = x - (-1)$$ is $$(-1)^{10} + 1 = 2$$, with no long division at all. The third choice in
those long division steps divides $$x^3 + 2x^2 - 5x - 6$$ by $$x + 3$$, and the remainder is $$0$$.
The theorem predicts it: at $$x = -3$$ the value is $$-27 + 18 + 15 - 6 = 0$$, so $$-3$$ is a root.

## Rational functions

Dividing whole numbers gives fractions, and dividing polynomials gives fractions of polynomials,
such as $$\frac{x^2 + 1}{x - 1}$$. Each one is a function you can work out at every number where the
bottom isn't $$0$$. They add like fractions, and long division splits each one into a polynomial
plus a part that shrinks far out along the axis.

:::definition[Rational function]{#rational-function}

A **rational function** is a function $$x \mapsto \frac{f(x)}{g(x)}$$, where $$f$$ and $$g$$ are
polynomials and $$g$$ is not the zero polynomial. Its domain is the set of numbers $$x$$ with
$$g(x) \ne 0$$.

:::

By :ref{#root-bound}, $$g$$ has finitely many roots, so a rational function is defined at every
number except finitely many. For example, $$\frac{4x + 5}{(x + 2)(x - 1)}$$ is defined except at
$$x = -2$$ and $$x = 1$$.

Two rational functions add over a common denominator, in the same way as fractions
(:ref{#numbers/fraction-addition}). Multiply the top and bottom of each by the other's bottom:

```math
\begin{aligned}
\frac{1}{x + 2} + \frac{3}{x - 1} &= \frac{x - 1}{(x + 2)(x - 1)} + \frac{3(x + 2)}{(x + 2)(x - 1)} && \text{a common bottom} \\
&= \frac{x - 1 + 3x + 6}{(x + 2)(x - 1)} && \text{adding the tops} \\
&= \frac{4x + 5}{(x + 2)(x - 1)} && \text{collecting terms}
\end{aligned}
```

for every $$x$$ other than $$-2$$ and $$1$$. Check at $$x = 0$$: the left side is
$$\frac{1}{2} + \frac{3}{-1} = -2.5$$, and the right side is $$\frac{5}{2 \cdot (-1)} = -2.5$$.

Long division turns a rational function into a polynomial plus a **proper** part, one whose top has
lower degree than its bottom. Divide both sides of $$f = qg + r$$ by $$g(x)$$:

```math
\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = q(x) + \frac{r(x)}{g(x)} \qquad \text{whenever } g(x) \ne 0
```

For example, dividing $$x^2 + 1$$ by $$x - 1$$ takes away $$x(x - 1)$$, leaving $$x + 1$$, and then
$$1 \cdot (x - 1)$$, leaving $$2$$. So $$x^2 + 1 = (x + 1)(x - 1) + 2$$, and

```math
\frac{x^2 + 1}{x - 1} = x + 1 + \frac{2}{x - 1}
```

Check at $$x = 3$$: the left side is $$\frac{10}{2} = 5$$, and the right side is
$$4 + \frac{2}{2} = 5$$. Far from $$x = 1$$ the proper part is small: at $$x = 101$$ it is
$$\frac{2}{100} = 0.02$$. So the graph runs close to the line $$y = x + 1$$, a little above it on
the right and a little below it on the left, where $$x - 1$$ is negative.

<RationalNearLine caption="The graph of y = (x² + 1)/(x − 1) on a square grid. Far from the dashed line x = 1 it runs close to the faint line y = x + 1, and at x = 5 it is 2/(5 − 1) = 0.5 above the line: (5, 6.5) against (5, 6)." />

One slip waits here. Dividing $$x^2 - 1$$ by $$x - 1$$ gives the quotient $$x + 1$$ and the
remainder $$0$$, so $$\frac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1} = x + 1$$ at every $$x$$ where the left side is defined.
But the left side is not defined at $$x = 1$$, and $$x + 1$$ is. The two are different functions,
since equal functions have the same domain (:ref{#function-equality}). Cancelling a factor of
$$x - 1$$ gives a new function that is defined at $$x = 1$$, where the old one was not.

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on dividing polynomials, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-dividing-polynomials-whole-numbers}

Divide $$389$$ by $$12$$ with a remainder, writing out each batch you take away. Check your answer.

:::solution

Twelve tens are $$120$$ and twelve hundreds are $$1200$$, so the first batch is tens of twelves.
Since $$12$$ goes into $$38$$ three times, take away $$30 \cdot 12 = 360$$, which leaves $$29$$.
Then $$12$$ goes into $$29$$ twice, so take away $$2 \cdot 12 = 24$$, which leaves $$5$$. Since
$$5 < 12$$ we stop, with $$30 + 2 = 32$$ twelves taken away:

```math
389 = 32 \cdot 12 + 5
```

Check: $$32 \cdot 12 = 384$$, and $$384 + 5 = 389$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-dividing-polynomials-linear}

Divide $$x^2 + 5x + 9$$ by $$x + 2$$. Check your answer at $$x = 0$$, and check the remainder with
:ref{#remainder-theorem}.

:::solution

**Round 1.** To turn $$x$$ into $$x^2$$, multiply by $$x$$. Taking away $$x(x + 2) = x^2 + 2x$$
leaves $$3x + 9$$.

**Round 2.** To turn $$x$$ into $$3x$$, multiply by $$3$$. Taking away $$3(x + 2) = 3x + 6$$ leaves
$$3$$, of degree $$0$$, so we stop.

So $$x^2 + 5x + 9 = (x + 3)(x + 2) + 3$$. At $$x = 0$$ the left side is $$9$$ and the right side is
$$3 \cdot 2 + 3 = 9$$. Dividing by $$x + 2 = x - (-2)$$, the remainder theorem says the remainder is
the value at $$-2$$: $$4 - 10 + 9 = 3$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-dividing-polynomials-quadratic-divisor}

Divide $$3x^3 - 2x^2 + 4x - 1$$ by $$x^2 + 1$$. Check your answer at $$x = 1$$.

:::solution

Write the divisor as $$x^2 + 0x + 1$$, so each power has a place.

**Round 1.** To turn $$x^2$$ into $$3x^3$$, multiply by $$3x$$. Taking away
$$3x(x^2 + 1) = 3x^3 + 3x$$ leaves $$-2x^2 + x - 1$$.

**Round 2.** To turn $$x^2$$ into $$-2x^2$$, multiply by $$-2$$. Taking away
$$-2(x^2 + 1) = -2x^2 - 2$$ leaves $$x + 1$$, of degree $$1 < 2$$, so we stop.

```math
3x^3 - 2x^2 + 4x - 1 = (3x - 2)(x^2 + 1) + (x + 1)
```

At $$x = 1$$ the left side is $$3 - 2 + 4 - 1 = 4$$, and the right side is $$1 \cdot 2 + 2 = 4$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-dividing-polynomials-remainders}

Without dividing, find the remainder of $$x^5 - 2x^3 + 3x + 7$$ on division by $$x - 1$$, and on
division by $$x + 1$$.

:::solution

By :ref{#remainder-theorem}, the remainder on division by $$x - c$$ is the value at $$c$$. For
$$x - 1$$ it is

```math
1 - 2 + 3 + 7 = 9
```

For $$x + 1 = x - (-1)$$ it is the value at $$-1$$. Odd powers of $$-1$$ are $$-1$$, so the value is
$$-1 + 2 - 3 + 7 = 5$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-dividing-polynomials-find-k}

Find the number $$k$$ for which $$x - 3$$ divides $$x^3 - 2x^2 + kx + 6$$. For that $$k$$, factor
the polynomial completely.

:::solution

By :ref{#remainder-theorem}, $$x - 3$$ divides it exactly when its value at $$3$$ is $$0$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
27 - 18 + 3k + 6 &= 0 && \text{putting in } x = 3 \\
15 + 3k &= 0 && \text{adding the numbers} \\
k &= -5 && \text{taking away 15 and dividing by 3}
\end{aligned}
```

Dividing $$x^3 - 2x^2 - 5x + 6$$ by $$x - 3$$ takes away $$x^2(x - 3)$$, then $$x(x - 3)$$, then
$$-2(x - 3)$$, leaving $$0$$. So the quotient is $$x^2 + x - 2$$, which is $$(x + 2)(x - 1)$$, and

```math
x^3 - 2x^2 - 5x + 6 = (x - 3)(x + 2)(x - 1)
```

Check at $$x = 0$$: the left side is $$6$$, and the right side is $$(-3) \cdot 2 \cdot (-1) = 6$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-dividing-polynomials-proper-part}

Write $$\frac{2x^2 + 3x - 1}{x + 2}$$ as a polynomial plus a proper part, and name the line its
graph runs close to far from $$x = -2$$.

:::solution

Taking away $$2x(x + 2) = 2x^2 + 4x$$ leaves $$-x - 1$$, and taking away $$-1 \cdot (x + 2)$$ leaves
$$1$$. So $$2x^2 + 3x - 1 = (2x - 1)(x + 2) + 1$$, and

```math
\frac{2x^2 + 3x - 1}{x + 2} = 2x - 1 + \frac{1}{x + 2} \qquad \text{for } x \ne -2
```

Check at $$x = 0$$: the left side is $$-\frac{1}{2}$$, and the right side is
$$-1 + \frac{1}{2} = -\frac{1}{2}$$. Far from $$x = -2$$ the part $$\frac{1}{x + 2}$$ is small, so
the graph runs close to the line $$y = 2x - 1$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-dividing-polynomials-add}

Write $$\frac{2}{x - 3} - \frac{1}{x + 1}$$ as one rational function, and give its domain. Check
your answer at $$x = 0$$.

:::solution

Over the common bottom $$(x - 3)(x + 1)$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
\frac{2}{x - 3} - \frac{1}{x + 1} &= \frac{2(x + 1) - (x - 3)}{(x - 3)(x + 1)} && \text{a common bottom} \\
&= \frac{x + 5}{(x - 3)(x + 1)} && \text{collecting terms}
\end{aligned}
```

The domain is every number except $$3$$ and $$-1$$, where the bottom is $$0$$. At $$x = 0$$ the left
side is $$-\frac{2}{3} - 1 = -\frac{5}{3}$$, and the right side is
$$\frac{5}{(-3) \cdot 1} = -\frac{5}{3}$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-dividing-polynomials-two-roots}

Let $$f$$ be a polynomial and $$a \ne b$$ two numbers. Prove that the remainder on dividing $$f$$ by
$$(x - a)(x - b)$$ is the polynomial $$px + s$$ whose graph is the line through $$(a, f(a))$$ and
$$(b, f(b))$$. Use this to find the remainder of $$x^4$$ on division by $$(x - 1)(x + 2)$$, and
check it by long division.

:::solution

The divisor has degree $$2$$, so by :ref{#polynomial-division} the remainder is zero or has degree
less than $$2$$. Either way it is $$px + s$$ for some numbers $$p$$ and $$s$$, and

```math
f(x) = q(x)(x - a)(x - b) + px + s
```

Putting in $$x = a$$ and $$x = b$$ makes the first part $$0$$, so $$pa + s = f(a)$$ and
$$pb + s = f(b)$$. That says the line $$y = px + s$$ passes through $$(a, f(a))$$ and $$(b, f(b))$$.
Two points with different first coordinates lie on only one line of this form, so these two
conditions fix $$p$$ and $$s$$.

For $$f(x) = x^4$$ with $$a = 1$$ and $$b = -2$$, the points are $$(1, 1)$$ and $$(-2, 16)$$. The
slope is $$\frac{16 - 1}{-2 - 1} = -5$$, and $$s = 1 - (-5) \cdot 1 = 6$$, so the remainder is
$$-5x + 6$$.

By long division, $$(x - 1)(x + 2) = x^2 + x - 2$$. Taking away $$x^2$$, $$-x$$ and $$3$$ times it
in turn:

```math
\begin{array}{lrrrrr}
 & x^4 & {} + 0x^3 & {} + 0x^2 & {} + 0x & {} + 0 \\
\text{take away } x^2(x^2 + x - 2) & x^4 & {} + x^3 & {} - 2x^2 & & \\
\hline
\text{leaves} & & -x^3 & {} + 2x^2 & {} + 0x & {} + 0 \\
\text{take away } -x(x^2 + x - 2) & & -x^3 & {} - x^2 & {} + 2x & \\
\hline
\text{leaves} & & & 3x^2 & {} - 2x & {} + 0 \\
\text{take away } 3(x^2 + x - 2) & & & 3x^2 & {} + 3x & {} - 6 \\
\hline
\text{leaves} & & & & -5x & {} + 6
\end{array}
```

The remainder is $$-5x + 6$$, as predicted, and the quotient is $$x^2 - x + 3$$.

:::
