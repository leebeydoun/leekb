Imagine standing by a still lake early in the morning, with a mountain on the far shore. The water
shows a second mountain hanging upside down beneath the first. Every rock on the real mountain has a
partner in the water, the same distance below the waterline as the rock is above it. Nothing about
the shape has changed except which way up it is.

<LakeReflection caption="A mountain and its reflection in a still lake. Each point of the reflection is as far below the waterline as its partner on the mountain is above it." />

Some graphs are like that mountain and its reflection put together: flip them in a line and they
land on themselves. [Moving graphs](./moving-graphs.mdx) found what a flip does to the rule of a
function. In this guide we'll use it to find the functions whose graphs are symmetric, and to see
that undoing a function flips its graph in the line $$y = x$$.

## Even and odd functions

Some graphs are their own reflection. The graph of $$x^2$$ is a cup that looks the same in a mirror
standing on the $$y$$-axis, because $$(-3)^2 = 9 = 3^2$$: the value at $$-3$$ equals the value at
$$3$$. The graph of $$x^3$$ is different. There $$(-2)^3 = -8$$ is the negative of $$2^3 = 8$$, so
the value at $$-2$$ is the value at $$2$$ with its sign changed.

For a function $$f$$ with domain $$S$$, write $$-S$$ for the set of the numbers $$-x$$ with $$x$$ in
$$S$$. Saying $$S = -S$$ means that whenever $$x$$ is in the domain, so is $$-x$$. All numbers, and
all numbers except $$0$$, are domains like that.

:::definition[Even and odd functions]{#even-odd-function}

Let $$f$$ be a function whose domain $$S$$ has $$-S = S$$.

- $$f$$ is **even** when $$f(-x) = f(x)$$ for every $$x$$ in $$S$$
- $$f$$ is **odd** when $$f(-x) = -f(x)$$ for every $$x$$ in $$S$$

:::

The absolute value is even, since $$|-x| = |x|$$, and $$x \mapsto \frac{1}{x}$$ on the numbers other
than $$0$$ is odd, since $$\frac{1}{-x} = -\frac{1}{x}$$. A function can also be neither. For
$$f(x) = x^2 + x$$, $$f(1) = 2$$ and $$f(-1) = 0$$, which are neither equal nor negatives of each
other. Two of the most useful examples come in the [Trigonometry](../trigonometry/symmetries.mdx)
chapter, where cosine turns out even and sine odd.

The names come from powers. For a positive integer $$n$$, $$(-x)^n$$ is the product of $$n$$ copies
of $$-x$$, and the minus signs cancel in pairs (:ref{#numbers/two-minus}). So $$(-x)^n = x^n$$ when
$$n$$ is even, and $$(-x)^n = -x^n$$ when $$n$$ is odd, with one minus sign left over. Check with
$$x = 2$$: $$(-2)^4 = 16 = 2^4$$ and $$(-2)^5 = -32 = -2^5$$. So $$x \mapsto x^n$$ is an even
function for even $$n$$ and an odd function for odd $$n$$.

## Symmetric graphs

Flip the graph of an even function in the $$y$$-axis, and the point above $$x$$ lands above $$-x$$
at the same height. That height is $$f(x)$$, which is $$f(-x)$$, so the flipped point is on the
graph already. For an odd function the matching move is the half turn about $$O$$, which changes
both signs: $$(x, f(x))$$ lands on $$(-x, -f(x))$$, which is $$(-x, f(-x))$$.

The half turn about $$O$$ sends each point $$P$$ to $$-P$$, so it is $$H_O(x, y) = (-x, -y)$$
(:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/negative-half-turn}). It is also the flip in the $$x$$-axis
followed by the flip in the $$y$$-axis, since those send $$(x, y)$$ to $$(x, -y)$$ and then to
$$(-x, -y)$$. A symmetry of a shape is an isometry that sends the shape onto itself
(:ref{#isometries/symmetry}), and both flips and the half turn are isometries
(:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/formula-isometries}).

:::proposition[Symmetry of even and odd graphs]{#even-odd-graphs}

Let $$f$$ be a function whose domain $$S$$ has $$-S = S$$.

1. $$f$$ is even exactly when the flip in the $$y$$-axis is a symmetry of its graph
2. $$f$$ is odd exactly when the half turn about $$O$$ is a symmetry of its graph

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Even_and_odd_functions#Basic_properties"}

Two functions with the same graph are the same function. The domain is the set of first coordinates
of points on the graph, and the value at $$x$$ is the second coordinate of the one point of the
graph above $$x$$. So two graphs are equal exactly when the functions have the same domain and the
same value at every point (:ref{#function-equality}).

**Part 1.** By part 5 of :ref{#function-graph-moves}, the flip in the $$y$$-axis sends the graph of
$$f$$ onto the graph of $$g(x) = f(-x)$$. Its domain is the numbers $$x$$ with $$-x$$ in $$S$$,
which is $$-S = S$$. So the flip is a symmetry exactly when the graph of $$g$$ is the graph of
$$f$$, that is, when $$f(-x) = f(x)$$ for every $$x$$ in $$S$$. That is what it means for $$f$$ to
be even.

**Part 2.** The half turn is the flip in the $$x$$-axis followed by the flip in the $$y$$-axis. By
part 4 of :ref{#function-graph-moves}, the first flip sends the graph of $$f$$ onto the graph of
$$x \mapsto -f(x)$$. By part 5, the second sends that onto the graph of

```math
g(x) = -f(-x)
```

again with domain $$-S = S$$. So the half turn is a symmetry exactly when $$-f(-x) = f(x)$$ for
every $$x$$ in $$S$$. Multiplying both sides by $$-1$$, that says $$f(-x) = -f(x)$$, so $$f$$ is
odd.

:::

In the figure below, the graph of the even function $$4 - x^2$$ passes through $$(1, 3)$$ and its
mirror image $$(-1, 3)$$. The graph of the odd function $$x^3 - 3x$$ passes through $$(2, 2)$$ and
through $$(-2, -2)$$. The half turn about $$O$$ swaps those two points, and $$O$$ is the midpoint of
the segment joining them.

## Even part and odd part

A function can be neither even nor odd and still be an even function plus an odd function. In fact
every function on a domain with $$-S = S$$ splits that way. For a polynomial you can see the two
pieces straight away. Take

```math
f(x) = x^3 + 2x^2 - 5x + 4
```

The terms with even powers, $$2x^2 + 4$$, make an even function, since $$4 = 4x^0$$ and $$0$$ is
even. The terms with odd powers, $$x^3 - 5x$$, make an odd function. Their sum is $$f$$.

There is a formula that finds the two pieces without looking at powers. Here
$$f(-x) = -x^3 + 2x^2 + 5x + 4$$. Adding $$f(x)$$ and $$f(-x)$$ cancels the odd powers and doubles
the even ones, so half the sum is $$2x^2 + 4$$. Taking $$f(-x)$$ from $$f(x)$$ cancels the even
powers instead, and half the difference is $$x^3 - 5x$$.

:::proposition[Even part and odd part]{#even-odd-parts}

Let $$f$$ be a function whose domain $$S$$ has $$-S = S$$. Then $$f = e + o$$ for an even function
$$e$$ and an odd function $$o$$ on $$S$$, namely

```math
e(x) = \frac{f(x) + f(-x)}{2} \qquad o(x) = \frac{f(x) - f(-x)}{2}
```

and these are the only even $$e$$ and odd $$o$$ with $$f = e + o$$. They are called the **even
part** and the **odd part** of $$f$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Even_and_odd_functions"}

**They add up to f.** For $$x$$ in $$S$$,

```math
e(x) + o(x) = \frac{f(x) + f(-x) + f(x) - f(-x)}{2} = \frac{2f(x)}{2} = f(x)
```

**e is even and o is odd.** Put $$-x$$ in place of $$x$$, and use $$-(-x) = x$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
e(-x) &= \frac{f(-x) + f(x)}{2} = e(x) && \text{the same two terms, swapped} \\
o(-x) &= \frac{f(-x) - f(x)}{2} = -o(x) && \text{the same two terms, the sign changed}
\end{aligned}
```

**There is only one way.** Suppose also $$f = e_2 + o_2$$ with $$e_2$$ even and $$o_2$$ odd. Then
$$e + o = e_2 + o_2$$, so the function $$g = e - e_2$$ equals $$o_2 - o$$. Take any $$x$$ in $$S$$.
Using the first form of $$g$$, and then the second,

```math
\begin{aligned}
g(-x) &= e(-x) - e_2(-x) = e(x) - e_2(x) = g(x) && e \text{ and } e_2 \text{ are even} \\
g(-x) &= o_2(-x) - o(-x) = -o_2(x) + o(x) = -g(x) && o \text{ and } o_2 \text{ are odd}
\end{aligned}
```

So $$g(x) = -g(x)$$, which gives $$2g(x) = 0$$ and $$g(x) = 0$$. That gives $$e_2 = e$$, and then
$$o_2 = f - e_2 = f - e = o$$.

:::

Check the formula with the polynomial at $$x = 1$$. There $$f(1) = 1 + 2 - 5 + 4 = 2$$ and
$$f(-1) = -1 + 2 + 5 + 4 = 10$$. So $$e(1) = \frac{2 + 10}{2} = 6$$ and
$$o(1) = \frac{2 - 10}{2} = -4$$, which match $$2 \cdot 1^2 + 4 = 6$$ and $$1^3 - 5 = -4$$.

The formula also works where there are no powers to sort. Take

```math
f(x) = \frac{(x + 1)^2}{x^2 + 1}
```

defined for every $$x$$, since $$x^2 + 1 \ge 1$$. Multiplying out the top gives
$$x^2 + 2x + 1 = (x^2 + 1) + 2x$$, so $$f(-x) = \frac{(x^2 + 1) - 2x}{x^2 + 1}$$. The formula gives
the even part $$e(x) = 1$$ and the odd part $$o(x) = \frac{2x}{x^2 + 1}$$. Check at $$x = 1$$:
$$f(1) = \frac{4}{2} = 2$$, and $$e(1) + o(1) = 1 + \frac{2}{2} = 2$$.

## Adding and multiplying even and odd functions

Even and odd functions combine like the signs $$+$$ and $$-$$ under multiplication. An even function
times an even function is even, odd times odd is even, like two minus signs, and even times odd is
odd. Sums keep the type: even plus even is even, odd plus odd is odd. Sums and products of functions
are worked out point by point (:ref{#function-operations}).

:::proposition[Combining even and odd functions]{#even-odd-arithmetic}

Let $$f$$ and $$g$$ be functions on a domain $$S$$ with $$-S = S$$.

1. If $$f$$ and $$g$$ are both even, then $$f + g$$ and $$fg$$ are even
2. If $$f$$ and $$g$$ are both odd, then $$f + g$$ is odd and $$fg$$ is even
3. If $$f$$ is even and $$g$$ is odd, then $$fg$$ is odd

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Even_and_odd_functions#Addition_and_subtraction"}

Take any $$x$$ in $$S$$.

**Part 1.** Using $$f(-x) = f(x)$$ and $$g(-x) = g(x)$$,
$$(f + g)(-x) = f(-x) + g(-x) = f(x) + g(x) = (f + g)(x)$$, and in the same way
$$(fg)(-x) = f(-x)g(-x) = f(x)g(x) = (fg)(x)$$.

**Part 2.** Using $$f(-x) = -f(x)$$ and $$g(-x) = -g(x)$$,

```math
\begin{aligned}
(f + g)(-x) &= -f(x) - g(x) = -(f + g)(x) && \text{take out the minus sign} \\
(fg)(-x) &= \big(-f(x)\big)\big(-g(x)\big) = f(x)g(x) = (fg)(x) && \text{two minus signs cancel}
\end{aligned}
```

**Part 3.** Using $$f(-x) = f(x)$$ and $$g(-x) = -g(x)$$,

```math
(fg)(-x) = f(x)\big(-g(x)\big) = -f(x)g(x) = -(fg)(x)
```

:::

So $$x \mapsto x\lvert x \rvert$$ is odd, as odd times even, and $$x \mapsto (x^3 - x)(x^5 + x)$$ is
even, as odd times odd. Check the first at $$x = 3$$: the value there is $$3 \cdot 3 = 9$$, and at
$$-3$$ it is $$(-3) \cdot 3 = -9$$. An even function plus an odd one can be neither, as $$x^2 + x$$
showed.

## The graph of an inverse

An inverse function swaps inputs and outputs. If $$f$$ sends $$1$$ to $$3$$, then $$f^{-1}$$ sends
$$3$$ back to $$1$$ (:ref{#inverse-function}). So the point $$(1, 3)$$ on the graph of $$f$$ becomes
the point $$(3, 1)$$ on the graph of $$f^{-1}$$, and swapping the two coordinates of a point is the
flip in the line $$y = x$$ (:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/diagonal-reflection}).

Take $$f(x) = 2x + 1$$, which is one to one, with inverse $$f^{-1}(y) = \frac{y - 1}{2}$$: to undo
"double, then add $$1$$", take away $$1$$ and then halve. Here $$f(1) = 3$$ and
$$f^{-1}(3) = \frac{3 - 1}{2} = 1$$, and also $$f(-2) = -3$$ and $$f^{-1}(-3) = -2$$. Each point of
one graph, mirrored in $$y = x$$, is a point of the other.

To draw $$f^{-1}$$ on the same axes as $$f$$, we use the letter $$x$$ for its input too, and write
its graph as the points $$(x, f^{-1}(x))$$. The letter makes no difference to the function.

:::proposition[The graph of an inverse]{#inverse-graph}

Let $$f$$ be a one-to-one function (:ref{#one-to-one}). The flip in the line $$y = x$$ sends the
graph of $$f$$ onto the graph of $$f^{-1}$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_function#Graph_of_the_inverse"}

Let $$S$$ be the domain of $$f$$ and $$R$$ its range, so $$f^{-1}$$ has domain $$R$$. As in the
proof of :ref{#function-graph-moves}, the graph of $$f$$ is the graph of $$E(x, y)$$, the statement
"$$x$$ is in $$S$$ and $$y = f(x)$$".

By part 3 of :ref{#flip-graph}, the flip in $$y = x$$ sends that graph onto the graph of
$$E(y, x)$$, with the letters swapped: "$$y$$ is in $$S$$ and $$x = f(y)$$". So a point $$(u, v)$$
is on the flipped graph exactly when $$v$$ is in $$S$$ and $$f(v) = u$$.

Now compare with the graph of $$f^{-1}$$. If $$v$$ is in $$S$$ and $$f(v) = u$$, then $$u$$ is a
value of $$f$$, so $$u$$ is in $$R$$, and $$v$$ is the one number of $$S$$ that $$f$$ sends to
$$u$$. That says $$v = f^{-1}(u)$$. Going back, if $$u$$ is in $$R$$ and $$v = f^{-1}(u)$$, then
$$v$$ is in $$S$$ and $$f(v) = u$$ by :ref{#inverse-undoes}. So the flipped graph is the set of
points $$(u, f^{-1}(u))$$ with $$u$$ in $$R$$, which is the graph of $$f^{-1}$$.

:::

Squaring is not one to one on all numbers, since $$2^2 = (-2)^2$$, but it is on the numbers
$$x \ge 0$$, where its inverse is the square root. So the graph of $$\sqrt{x}$$ is the right half of
the cup $$y = x^2$$, mirrored in $$y = x$$. The point $$(2, 4)$$ on the cup becomes $$(4, 2)$$, and
$$\sqrt4 = 2$$. The guide [Logarithms](./logarithms.mdx) draws the graph of a logarithm the same
way.

<InverseMirror caption="First, y = 2x + 1 and its inverse y = (x − 1)/2, mirror images in the dashed line y = x, with (1, 3) and (3, 1) marked. Second, y = x² for x ≥ 0 and its inverse y = √x, with (2, 4) and (4, 2) marked." />

The $$-1$$ in $$f^{-1}$$ is not a power. For $$f(x) = 2x + 1$$, $$f^{-1}(1) = \frac{1 - 1}{2} = 0$$,
but $$\frac{1}{f(1)} = \frac{1}{3}$$. The inverse undoes $$f$$, and $$\frac{1}{f(x)}$$ is a
different function altogether.

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on symmetric graphs and inverses, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-symmetric-graphs-even-odd}

Decide whether each function is even, odd or neither, and give a reason.

1. $$x \mapsto x^4 - 3x^2 + 1$$
2. $$x \mapsto x^5 + 2x$$
3. $$x \mapsto x^3 + 1$$
4. $$x \mapsto \dfrac{x}{x^2 + 4}$$
5. $$x \mapsto |x|(x^2 - 1)$$

:::solution

Use :ref{#even-odd-function}, and :ref{#even-odd-arithmetic} for sums and products.

1. Even: only even powers appear, and $$1 = x^0$$, so $$f(-x) = f(x)$$.
2. Odd: $$(-x)^5 + 2(-x) = -x^5 - 2x = -(x^5 + 2x)$$.
3. Neither: $$f(1) = 2$$ and $$f(-1) = 0$$, which are neither equal nor negatives of each other.
4. Odd: the top is odd and the bottom is even and never $$0$$, so
   $$f(-x) = \frac{-x}{x^2 + 4} = -f(x)$$.
5. Even: $$|x|$$ and $$x^2 - 1$$ are both even, so their product is even.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-symmetric-graphs-parts}

Find the even and odd parts of $$f(x) = (x + 2)^3$$, and check that they add up to $$f$$ at
$$x = 1$$.

:::solution

Multiplying out, $$(x + 2)^3 = x^3 + 6x^2 + 12x + 8$$. By :ref{#even-odd-parts}, the even part is
half of

```math
f(x) + f(-x) = (x^3 + 6x^2 + 12x + 8) + (-x^3 + 6x^2 - 12x + 8) = 12x^2 + 16
```

so $$e(x) = 6x^2 + 8$$, the even powers. In the same way the odd part is $$o(x) = x^3 + 12x$$, the
odd powers. At $$x = 1$$, $$e(1) + o(1) = 14 + 13 = 27$$, and $$f(1) = 3^3 = 27$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-symmetric-graphs-zero}

Let $$f$$ be an odd function whose domain contains $$0$$. Prove that $$f(0) = 0$$. Then prove that
the only function on all numbers that is both even and odd is the zero function.

:::solution

**The value at 0.** Since $$f$$ is odd, $$f(-0) = -f(0)$$. But $$-0 = 0$$, so $$f(0) = -f(0)$$.
Adding $$f(0)$$ to both sides gives $$2f(0) = 0$$, so $$f(0) = 0$$. On the graph, the half turn
about $$O$$ sends $$(0, f(0))$$ to $$(0, -f(0))$$, and both are the one point of the graph above
$$0$$.

**Both even and odd.** If $$g$$ is both, then for every $$x$$, $$g(x) = g(-x) = -g(x)$$, so
$$2g(x) = 0$$ and $$g(x) = 0$$. This is the step that proved uniqueness in :ref{#even-odd-parts}.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-symmetric-graphs-inverse}

The function $$f(x) = 3x - 6$$ is one to one. Find its inverse, and check with two points that each
graph is the mirror image of the other in the line $$y = x$$.

:::solution

Solve $$y = 3x - 6$$ for $$x$$: adding $$6$$ gives $$y + 6 = 3x$$, and dividing by $$3$$ gives
$$x = \frac{y + 6}{3}$$. So $$f^{-1}(x) = \frac{x + 6}{3}$$, written with the letter $$x$$ for its
input. Check: $$f(f^{-1}(x)) = 3 \cdot \frac{x + 6}{3} - 6 = x$$.

The points $$(2, 0)$$ and $$(0, -6)$$ are on the graph of $$f$$, since $$f(2) = 0$$ and
$$f(0) = -6$$. Their mirror images in $$y = x$$ are $$(0, 2)$$ and $$(-6, 0)$$, and indeed
$$f^{-1}(0) = \frac{6}{3} = 2$$ and $$f^{-1}(-6) = 0$$, as :ref{#inverse-graph} says.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-symmetric-graphs-composite}

Let $$f$$ and $$g$$ be functions on all numbers. Prove that if $$f$$ is even, then $$g \circ f$$ is
even, and that if $$f$$ and $$g$$ are both odd, then $$g \circ f$$ is odd. Check the second with
$$f(x) = x^3$$ and $$g(x) = 2x + x^3$$ at $$x = 1$$.

:::solution

By :ref{#function-composite}, $$(g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x))$$.

**f even.** $$(g \circ f)(-x) = g(f(-x)) = g(f(x)) = (g \circ f)(x)$$, whatever $$g$$ is.

**Both odd.** Using first that $$f$$ is odd and then that $$g$$ is odd,

```math
(g \circ f)(-x) = g\big(f(-x)\big) = g\big(-f(x)\big) = -g\big(f(x)\big) = -(g \circ f)(x)
```

**Check.** $$(g \circ f)(x) = 2x^3 + x^9$$. At $$x = 1$$ it is $$3$$, and at $$x = -1$$ it is
$$-2 - 1 = -3$$.

:::
