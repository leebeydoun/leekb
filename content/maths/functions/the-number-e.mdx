  Interest paid more and more often, the number e it settles towards, the natural logarithm, and
  rates, doubling times and half-lives worked out from real measurements.
---

Imagine putting £1 into a bank that pays interest of 100% a year. If it pays once, at the end of the
year, you finish with £2. If instead it pays half of that, 50%, every six months, the second payment
earns interest on the first: £1.50 after six months, then £2.25 at the end of the year. Paying a
twelfth of the rate every month gives about £2.61, and paying every day gives about £2.71. The more
often the bank pays, the more you end with, but the totals creep up more and more slowly, and
however often it pays, they never pass £2.72.

The number those totals settle towards is called **e**, and it is about $$2.718$$. Logarithms to
base $$e$$, the ln button on a calculator, are the ones scientists use for growth and decay. In this
guide we'll meet $$e$$ through this bank account, and then use logarithms to read rates, doubling
times and half-lives from real measurements.

## Paying interest more often

Suppose the bank pays $$n$$ times a year. Each payment is $$\frac{1}{n}$$ of 100%, so it adds
$$\frac{1}{n}$$ of whatever is in the account, and that multiplies the amount by
$$1 + \frac{1}{n}$$. After the year's $$n$$ payments, £1 has been multiplied by $$1 + \frac{1}{n}$$
a total of $$n$$ times, so it has become

```math
\left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^n \text{ pounds}
```

With $$n = 4$$, for example, each quarter multiplies by $$1.25$$. The amount goes from £1 to £1.25,
then £1.5625, then £1.953125, and finishes the year at $$1.25^4 = 2.44140625$$ pounds. The table
gives the total for a few more choices of $$n$$, rounded to six decimal places.

| Payments in the year, $$n$$ | Paid             | Total after a year, $$\left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^n$$ |
| --------------------------- | ---------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| $$1$$                       | once             | $$2.000000$$                                           |
| $$2$$                       | every six months | $$2.250000$$                                           |
| $$4$$                       | every quarter    | $$2.441406$$                                           |
| $$12$$                      | every month      | $$2.613035$$                                           |
| $$365$$                     | every day        | $$2.714567$$                                           |
| $$8760$$                    | every hour       | $$2.718127$$                                           |
| $$1{,}000{,}000$$           | a million times  | $$2.718280$$                                           |

Each total is bigger than the one above it, but the gains shrink fast. Going from one payment to two
gains 25p, while going from daily to hourly payments gains less than half a penny. Move the slider
below to try other choices of $$n$$, and watch the staircase of payments close in on a curve.

<CompoundDial />

So the totals keep growing, yet stay below a ceiling, and they settle towards one number. Proving
that needs limits, the idea at the start of a course on analysis, so we take it as given. You can
check the first few cases of "they keep growing" yourself in the exercises.

:::remark[The number e]{#e-exists}

As $$n$$ runs through $$1, 2, 3, \ldots$$, the numbers $$\left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^n$$ increase,
and they settle towards one number, called **e**:

```math
e \approx 2.718281828
```

Settling towards $$e$$ means that every one of the numbers is less than $$e$$, and that by taking
$$n$$ large enough, they come as close to $$e$$ as you like.

This is taken as given.

::see-also{src="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E_(mathematical_constant)#Compound_interest"}

:::

Since the first total is $$2$$ and they all lie below $$e$$, the number $$e$$ is more than $$2$$. In
particular $$e > 1$$, so it can be the base of an exponential and of a logarithm.

## Interest at any rate

Real banks pay less than 100%. At 5% a year, paid once, £1 becomes $$1 + 0.05 = 1.05$$ pounds. Paid
monthly, each payment is $$\frac{0.05}{12}$$, and £1 becomes
$$\left(1 + \frac{0.05}{12}\right)^{12} \approx 1.051162$$ pounds. Paid daily, it becomes
$$\left(1 + \frac{0.05}{365}\right)^{365} \approx 1.051267$$ pounds. These settle towards
$$e^{0.05} \approx 1.051271$$, and the same thing happens at every rate.

:::remark[Interest at any rate]{#the-number-e-rate}

For every number $$r$$, as $$n$$ grows, the numbers $$\left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^n$$ settle
towards $$e^r$$.

This is taken as given.

::see-also{src="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponential_function#Compound_interest"}

:::

Paying more and more often, without end, is called paying interest **continuously**. At a rate
$$r$$, each year then multiplies the money by $$e^r$$. Over $$t$$ whole years, £$$P$$ becomes
$$P(e^r)^t = Pe^{rt}$$ pounds, by the rule for a power of a power in :ref{#exponential-rules}, and
the same formula $$Pe^{rt}$$ is used for any length of time $$t \ge 0$$. For example, £1000 at 5%
paid continuously for 10 years becomes $$1000e^{0.5} \approx 1648.72$$ pounds, while paid once a
year it becomes $$1000 \cdot 1.05^{10} \approx 1628.89$$ pounds.

## The natural logarithm

Since $$e > 1$$, there is a logarithm to base $$e$$, and it has its own name.

:::definition[Natural logarithm]{#natural-log}

For each number $$x > 0$$, the **natural logarithm** of $$x$$ is

```math
\ln x = \log_e x
```

the one number $$t$$ with $$e^t = x$$.

:::

Read $$\ln x$$ aloud as "the natural log of $$x$$". Everything in [Logarithms](./logarithms.mdx)
holds for it, with base $$e$$:

- $$\ln 1 = 0$$ and $$\ln e = 1$$, by :ref{#log-laws}
- $$\ln(e^x) = x$$ for every $$x$$, and $$e^{\ln x} = x$$ for every $$x > 0$$, by :ref{#log-inverse}
- $$\ln(xy) = \ln x + \ln y$$, $$\ln \frac{x}{y} = \ln x - \ln y$$ and $$\ln(x^r) = r\ln x$$, by
  :ref{#log-laws}

On a calculator, the ln button gives $$\ln 2 \approx 0.693147$$, and as a check, the $$e^x$$ button
gives $$e^{0.693147} \approx 2.000000$$. Similarly, $$\ln 10 \approx 2.302585$$. The word "natural"
comes from calculus: of all the graphs $$y = a^x$$, the graph of $$y = e^x$$ is the one that crosses
the $$y$$-axis at a slope of exactly $$1$$, and that makes its formulas the tidiest. We won't need
calculus here.

## Every exponential is a power of e

Since $$\ln 2 \approx 0.693$$, the number $$2$$ is $$e^{0.693}$$. So doubling is the same as
multiplying by $$e^{0.693}$$, and $$2^x$$ is $$(e^{0.693})^x = e^{0.693x}$$. With $$x = 3$$, as a
check, $$e^{3\ln 2} \approx e^{2.0794} \approx 8.000$$, which is $$2^3$$. Every positive base is a
power of $$e$$ in the same way, so every exponential can be written with base $$e$$.

:::proposition[Every exponential is a power of e]{#base-e}

For every number $$a > 0$$ and every number $$x$$,

```math
a^x = e^{x\ln a}
```

So a growth or decay $$f(t) = C \cdot a^{kt}$$ can be written $$f(t) = Ce^{Kt}$$ with
$$K = k\ln a$$. It is growth, with $$a^k > 1$$, exactly when $$K > 0$$, and decay, with $$a^k < 1$$,
exactly when $$K < 0$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponential_function#General_exponential_functions"}

By :ref{#log-inverse}, $$a = e^{\ln a}$$. So

```math
\begin{aligned}
a^x &= \left(e^{\ln a}\right)^x &&\qquad \text{put in } a = e^{\ln a} \\
&= e^{(\ln a)x} &&\qquad \text{a power of a power multiplies the exponents}
\end{aligned}
```

The second step is the rule $$(a^s)^t = a^{st}$$ from :ref{#exponential-rules}. Putting $$kt$$ in
place of $$x$$ gives $$a^{kt} = e^{kt\ln a} = e^{Kt}$$, so $$C \cdot a^{kt} = Ce^{Kt}$$.

For the sign of $$K$$, the power law in :ref{#log-laws} gives $$K = k\ln a = \ln(a^k)$$. The base
$$e$$ is more than $$1$$ and $$\ln 1 = 0$$, so by part 1 of :ref{#log-increasing}, $$\ln(a^k) > 0$$
exactly when $$a^k > 1$$, and $$\ln(a^k) < 0$$ exactly when $$a^k < 1$$.

:::

Take the bacteria from [Exponential functions](./exponential-functions.mdx), 500 of them doubling
every 20 minutes, so $$f(t) = 500 \cdot 2^{t/20}$$ with $$t$$ in minutes. Here $$a = 2$$ and
$$k = \frac{1}{20}$$, so $$K = \frac{\ln 2}{20} \approx 0.034657$$, and $$f(t) = 500e^{0.034657t}$$.
As a check, after an hour, $$500e^{0.034657 \cdot 60} \approx 4000$$, which is $$500 \cdot 2^3$$.
Writing every growth and decay with the one base $$e$$ means each is described by one number $$K$$,
which we'll call its **rate**: the bacteria grow at the rate $$0.034657$$ per minute.

## The rate from two measurements

Out in the world, nobody tells you $$C$$ and $$K$$. You measure the amount at two times, and the
rate has to come from those two numbers. The key is to divide one measurement by the other. The
starting amount $$C$$ appears in both, so it cancels, and what is left is a power of $$e$$ whose
logarithm you can take.

Here is that with numbers. A cup of tea is poured at 90° in a room at 20°, so it starts 70° above
room temperature. Ten minutes later it is at 70°, which is 50° above. Physics says that the
difference between the tea and the room decays exponentially, to a good approximation, so the
difference after $$t$$ minutes is $$D(t) = 70e^{Kt}$$. Dividing the two measurements,

```math
\begin{aligned}
e^{10K} &= \frac{70e^{10K}}{70} = \frac{D(10)}{D(0)} &&\qquad \text{the 70s cancel} \\
&= \frac{50}{70} = \frac{5}{7} &&\qquad \text{the measurements} \\
10K &= \ln\frac{5}{7} &&\qquad \text{take ln of both sides} \\
K &= \frac{1}{10}\ln\frac{5}{7} \approx -0.033647 &&\qquad \text{divide both sides by } 10
\end{aligned}
```

The rate is negative, as it should be for something that is decaying.

:::proposition[The rate from two measurements]{#rate-from-two}

Let $$f(t) = Ce^{Kt}$$ with $$C > 0$$. For any two different times $$t_1$$ and $$t_2$$,

```math
K = \frac{\ln\left(f(t_2)/f(t_1)\right)}{t_2 - t_1}
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponential_growth#Basic_formula"}

Both measurements are positive, since $$C > 0$$ and every power of $$e$$ is positive
(:ref{#exponential-positive}), so their quotient has a logarithm. Then

```math
\begin{aligned}
\frac{f(t_2)}{f(t_1)} &= \frac{Ce^{Kt_2}}{Ce^{Kt_1}} &&\qquad \text{the formula for } f \\
&= \frac{e^{Kt_2}}{e^{Kt_1}} &&\qquad \text{cancel } C \text{, which is not } 0 \\
&= e^{Kt_2 - Kt_1} &&\qquad \text{dividing powers subtracts the exponents} \\
&= e^{K(t_2 - t_1)} &&\qquad \text{take out the common factor } K
\end{aligned}
```

The third step holds because $$e^{u - v} \cdot e^v = e^u$$ by the rule for adding exponents in
:ref{#exponential-rules}, as in the proof of the quotient law in :ref{#log-laws}. Taking $$\ln$$ of
both sides gives $$\ln\left(f(t_2)/f(t_1)\right) = K(t_2 - t_1)$$, by :ref{#log-inverse}. Since
$$t_1 \ne t_2$$, we can divide both sides by $$t_2 - t_1$$.

:::

The cancelling of $$C$$ is the useful part. You don't need to know how much there was at the start,
only how many times bigger one measurement is than the other. With the tea, $$t_1 = 0$$ and
$$t_2 = 10$$ give $$K = \frac{1}{10}\ln\frac{50}{70}$$, the rate we found above.

## Doubling time and half-life

Bacteria that double every 20 minutes double from 500 to 1000 in the first 20 minutes, and from 1000
to 2000 in the next 20. Each step of the same length multiplies the amount by the same factor,
whatever time it starts at (:ref{#exponential-steps}). So the time it takes to double doesn't depend
on when you start counting, and it is a fixed property of the growth. Decay works the same way with
halving.

:::proposition[Doubling time and half-life]{#half-life}

Let $$f(t) = Ce^{Kt}$$ with $$C > 0$$.

1. If $$K > 0$$, then $$f\left(t + \frac{\ln 2}{K}\right) = 2f(t)$$ for every $$t$$. The number
   $$\frac{\ln 2}{K}$$ is the **doubling time**.
2. If $$K < 0$$, then $$f\left(t + \frac{\ln 2}{-K}\right) = \frac{1}{2}f(t)$$ for every $$t$$. The
   number $$\frac{\ln 2}{-K}$$ is the **half-life**.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponential_decay#Half-life"}

By :ref{#exponential-steps} with the base $$e$$, $$f(t + s) = f(t)e^{Ks}$$ for every $$t$$ and
$$s$$.

1. With $$s = \frac{\ln 2}{K}$$, $$Ks = \ln 2$$, and $$e^{\ln 2} = 2$$ by :ref{#log-inverse}. So
   $$f(t + s) = 2f(t)$$.
2. With $$s = \frac{\ln 2}{-K}$$, $$Ks = -\ln 2$$. By :ref{#exponential-positive},
   $$e^{-\ln 2} = \frac{1}{e^{\ln 2}} = \frac{1}{2}$$. So $$f(t + s) = \frac{1}{2}f(t)$$.

:::

Turned round, a doubling time $$T$$ gives the rate $$K = \frac{\ln 2}{T}$$, and a half-life $$T$$
gives $$K = -\frac{\ln 2}{T}$$. As a check, the bacteria have $$K = \frac{\ln 2}{20}$$, and their
doubling time is $$\frac{\ln 2}{K} = 20$$ minutes. The tea's rate $$K \approx -0.033647$$ gives a
half-life of $$\frac{0.693147}{0.033647} \approx 20.6$$ minutes, for its distance above room
temperature.

## Three problems from real measurements

Each problem below follows the same plan. Find the rate $$K$$, from two measurements or from a
half-life, then set the formula equal to the amount you're asking about and solve with $$\ln$$.

:::example[When the tea is drinkable]

The tea from above starts 70° above room temperature, with $$K = \frac{1}{10}\ln\frac{5}{7}$$. It is
at 40°, which is 20° above the room, when $$D(t) = 20$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
70e^{Kt} &= 20 \\
e^{Kt} &= \frac{2}{7} &&\qquad \text{divide both sides by } 70 \\
Kt &= \ln\frac{2}{7} &&\qquad \text{take ln of both sides} \\
t &= \frac{\ln(2/7)}{K} &&\qquad \text{divide both sides by } K \text{, which is not } 0 \\
&= \frac{10\ln(2/7)}{\ln(5/7)} \approx \frac{10 \cdot (-1.25276)}{-0.33647} \approx 37.2 &&\qquad \text{put in } K
\end{aligned}
```

So the tea has cooled to 40° after about 37 minutes. As a check,
$$70e^{-0.033647 \cdot 37.2} \approx 20.0$$.

:::

<TeaCooling caption="How far the tea is above room temperature, minute by minute (across) and in degrees (up). The two measurements are the blue dots, and the green dot is the answer: 20° above, after about 37 minutes. Each step of 10° up is drawn half as long as each step of 10 minutes across." />

:::example[How old is the bone]

A living animal keeps a steady share of carbon-14 in its body. When it dies, the carbon-14 decays,
with a half-life of about 5730 years. A bone is found with 30% of its carbon-14 left. By
:ref{#half-life}, the rate is $$K = -\frac{\ln 2}{5730} \approx -0.00012097$$ per year, and the
share left after $$t$$ years is $$e^{Kt}$$. It is 30% when

```math
\begin{aligned}
e^{Kt} &= 0.3 \\
Kt &= \ln 0.3 &&\qquad \text{take ln of both sides} \\
t &= \frac{\ln 0.3}{K} = \frac{5730\ln 0.3}{-\ln 2} &&\qquad \text{divide by } K \text{, and put it in} \\
&\approx \frac{5730 \cdot (-1.20397)}{-0.69315} \approx 9953 &&\qquad \text{calculator}
\end{aligned}
```

So the bone is about 10,000 years old. That fits the halvings: 30% lies between 50%, after one
half-life of 5730 years, and 25%, after two half-lives, 11,460 years. As a check,
$$2^{-9953/5730} \approx 0.300$$.

:::

<CarbonHalving caption="The share of carbon-14 left in a bone, against thousands of years since the animal died. It halves every 5730 years (orange dots), and the green dot marks 30% left, at about 9950 years. Each step of 10% up is drawn a little longer than each step of 1000 years across." />

:::example[A growing town]

A town had 36,000 people at a census in 2011 and 41,400 at the next census, in 2021. Suppose it
keeps growing exponentially, and measure $$t$$ in years after 2011. By :ref{#rate-from-two}, with
$$t_1 = 0$$ and $$t_2 = 10$$,

```math
K = \frac{\ln(41400/36000)}{10} = \frac{\ln 1.15}{10} \approx 0.013976 \text{ per year}
```

The population is $$36000e^{Kt}$$, and it reaches 50,000 when

```math
\begin{aligned}
36000e^{Kt} &= 50000 \\
e^{Kt} &= \frac{50000}{36000} \approx 1.38889 &&\qquad \text{divide both sides by } 36000 \\
Kt &= \ln 1.38889 \approx 0.32850 &&\qquad \text{take ln of both sides} \\
t &\approx \frac{0.32850}{0.013976} \approx 23.5 &&\qquad \text{divide both sides by } K
\end{aligned}
```

So the town passes 50,000 people about 23.5 years after 2011, during 2034. As a check,
$$36000e^{0.013976 \cdot 23.5} \approx 49{,}997$$. Its doubling time is
$$\frac{\ln 2}{K} \approx 49.6$$ years. A real town grows only roughly like an exponential, so these
answers are estimates.

:::

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on e and using logarithms, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-the-number-e-growing}

Work out $$\left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^n$$ to six decimal places for $$n = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6$$, and
check that each number is bigger than the one before.

:::solution

```math
\begin{aligned}
n = 1:&\quad 2^1 = 2.000000 \\
n = 2:&\quad 1.5^2 = 2.250000 \\
n = 3:&\quad \left(\tfrac{4}{3}\right)^3 = \tfrac{64}{27} \approx 2.370370 \\
n = 4:&\quad 1.25^4 \approx 2.441406 \\
n = 5:&\quad 1.2^5 = 2.488320 \\
n = 6:&\quad \left(\tfrac{7}{6}\right)^6 \approx 2.521626
\end{aligned}
```

Each is bigger than the one before, as :ref{#e-exists} says, and all are below
$$e \approx 2.718282$$. A handful of cases is a check, not a proof.

::::exercise{#ex-the-number-e-ln-values}

Work out each one without a calculator.

1. $$\ln(e^3)$$
2. $$\ln \frac{1}{e}$$
3. $$e^{\ln 5}$$
4. $$\ln \sqrt{e}$$
5. $$\ln(e^2 \cdot e^5)$$

:::solution

1. $$\ln(e^3) = 3$$, by :ref{#log-inverse}.
2. $$\frac{1}{e} = e^{-1}$$, so $$\ln \frac{1}{e} = -1$$.
3. $$e^{\ln 5} = 5$$, by :ref{#log-inverse}.
4. $$\sqrt{e} = e^{1/2}$$, so $$\ln \sqrt{e} = \frac{1}{2}$$.
5. By the product law, $$\ln(e^2 \cdot e^5) = \ln(e^2) + \ln(e^5) = 2 + 5 = 7$$. Or, since
   $$e^2 \cdot e^5 = e^7$$, it is $$\ln(e^7) = 7$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-the-number-e-continuous}

£500 is saved at 4% a year for 6 years. Find how much there is at the end if the interest is paid
continuously, and how much there is if it is paid once a year.

:::solution

Paid continuously, £500 becomes $$500e^{0.04 \cdot 6} = 500e^{0.24} \approx 635.62$$ pounds. Paid
once a year, each year multiplies the money by $$1.04$$, so it becomes
$$500 \cdot 1.04^6 \approx 632.66$$ pounds. Paying continuously gives about £2.96 more.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-the-number-e-solve}

Solve each equation, giving an exact answer and a decimal, and check it.

1. $$e^{2x} = 9$$
2. $$4e^{-0.5t} = 1$$

:::solution

1. Take ln of both sides: $$2x = \ln 9$$, so $$x = \frac{\ln 9}{2}$$. Since
   $$\ln 9 = \ln(3^2) = 2\ln 3$$ by the power law, $$x = \ln 3 \approx 1.0986$$. As a check,
   $$e^{2\ln 3} = (e^{\ln 3})^2 = 3^2 = 9$$.

2. Divide both sides by $$4$$ to get $$e^{-0.5t} = \frac{1}{4}$$, then take ln:
   $$-0.5t = \ln \frac{1}{4} = -\ln 4$$. So $$t = 2\ln 4 \approx 2.7726$$. As a check,
   $$e^{-0.5 \cdot 2\ln 4} = e^{-\ln 4} = \frac{1}{4}$$, and $$4 \cdot \frac{1}{4} = 1$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-the-number-e-convert}

Write each one in the form $$Ce^{Kt}$$, say whether it is growth or decay, and find its doubling
time or half-life.

1. $$3 \cdot 5^t$$
2. $$80 \cdot 0.9^t$$

:::solution

By :ref{#base-e}, $$a^t = e^{t\ln a}$$.

1. $$3 \cdot 5^t = 3e^{t\ln 5}$$, so $$K = \ln 5 \approx 1.6094$$. This is positive, so it is
   growth, with doubling time $$\frac{\ln 2}{\ln 5} \approx 0.4307$$. As a check at $$t = 1$$,
   $$3e^{1.6094} \approx 15 = 3 \cdot 5$$.
2. $$80 \cdot 0.9^t = 80e^{t\ln 0.9}$$, so $$K = \ln 0.9 \approx -0.10536$$. This is negative, so it
   is decay, with half-life $$\frac{\ln 2}{0.10536} \approx 6.579$$. As a check at $$t = 2$$,
   $$80e^{-0.21072} \approx 64.8 = 80 \cdot 0.81$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-the-number-e-medicine}

A medicine has a half-life of 6 hours in the blood, and a patient takes a dose of 200 mg. Find how
much is left after 15 hours, and how long it takes until less than 10 mg is left.

:::solution

By :ref{#half-life}, $$K = -\frac{\ln 2}{6}$$, and the amount after $$t$$ hours is
$$200e^{Kt} = 200 \cdot 2^{-t/6}$$, since $$e^{-t\ln 2/6} = (e^{\ln 2})^{-t/6}$$. After 15 hours,
that is $$200 \cdot 2^{-2.5} \approx 35.36$$ mg.

It is $$10$$ mg when

```math
\begin{aligned}
200e^{Kt} &= 10 \\
e^{Kt} &= \frac{1}{20} &&\qquad \text{divide both sides by } 200 \\
Kt &= -\ln 20 &&\qquad \text{take ln, and } \ln\tfrac{1}{20} = -\ln 20 \\
t &= \frac{6\ln 20}{\ln 2} \approx 25.93 &&\qquad \text{divide by } K = -\tfrac{\ln 2}{6}
\end{aligned}
```

The amount keeps falling, so less than 10 mg is left from about 25.9 hours on. As a check, that is
between four half-lives (24 hours, 12.5 mg) and five (30 hours, 6.25 mg).

:::

::::exercise{#ex-the-number-e-two-counts}

A culture of bacteria has 1200 cells after 2 hours and 4800 after 5 hours. Assuming exponential
growth $$Ce^{Kt}$$, find the rate $$K$$, the doubling time, and the number of cells at the start.

:::solution

By :ref{#rate-from-two}, with $$t_1 = 2$$ and $$t_2 = 5$$,

```math
K = \frac{\ln(4800/1200)}{5 - 2} = \frac{\ln 4}{3} \approx 0.46210 \text{ per hour}
```

By :ref{#half-life}, the doubling time is
$$\frac{\ln 2}{K} = \frac{3\ln 2}{\ln 4} = \frac{3\ln 2}{2\ln 2} = 1.5$$ hours. As a check, from 2
hours to 5 hours is two doubling times, and $$1200 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 = 4800$$.

At the start, $$t = 0$$, so going back 2 hours from the first count,
$$C = 1200e^{-2K} \approx 1200e^{-0.92420} \approx 476$$ cells.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-the-number-e-any-factor}

Let $$f(t) = Ce^{Kt}$$ with $$C > 0$$ and $$K > 0$$, and let $$m > 1$$. Prove that $$f$$ is
multiplied by $$m$$ over every stretch of time of length $$\frac{\ln m}{K}$$, whenever it starts.
Then find how long the town in
[Three problems from real measurements](#three-problems-from-real-measurements) takes to triple.

:::solution

By :ref{#exponential-steps} with base $$e$$, $$f(t + s) = f(t)e^{Ks}$$ for every $$t$$ and $$s$$.
With $$s = \frac{\ln m}{K}$$, $$Ks = \ln m$$, and $$e^{\ln m} = m$$ by :ref{#log-inverse}. So
$$f(t + s) = mf(t)$$, whatever $$t$$ is. With $$m = 2$$ this is the doubling time of
:ref{#half-life}.

The town has $$K \approx 0.013976$$ per year, so it triples every
$$\frac{\ln 3}{K} \approx \frac{1.09861}{0.013976} \approx 78.6$$ years.

:::
