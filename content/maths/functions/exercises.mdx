These exercises use the whole chapter, and most need ideas from more than one guide. They start
gently and get harder towards the end. Try each one before you open its solution, and name the rule
or result behind each step, as the guides do.

Two habits carry most of them. Before you solve anything, write down the domain, so that you know
which answers are allowed. And after you solve it, put your answer back into the function you
started with and check that it fits.

## Functions and their graphs

::::exercise{#ex-functions-ch-inverse}

Let $$f(x) = \frac{2x + 1}{x - 3}$$.

1. Find the domain of $$f$$.
2. Show that $$f$$ is one to one, and find its range.
3. Find a formula for $$f^{-1}$$, and check it at one point.

:::solution

1. The formula makes sense except where $$x - 3 = 0$$, so the domain is every number except $$3$$
   (:ref{#formula-domain}).
2. Take a number $$y$$ and look for the inputs $$x \ne 3$$ with $$f(x) = y$$. Multiplying by
   $$x - 3$$:

   ```math
   \begin{aligned}
   y(x - 3) &= 2x + 1 && \text{multiply both sides by } x - 3 \\
   yx - 2x &= 3y + 1 && \text{gather the } x \text{ terms on the left} \\
   x(y - 2) &= 3y + 1 && \text{factor out } x
   \end{aligned}
   ```

   If $$y = 2$$ the last line says $$0 = 7$$, so $$2$$ is never a value. If $$y \ne 2$$, there is
   exactly one $$x$$, namely $$x = \frac{3y + 1}{y - 2}$$, and it isn't $$3$$, since $$x = 3$$ would
   make the first line read $$0 = 7$$. So each value comes from exactly one input, which makes $$f$$
   one to one (:ref{#one-to-one}), and the range is every number except $$2$$ (:ref{#range}).

3. The working in part 2 gives the one input for each value, so by :ref{#inverse-function}

   ```math
   f^{-1}(y) = \frac{3y + 1}{y - 2} \qquad \text{for } y \ne 2
   ```

   Check: $$f(4) = \frac{9}{1} = 9$$, and $$f^{-1}(9) = \frac{28}{7} = 4$$, as :ref{#inverse-undoes}
   says.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-functions-ch-composite}

Let $$f(x) = x^2$$, $$g(x) = x - 3$$ and $$h(x) = 2x$$, all on all numbers.

1. Find formulas for $$g \circ f$$, $$f \circ g$$ and $$h \circ f \circ g$$.
2. Describe how each of the three graphs is moved from the graph of $$f$$.

:::solution

1. By :ref{#function-composite}, the right-hand function goes first:
   - $$(g \circ f)(x) = g(x^2) = x^2 - 3$$
   - $$(f \circ g)(x) = f(x - 3) = (x - 3)^2$$
   - $$(h \circ f \circ g)(x) = h((x - 3)^2) = 2(x - 3)^2$$
2. By :ref{#function-graph-moves}, $$x^2 - 3$$ is the graph of $$f$$ slid $$3$$ down, while
   $$(x - 3)^2$$ is the graph of $$f$$ slid $$3$$ to the right. Putting $$x - 3$$ in place of $$x$$
   moves the graph right, not left, and the two answers differ, so the order of $$f$$ and $$g$$
   matters. Last, $$2(x - 3)^2$$ is the right-slid graph stretched by $$2$$ away from the
   $$x$$-axis.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-functions-ch-parts}

Let $$f(x) = x^4 - 3x^3 + 2x - 5$$.

1. Find the even part and the odd part of $$f$$.
2. Use part 1 to find $$f(2) + f(-2)$$ without working out $$f(2)$$ or $$f(-2)$$, and then check by
   working them out.

:::solution

1. By :ref{#even-odd-parts}, the even part is $$e(x) = \frac{f(x) + f(-x)}{2}$$. Since
   $$f(-x) = x^4 + 3x^3 - 2x - 5$$, the odd powers cancel in the sum:

   ```math
   e(x) = \frac{2x^4 - 10}{2} = x^4 - 5 \qquad o(x) = \frac{-6x^3 + 4x}{2} = -3x^3 + 2x
   ```

   The even part collects the even powers, and the odd part the odd powers.

2. Since $$e$$ is even and $$o$$ is odd, $$f(2) + f(-2) = e(2) + o(2) + e(2) - o(2) = 2e(2)$$, which
   is $$2(16 - 5) = 22$$. Check: $$f(2) = 16 - 24 + 4 - 5 = -9$$ and
   $$f(-2) = 16 + 24 - 4 - 5 = 31$$, and $$-9 + 31 = 22$$.

:::

## Polynomials

::::exercise{#ex-functions-ch-cubic}

Let $$f(x) = 2x^3 - 3x^2 - 11x + 6$$.

1. Show that $$3$$ is a root of $$f$$, and use division to factor $$f$$ completely.
2. Find every root of $$f$$, and say where the graph of $$f$$ lies above the $$x$$-axis.

:::solution

1. $$f(3) = 54 - 27 - 33 + 6 = 0$$, so by :ref{#remainder-theorem}, $$x - 3$$ divides $$f$$ with
   remainder $$0$$. Long division (:ref{#polynomial-division}) gives the quotient:

   ```math
   2x^3 - 3x^2 - 11x + 6 = (x - 3)(2x^2 + 3x - 2)
   ```

   The quadratic factors as $$(2x - 1)(x + 2)$$: check $$2x^2 + 4x - x - 2 = 2x^2 + 3x - 2$$. So
   $$f(x) = (x - 3)(2x - 1)(x + 2)$$.

2. A product is $$0$$ exactly when a factor is (:ref{#numbers/zero-product}), so the roots are
   $$3$$, $$\frac12$$ and $$-2$$. By :ref{#root-bound} there are no more. Each factor changes sign
   at its own root:

   | $$x$$      | $$x < -2$$ | $$-2 < x < \frac12$$ | $$\frac12 < x < 3$$ | $$x > 3$$ |
   | ---------- | ---------- | -------------------- | ------------------- | --------- |
   | $$x + 2$$  | $$-$$      | $$+$$                | $$+$$               | $$+$$     |
   | $$2x - 1$$ | $$-$$      | $$-$$                | $$+$$               | $$+$$     |
   | $$x - 3$$  | $$-$$      | $$-$$                | $$-$$               | $$+$$     |
   | $$f(x)$$   | $$-$$      | $$+$$                | $$-$$               | $$+$$     |

   So the graph is above the axis for $$-2 < x < \frac12$$ and for $$x > 3$$. Check:
   $$f(0) = 6 > 0$$, and $$0$$ is in the first of those.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-functions-ch-remainders}

Find the numbers $$a$$ and $$b$$ for which $$f(x) = x^3 + ax^2 + bx - 6$$ leaves remainder $$0$$ on
division by $$x - 1$$ and remainder $$-12$$ on division by $$x + 1$$. Then show that $$1$$ is the
only root of $$f$$.

:::solution

By :ref{#remainder-theorem}, the remainders are $$f(1)$$ and $$f(-1)$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
f(1) &= 1 + a + b - 6 = 0 && \text{so } a + b = 5 \\
f(-1) &= -1 + a - b - 6 = -12 && \text{so } a - b = -5
\end{aligned}
```

Adding the two gives $$2a = 0$$, so $$a = 0$$ and $$b = 5$$, and $$f(x) = x^3 + 5x - 6$$. Check:
$$f(1) = 1 + 5 - 6 = 0$$ and $$f(-1) = -1 - 5 - 6 = -12$$.

Since $$1$$ is a root, dividing by $$x - 1$$ gives $$f(x) = (x - 1)(x^2 + x + 6)$$. The quadratic
has discriminant $$1 - 24 = -23 < 0$$, so it has no roots (:ref{#quadratics/root-count}). A product
is $$0$$ exactly when a factor is, so $$1$$ is the only root of $$f$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-functions-ch-hole}

Let $$r(x) = \frac{x^3 - 2x + 1}{x - 1}$$.

1. Give the domain of $$r$$.
2. Show that $$r(x) = x^2 + x - 1$$ for every $$x$$ in the domain.
3. Explain why $$r$$ and $$x \mapsto x^2 + x - 1$$ are still different functions, and describe the
   graph of $$r$$.

:::solution

1. The denominator is $$0$$ exactly at $$x = 1$$, so the domain is every number except $$1$$
   (:ref{#rational-function}).
2. The top is $$0$$ at $$x = 1$$, so by :ref{#factor-theorem} $$x - 1$$ is a factor. Dividing gives
   $$x^3 - 2x + 1 = (x - 1)(x^2 + x - 1)$$: check $$x^3 + x^2 - x - x^2 - x + 1 = x^3 - 2x + 1$$. So
   for $$x \ne 1$$ the factor $$x - 1$$ cancels.
3. Equal functions need the same domain (:ref{#function-equality}), and $$x^2 + x - 1$$ is defined
   at $$1$$ while $$r$$ is not. The graph of $$r$$ is the parabola $$y = x^2 + x - 1$$ with the
   single point $$(1, 1)$$ left out, drawn as an open dot.

:::

## Exponentials and logarithms

::::exercise{#ex-functions-ch-hidden-quadratic}

Solve $$4^x - 3 \cdot 2^x - 4 = 0$$.

:::solution

Write $$u = 2^x$$. Then $$4^x = (2^2)^x = 2^{2x} = (2^x)^2 = u^2$$ by the power-of-a-power rule in
:ref{#exponential-rules}, so the equation is a quadratic in $$u$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
u^2 - 3u - 4 &= 0 \\
(u - 4)(u + 1) &= 0 && \text{factor}
\end{aligned}
```

So $$u = 4$$ or $$u = -1$$. But $$u = 2^x$$ is always positive (:ref{#exponential-positive}), so
$$u = -1$$ gives no solution. That leaves $$2^x = 4 = 2^2$$, and by :ref{#exponential-one-to-one},
$$x = 2$$. Check: $$16 - 12 - 4 = 0$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-functions-ch-log-domain}

Solve $$\log_2 x + \log_2(x - 2) = 3$$.

:::solution

Both logarithms need positive inputs, so any solution has $$x > 0$$ and $$x > 2$$, that is
$$x > 2$$. For those $$x$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
\log_2\big(x(x - 2)\big) &= 3 && \text{product law} \\
x(x - 2) &= 2^3 = 8 && \text{definition of a logarithm} \\
x^2 - 2x - 8 &= 0 \\
(x - 4)(x + 2) &= 0 && \text{factor}
\end{aligned}
```

The first step is the product law of :ref{#log-laws} and the second is :ref{#logarithm}. The
quadratic gives $$x = 4$$ or $$x = -2$$, and only $$4$$ is greater than $$2$$. So $$x = 4$$. Check:
$$\log_2 4 + \log_2 2 = 2 + 1 = 3$$. The other answer, $$-2$$, solves the quadratic but not the
equation, because $$\log_2(-2)$$ isn't defined. That's why the domain came first.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-functions-ch-exp-inverse}

Let $$f(x) = 3 \cdot 2^x - 1$$ on all numbers.

1. Find the range of $$f$$.
2. Find a formula for $$f^{-1}$$, and check it at one point.
3. Describe how the graph of $$f$$ is moved from the graph of $$2^x$$.

:::solution

1. Every $$2^x$$ is positive, and every positive number is $$2^x$$ for some $$x$$
   (:ref{#exponential-positive}, :ref{#log-exists}). So $$3 \cdot 2^x$$ takes every positive value,
   and $$f$$ takes every value greater than $$-1$$. The range is the numbers $$y > -1$$.
2. For $$y > -1$$, solve $$y = 3 \cdot 2^x - 1$$ for $$x$$:

   ```math
   \begin{aligned}
   y + 1 &= 3 \cdot 2^x && \text{add } 1 \\
   \frac{y + 1}{3} &= 2^x && \text{divide by } 3 \\
   x &= \log_2 \frac{y + 1}{3} && \text{definition of a logarithm}
   \end{aligned}
   ```

   So $$f^{-1}(y) = \log_2 \frac{y + 1}{3}$$ for $$y > -1$$ (:ref{#inverse-function}). Check:
   $$f(2) = 12 - 1 = 11$$, and $$f^{-1}(11) = \log_2 4 = 2$$.

3. By :ref{#function-graph-moves}, it is the graph of $$2^x$$ stretched by $$3$$ away from the
   $$x$$-axis and then slid $$1$$ down. The curve now hugs the line $$y = -1$$ on the left instead
   of the $$x$$-axis, which matches the range in part 1.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-functions-ch-log-moves}

1. Show that the graph of $$y = \log_2(4x)$$ is the graph of $$y = \log_2 x$$ slid $$2$$ up. Then
   explain why it is also the graph of $$y = \log_2 x$$ squeezed by $$4$$ towards the $$y$$-axis.
2. The functions $$x \mapsto \ln(x^2)$$ and $$x \mapsto 2\ln x$$ agree wherever both are defined.
   Explain why they are different functions.

:::solution

1. For $$x > 0$$, the product law of :ref{#log-laws} gives
   $$\log_2(4x) = \log_2 4 + \log_2 x = 2 + \log_2 x$$, a slide by $$(0, 2)$$
   (:ref{#function-graph-moves}). Also, $$\log_2(4x) = \log_2\left(\frac{x}{1/4}\right)$$, which is
   the stretch by $$\frac14$$ away from the $$y$$-axis, that is, a squeeze by $$4$$ towards it. For
   a logarithm a squeeze across and a slide up give the same graph, because multiplying the input
   adds to the output.
2. $$\ln(x^2)$$ is defined for every $$x \ne 0$$, since $$x^2 > 0$$ there, while $$2 \ln x$$ is
   defined for $$x > 0$$ only. Equal functions need the same domain (:ref{#function-equality}), so
   they are different. For $$x > 0$$ they agree by the power law. For $$x < 0$$,
   $$\ln(x^2) = 2\ln\lvert x \rvert$$, so the graph of $$\ln(x^2)$$ has a second branch, the mirror
   image of the first in the $$y$$-axis: $$\ln(x^2)$$ is an even function
   (:ref{#even-odd-function}).

:::

::::exercise{#ex-functions-ch-overtake}

Town A has $$20{,}000$$ people and grows by $$3\%$$ a year. Town B has $$30{,}000$$ people and grows
by $$1\%$$ a year. Assuming both keep growing this way, find when A overtakes B.

:::solution

After $$t$$ years, A has $$20000 \cdot 1.03^t$$ people and B has $$30000 \cdot 1.01^t$$
(:ref{#growth-decay}). They are equal when

```math
\begin{aligned}
20000 \cdot 1.03^t &= 30000 \cdot 1.01^t \\
\left(\frac{1.03}{1.01}\right)^t &= 1.5 && \text{divide by } 20000 \cdot 1.01^t \\
t \ln\frac{1.03}{1.01} &= \ln 1.5 && \text{take } \ln \text{ and use the power law} \\
t &= \frac{\ln 1.5}{\ln(1.03/1.01)} \approx \frac{0.4055}{0.01961} \approx 20.7
\end{aligned}
```

The step taking $$\ln$$ is allowed because $$\ln$$ is one to one, and it uses the power law of
:ref{#log-laws}. Since $$\frac{1.03}{1.01} > 1$$, the left side of the second line is increasing in
$$t$$ (:ref{#exponential-rules}), so A is behind before $$20.7$$ years and ahead after. Check with
whole years: after $$20$$ years A has about $$36{,}100$$ and B about $$36{,}600$$; after $$21$$
years A has about $$37{,}200$$ and B about $$37{,}000$$. So A overtakes B during the $$21$$st year.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-functions-ch-irrational-log}

Prove that $$\log_2 3$$ is irrational.

:::solution

First, $$\log_2 3 > 0$$, since $$3 > 1 = 2^0$$ and logarithms to base $$2$$ keep order
(:ref{#log-increasing}). Suppose, to get a contradiction, that $$\log_2 3 = \frac{p}{q}$$ with
positive integers $$p$$ and $$q$$. Then, by :ref{#logarithm},

```math
\begin{aligned}
2^{p/q} &= 3 \\
2^p &= 3^q && \text{raise both sides to the power } q
\end{aligned}
```

using the power-of-a-power rule in :ref{#exponential-rules}. Now $$2^p$$ is even, since $$p \ge 1$$,
and $$3^q$$ is odd, since a product of odd numbers is odd (:ref{#numbers/parity-products}). An even
number can't equal an odd one, so no such $$p$$ and $$q$$ exist, and $$\log_2 3$$ is irrational.

:::
