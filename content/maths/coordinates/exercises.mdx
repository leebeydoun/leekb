These exercises use the whole chapter, and most need ideas from more than one guide. Try each one
before you open its solution, and name the rule or result behind each step, as the guides do.

Most of them come down to one move: write a fact about distance as a fact about numbers. A distance
is the square root of a sum of squares, so it often pays to compare squared distances instead, and
to take a square root only at the end.

## Distances and right angles

::::exercise{#ex-coordinates-right-corner}

A triangle has corners $$A = (-2, 1)$$, $$B = (1, -1)$$ and $$C = (5, 5)$$.

1. Show that it has a right angle, and say at which corner.
2. Find its area.

:::solution

1. By :ref{#distance-formula}, the squared lengths of the sides are

   - $$d(A, B)^2 = 3^2 + (-2)^2 = 13$$
   - $$d(B, C)^2 = 4^2 + 6^2 = 52$$
   - $$d(A, C)^2 = 7^2 + 4^2 = 65$$

   Since $$13 + 52 = 65$$, the squares of $$AB$$ and $$BC$$ add up to the square of $$AC$$. By
   :ref{#pythagoras-converse}, the angle opposite $$AC$$ is a right angle. That is the angle at
   $$B$$.

2. The legs meet at the right angle, so they are $$AB$$ and $$BC$$, with lengths $$\sqrt{13}$$ and
   $$\sqrt{52}$$. By :ref{#distance-and-angles/right-triangle-area} and
   :ref{#real-numbers/root-product}, the area is

   ```math
   \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{13}\,\sqrt{52} = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{676} = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 26 = 13
   ```

No angle was measured. Three squared lengths were enough to find the right angle and the area.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-coordinates-square-or-diamond}

1. Show that $$A = (1, 2)$$, $$B = (4, 3)$$, $$C = (3, 6)$$ and $$D = (0, 5)$$, joined in that
   order, make a square.
2. The points $$E = (0, 0)$$, $$F = (5, 0)$$, $$G = (8, 4)$$ and $$H = (3, 4)$$ also make four sides
   of the same length. Show that they do not make a square.

:::solution

1. By :ref{#distance-formula}, each side has squared length $$10$$:

   - $$d(A, B)^2 = 3^2 + 1^2 = 10$$
   - $$d(B, C)^2 = (-1)^2 + 3^2 = 10$$
   - $$d(C, D)^2 = (-3)^2 + (-1)^2 = 10$$
   - $$d(D, A)^2 = 1^2 + (-3)^2 = 10$$

   The two diagonals have $$d(A, C)^2 = 2^2 + 4^2 = 20$$ and $$d(B, D)^2 = (-4)^2 + 2^2 = 20$$.

   Now look at each corner with the diagonal facing it. In the triangle $$ABC$$, the sides at $$B$$
   give $$10 + 10 = 20$$, the square of $$AC$$, so the angle at $$B$$ is a right angle by
   :ref{#pythagoras-converse}. The triangles $$BCD$$, $$CDA$$ and $$DAB$$ give right angles at
   $$C$$, $$D$$ and $$A$$ in the same way. So each side is perpendicular to the two next to it, and
   the four sides are equal: $$ABCD$$ is a square (:ref{#distance-and-angles/rectangle}).

2. The sides have $$d(E, F)^2 = 25$$, $$d(F, G)^2 = 3^2 + 4^2 = 25$$, $$d(G, H)^2 = 25$$ and
   $$d(H, E)^2 = 3^2 + 4^2 = 25$$, so all four have length $$5$$. But the diagonal $$EG$$ has
   $$d(E, G)^2 = 8^2 + 4^2 = 80$$, while a right angle at $$F$$ would need $$25 + 25 = 50$$ by
   :ref{#distance-and-angles/pythagoras}. So the angle at $$F$$ is not a right angle, and $$EFGH$$
   is a leaning diamond, not a square.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-coordinates-same-distance-new-axes}

The points $$P = (1, 1)$$ and $$Q = (5, 4)$$ are given in the usual axes.

1. Find $$d(P, Q)$$.
2. Turn both axes a quarter turn anticlockwise about the origin, as in
   [Choosing the axes](./coordinates.mdx#choosing-the-axes). Write down the new coordinates of $$P$$
   and $$Q$$, and find the distance from them.
3. Explain with algebra why the two answers always agree, for any two points.

:::solution

1. The differences are $$4$$ and $$3$$, so $$d(P, Q) = \sqrt{16 + 9} = 5$$
   (:ref{#distance-formula}).
2. After the turn, the point that was $$(x, y)$$ has new coordinates $$(y, -x)$$, by
   :ref{#ex-coordinates-turned}. So $$P$$ becomes $$(1, -1)$$ and $$Q$$ becomes $$(4, -5)$$. The
   differences are $$3$$ and $$-4$$, and the distance is $$\sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$$ again.
3. Write $$P = (x_1, y_1)$$ and $$Q = (x_2, y_2)$$. In the new axes the differences are
   $$y_2 - y_1$$ and $$-x_2 - (-x_1) = -(x_2 - x_1)$$. A number and its negative have the same
   square (:ref{#numbers/two-minus}), so the sum of the squares is

   ```math
   (y_2 - y_1)^2 + (x_2 - x_1)^2
   ```

   which is the sum from the old coordinates, in the other order. So the formula gives the same
   distance in both sets of axes. That has to happen, since turning the axes moves no points.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-coordinates-straight-detour}

Let $$P = (-1, 4)$$ and $$M = (5, -4)$$, and let $$Q = (2, k)$$ be a point on the vertical line
$$x = 2$$.

1. Find $$d(P, M)$$, and the length of the route from $$P$$ to $$M$$ through $$Q$$ when $$k = 3$$.
2. For which $$k$$ is the route through $$Q$$ exactly as long as going straight? Use the steps
   $$a, b, s, t$$ and the proof of :ref{#triangle-inequality-plane}.

:::solution

1. By :ref{#distance-formula}, $$d(P, M) = \sqrt{6^2 + (-8)^2} = \sqrt{100} = 10$$. For $$k = 3$$,
   $$d(P, Q) = \sqrt{3^2 + (-1)^2} = \sqrt{10} \approx 3.16$$ and
   $$d(Q, M) = \sqrt{3^2 + (-7)^2} = \sqrt{58} \approx 7.62$$. The route through $$Q$$ is about
   $$10.78$$, longer than $$10$$, as :ref{#triangle-inequality-plane} says it must be.

2. The steps from $$P$$ to $$Q$$ are $$a = 3$$ and $$b = k - 4$$, and from $$Q$$ to $$M$$ they are
   $$s = 3$$ and $$t = -4 - k$$. In the proof of :ref{#triangle-inequality-plane}, the squares of
   the two sides differ only in their middle terms, $$2(as + bt)$$ and
   $$2\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}\,\sqrt{s^2 + t^2}$$. So the route through $$Q$$ is exactly as long as going
   straight when these are equal.

   By the proof of :ref{#product-bound}, the two numbers have equal squares exactly when
   $$at - bs = 0$$. Here

   ```math
   at - bs = 3(-4 - k) - 3(k - 4) = -6k
   ```

   which is $$0$$ exactly when $$k = 0$$. Then $$as + bt = 9 + (-4)(-4) = 25$$, which is not
   negative, so it equals the product of the lengths rather than its negative. For every other $$k$$
   the squares differ, so the route through $$Q$$ is longer. So $$k = 0$$ is the only answer. Check:
   with $$Q = (2, 0)$$, $$d(P, Q) = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$$ and $$d(Q, M) = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$$, which
   add up to $$10$$.

   In the picture, $$Q = (2, 0)$$ is halfway along the straight path from $$P$$ to $$M$$: both steps
   are $$3$$ across and $$4$$ down.

:::

## Circles

::::exercise{#ex-coordinates-equidistant}

Find every point $$(x, y)$$ that is the same distance from $$P = (1, 2)$$ as from $$Q = (5, 4)$$.
Which of these points lies on the $$y$$-axis?

:::solution

Distances are not negative, so they are equal exactly when their squares are
(:ref{#real-numbers/equal-squares}). By :ref{#distance-formula}, that means

```math
(x - 1)^2 + (y - 2)^2 = (x - 5)^2 + (y - 4)^2
```

Multiplying out, both sides have $$x^2 + y^2$$, which cancels. What is left is

```math
-2x + 1 - 4y + 4 = -10x + 25 - 8y + 16
```

so $$8x + 4y = 36$$, that is, $$2x + y = 9$$. The points are exactly the solutions of this linear
equation, and its graph is a straight line (:ref{#linear-equations/line}). By
:ref{#distance-and-angles/perpendicular-bisector-rule}, that line is the perpendicular bisector of
$$PQ$$.

On the $$y$$-axis, $$x = 0$$, so $$y = 9$$ and the point is $$(0, 9)$$. Check: its squared distances
are $$1^2 + 7^2 = 50$$ and $$5^2 + 5^2 = 50$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-coordinates-three-points}

Find the equation of the circle through the three points $$(5, 3)$$, $$(-2, 2)$$ and $$(7, -1)$$,
and give its centre and radius.

:::solution

By :ref{#expanded-circle}, a circle has an equation $$x^2 + y^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0$$. Each point on
it makes the equation true, which gives three linear equations in $$D$$, $$E$$ and $$F$$:

- **$$(5, 3)$$**: $$25 + 9 + 5D + 3E + F = 0$$, so $$5D + 3E + F = -34$$
- **$$(-2, 2)$$**: $$4 + 4 - 2D + 2E + F = 0$$, so $$-2D + 2E + F = -8$$
- **$$(7, -1)$$**: $$49 + 1 + 7D - E + F = 0$$, so $$7D - E + F = -50$$

Take the second equation from the other two to get rid of $$F$$
(:ref{#linear-equations/elimination}):

```math
7D + E = -26 \qquad 9D - 3E = -42
```

The second of these is $$3D - E = -14$$ after dividing by $$3$$. Adding it to $$7D + E = -26$$ gives
$$10D = -40$$, so $$D = -4$$. Then $$E = -26 + 28 = 2$$, and the second equation gives
$$F = -8 + 2D - 2E = -8 - 8 - 4 = -20$$. So the circle is

```math
x^2 + y^2 - 4x + 2y - 20 = 0
```

Here $$c = \frac{16 + 4}{4} + 20 = 25$$, so the centre is $$(2, -1)$$ and the radius is $$5$$
(:ref{#expanded-circle}). Check with :ref{#circle-equation}: the three points are $$(3, 4)$$,
$$(-4, 3)$$ and $$(5, 0)$$ away from the centre, and each gives $$25$$.

The centre is the point where the perpendicular bisectors of the triangle's sides meet
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/circumcentre}).

:::

::::exercise{#ex-coordinates-hypotenuse-diameter}

A right triangle has its corners at $$O = (0, 0)$$, $$A = (a, 0)$$ and $$B = (0, b)$$, with
$$a, b > 0$$.

1. Show that the point $$N = (\frac{a}{2}, \frac{b}{2})$$ is the same distance from all three
   corners.
2. Write down the equation of the circle through the three corners, and multiply it out.
3. Check your answers with $$a = 6$$ and $$b = 8$$.

:::solution

1. By :ref{#distance-formula},

   ```math
   d(N, O)^2 = \frac{a^2}{4} + \frac{b^2}{4}, \qquad
   d(N, A)^2 = \left(-\frac{a}{2}\right)^2 + \frac{b^2}{4}, \qquad
   d(N, B)^2 = \frac{a^2}{4} + \left(-\frac{b}{2}\right)^2
   ```

   All three are $$\frac{a^2 + b^2}{4}$$, so the three distances are equal
   (:ref{#real-numbers/equal-squares}). Each is $$\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$$, half the hypotenuse
   $$AB$$.

2. The circle has centre $$N$$ and radius squared $$\frac{a^2 + b^2}{4}$$, so by
   :ref{#circle-equation} it is

   ```math
   \left(x - \frac{a}{2}\right)^2 + \left(y - \frac{b}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{a^2 + b^2}{4}
   ```

   Multiplying out, the $$\frac{a^2}{4}$$ and $$\frac{b^2}{4}$$ cancel and leave
   $$x^2 + y^2 - ax - by = 0$$. It has no number term, which is why it passes through $$O$$.

3. With $$a = 6$$ and $$b = 8$$, the centre is $$(3, 4)$$, which is $$5$$ from $$(0, 0)$$, from
   $$(6, 0)$$ and from $$(0, 8)$$, since each squared distance is $$9 + 16 = 25$$. The circle is
   $$x^2 + y^2 - 6x - 8y = 0$$, with radius $$5$$, half the hypotenuse $$\sqrt{36 + 64} = 10$$.

So the circle through the corners of a right triangle has the hypotenuse as a diameter. Placing the
axes along the two legs, as in [Choosing the axes](./coordinates.mdx#choosing-the-axes), made the
algebra short.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-coordinates-inside-which}

A disc is bounded by the circle $$x^2 + y^2 + 6x - 4y - 12 = 0$$.

1. Find the centre and radius.
2. Say whether each point is inside the circle, on it or outside it: $$(0, 6)$$, $$(-6, -1)$$,
   $$(2, -1)$$ and $$(1, 1)$$.
3. Explain why putting a point into the left side of the multiplied-out equation, and looking at the
   sign, gives the same answers.

:::solution

1. Here $$D = 6$$, $$E = -4$$ and $$F = -12$$, so $$c = \frac{36 + 16}{4} + 12 = 25$$. By
   :ref{#expanded-circle}, the centre is $$(-3, 2)$$ and the radius is $$5$$.

2. By :ref{#disc-inequality}, compare $$S = (x + 3)^2 + (y - 2)^2$$ with $$25$$:

   - $$(0, 6)$$: $$9 + 16 = 25$$, so it is on the circle
   - $$(-6, -1)$$: $$9 + 9 = 18 < 25$$, so it is inside
   - $$(2, -1)$$: $$25 + 9 = 34 > 25$$, so it is outside
   - $$(1, 1)$$: $$16 + 1 = 17 < 25$$, so it is inside

3. Completing the square in the proof of :ref{#expanded-circle} showed that the left side
   $$x^2 + y^2 + 6x - 4y - 12$$ equals $$S - 25$$ for every point. So it is negative, zero or
   positive exactly when $$S$$ is less than, equal to or more than $$25$$. For the four points the
   left side gives $$0$$, $$-7$$, $$9$$ and $$-8$$, the same answers as before.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-coordinates-road-coverage}

A straight road runs along the line $$y = 1$$ on a map marked in kilometres. A phone mast at
$$(3, 4)$$ reaches $$5$$ km.

1. Which stretch of the road is in range, and how long is it?
2. For which $$k$$ does a road along $$y = k$$ have at least one point in range?

:::solution

1. A point $$(x, 1)$$ of the road is in range exactly when it is in the disc with centre $$(3, 4)$$
   and radius $$5$$, that is, when $$(x - 3)^2 + (1 - 4)^2 \le 25$$ (:ref{#disc-inequality}). That
   says $$(x - 3)^2 \le 16$$.

   The two ends of the stretch solve $$(x - 3)^2 = 16$$, so $$x - 3 = 4$$ or $$x - 3 = -4$$
   (:ref{#real-numbers/square-root-count}), giving $$(-1, 1)$$ and $$(7, 1)$$. In between,
   $$(x - 3)^2 < 16$$ exactly when $$-4 < x - 3 < 4$$ (:ref{#real-numbers/square-inequality}). So
   the road is in range from $$x = -1$$ to $$x = 7$$, a stretch $$8$$ km long.

2. By :ref{#circle-horizontal-line}, the line $$y = k$$ meets the circle when
   $$\lvert k - 4 \rvert \le 5$$, that is, when $$-1 \le k \le 9$$. For other $$k$$ the line misses
   the circle, and every point of it is outside, since $$(x - 3)^2 + (k - 4)^2 \ge (k - 4)^2 > 25$$.
   At $$k = -1$$ or $$k = 9$$, a single point of the road is in range, right on the edge.

:::

## Moving points

::::exercise{#ex-coordinates-zoom-then-slide}

The map $$G$$ first zooms by $$3$$ about the origin, and then slides by $$(-2, 5)$$.

1. Show that $$G$$ sends $$(x, y)$$ to $$(3x - 2, 3y + 5)$$.
2. Find where $$G$$ sends $$P = (1, 1)$$ and $$Q = (2, 3)$$, and compare the distances before and
   after.
3. Explain why $$G$$ multiplies every distance by $$3$$.
4. Show that $$C = (1, -\frac{5}{2})$$ does not move. Then move the origin to $$C$$, keeping the
   directions of the axes, and show that in the new coordinates $$G$$ multiplies both coordinates by
   $$3$$.

:::solution

1. The zoom sends $$(x, y)$$ to $$(3x, 3y)$$, and the slide then adds $$-2$$ and $$5$$, giving
   $$(3x - 2, 3y + 5)$$.

2. $$G(P) = (1, 8)$$ and $$G(Q) = (4, 14)$$. Before, $$d(P, Q)^2 = 1^2 + 2^2 = 5$$. After,
   $$d\big(G(P), G(Q)\big)^2 = 3^2 + 6^2 = 45$$. So the distance goes from $$\sqrt{5}$$ to
   $$\sqrt{45} = 3\sqrt{5}$$ (:ref{#real-numbers/root-product}), three times as long.

3. The zoom multiplies every distance by $$3$$ (:ref{#origin-zoom}), and the slide then keeps every
   distance (:ref{#slide-distance}). So after both, every distance is $$3$$ times what it was.

4. $$G(C) = \left(3 - 2, -\frac{15}{2} + 5\right) = \left(1, -\frac{5}{2}\right) = C$$. With the
   origin at $$C$$, the point $$(x, y)$$ has new coordinates $$X = x - 1$$ and
   $$Y = y + \frac{5}{2}$$, as in [Choosing the axes](./coordinates.mdx#choosing-the-axes). Its
   image $$G(x, y)$$ has new coordinates

   ```math
   (3x - 2) - 1 = 3(x - 1) = 3X, \qquad (3y + 5) + \frac{5}{2} = 3\left(y + \frac{5}{2}\right) = 3Y
   ```

   So, measured from $$C$$, the map $$G$$ is the map $$(X, Y) \mapsto (3X, 3Y)$$: the zoom by $$3$$
   about $$C$$. A zoom followed by a slide is a zoom about a different centre.

:::

## Whole-number right triangles

::::exercise{#ex-coordinates-hypotenuse-65}

Find four right triangles with whole-number sides and hypotenuse $$65$$, no two the same shape. For
each one, find the slope $$t$$ of the line through $$(-1, 0)$$ that gives its point on the unit
circle.

:::solution

**From the formula.** By :ref{#triple-formula}, $$n^2 + m^2 = 65$$ with $$m < n$$ gives a triple
with $$c = 65$$. Two pairs work, since $$65 = 64 + 1 = 49 + 16$$:

- $$m = 1$$, $$n = 8$$: $$64 - 1 = 63$$, $$2 \cdot 1 \cdot 8 = 16$$, so $$63$$, $$16$$, $$65$$
- $$m = 4$$, $$n = 7$$: $$49 - 16 = 33$$, $$2 \cdot 4 \cdot 7 = 56$$, so $$33$$, $$56$$, $$65$$

**From multiples.** Since $$65 = 13 \cdot 5 = 5 \cdot 13$$, multiplying $$3$$, $$4$$, $$5$$ by
$$13$$ gives $$39$$, $$52$$, $$65$$, and multiplying $$5$$, $$12$$, $$13$$ by $$5$$ gives $$25$$,
$$60$$, $$65$$. Multiples of a triple are triples, as in
[Pythagorean triples](./pythagorean-triples.mdx#pythagorean-triples).

**The slopes.** By :ref{#triples-points}, a triple gives the point $$(\frac{a}{65}, \frac{b}{65})$$,
and by part 2 of :ref{#rational-points} its slope is
$$t = \frac{b/65}{a/65 + 1} = \frac{b}{a + 65}$$:

| Triple                 | $$t = \frac{b}{a + 65}$$         |
| ---------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| $$63$$, $$16$$, $$65$$ | $$\frac{16}{128} = \frac{1}{8}$$ |
| $$33$$, $$56$$, $$65$$ | $$\frac{56}{98} = \frac{4}{7}$$  |
| $$39$$, $$52$$, $$65$$ | $$\frac{52}{104} = \frac{1}{2}$$ |
| $$25$$, $$60$$, $$65$$ | $$\frac{60}{90} = \frac{2}{3}$$  |

The four slopes are different, so the four points are different (:ref{#rational-points}), and the
triangles have four different shapes. The last two have the slopes of $$3$$, $$4$$, $$5$$ and $$5$$,
$$12$$, $$13$$, because they are zooms of those triangles and land on the same points. A check of
every $$a$$ from $$1$$ to $$64$$ shows that these four are the only ones, apart from swapping the
two legs.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-coordinates-rational-points-elsewhere}

The circle with centre $$(1, -2)$$ and radius $$2$$ has the equation $$(x - 1)^2 + (y + 2)^2 = 4$$.

1. Let $$(u, v)$$ be a point on the unit circle. Show that $$(1 + 2u, -2 + 2v)$$ is on this circle.
2. Use the slopes $$t = \frac{1}{2}$$ and $$t = \frac{2}{3}$$ to find two rational points on it.
3. Explain why the circle has infinitely many rational points.

:::solution

1. Putting the point into the left side of the equation gives

   ```math
   (2u)^2 + (2v)^2 = 4(u^2 + v^2) = 4 \cdot 1 = 4
   ```

   since $$u^2 + v^2 = 1$$ on the unit circle. So the point is on the circle
   (:ref{#circle-equation}). In the picture, the unit circle is zoomed by $$2$$ about the origin and
   then slid by $$(1, -2)$$.

2. By :ref{#rational-points}, $$t = \frac{1}{2}$$ gives $$(u, v) = (\frac{3}{5}, \frac{4}{5})$$, and
   $$t = \frac{2}{3}$$ gives $$(\frac{5}{13}, \frac{12}{13})$$. So the circle has the points

   ```math
   \left(1 + \frac{6}{5}, -2 + \frac{8}{5}\right) = \left(\frac{11}{5}, -\frac{2}{5}\right), \qquad
   \left(1 + \frac{10}{13}, -2 + \frac{24}{13}\right) = \left(\frac{23}{13}, -\frac{2}{13}\right)
   ```

   Check the first: $$(\frac{6}{5})^2 + (\frac{8}{5})^2 = \frac{36 + 64}{25} = 4$$.

3. Every rational slope $$t$$ gives a rational point $$(u, v)$$ on the unit circle, and different
   slopes give different points (:ref{#rational-points}). Doubling a fraction and adding a whole
   number gives a fraction, so each one gives a rational point $$(1 + 2u, -2 + 2v)$$ on this circle.
   Different points $$(u, v)$$ give different points here, so infinitely many slopes give infinitely
   many rational points.

:::

## Space

::::exercise{#ex-coordinates-dome}

A planetarium dome is part of a sphere of radius $$13$$ m whose centre is $$5$$ m below the middle
of the floor. Put the origin at the middle of the floor, with the $$z$$-axis pointing up, so that
the centre is $$(0, 0, -5)$$ and the floor is the layer $$z = 0$$.

1. The dome meets the floor in a circle. Find its radius.
2. How high is the dome above the middle of the floor, and above the point $$(5, 0, 0)$$?
3. A visitor $$2$$ m tall stands at $$(8, 8, 0)$$. Is the top of their head inside the dome?

:::solution

The sphere has the equation $$x^2 + y^2 + (z + 5)^2 = 169$$, by :ref{#sphere-equation}.

1. On the floor $$z = 0$$, the equation becomes $$x^2 + y^2 + 25 = 169$$, so $$x^2 + y^2 = 144$$. In
   the floor, $$x$$ and $$y$$ are coordinates (:ref{#space-coordinate-rules}), so this is a circle
   with centre $$O$$ and radius $$12$$ m (:ref{#circle-equation}).

2. Above the middle, $$x = y = 0$$, so $$(z + 5)^2 = 169$$. The dome is above the floor, so
   $$z + 5 > 0$$, and $$z + 5 = 13$$: the dome is $$8$$ m high. Above $$(5, 0, 0)$$,
   $$(z + 5)^2 = 169 - 25 = 144$$, so $$z + 5 = 12$$ and the dome is $$7$$ m high.

3. The top of the head is at $$(8, 8, 2)$$. By :ref{#sphere-equation}, compare
   $$8^2 + 8^2 + 7^2 = 177$$ with $$169$$. Since $$177 > 169$$, the point is outside the ball, so
   the visitor's head would be through the dome. Above $$(8, 8, 0)$$ the dome is only
   $$\sqrt{169 - 128} - 5 = \sqrt{41} - 5 \approx 1.40$$ m high, so the visitor can't stand up
   straight there.

:::
