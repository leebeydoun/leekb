  Why a rope knotted 3, 4, 5 makes a square corner, and a formula that finds every such triangle.

Picture a builder marking out the corner of a new shed. She takes a loop of rope knotted into $$12$$
equal lengths and pegs it out as a triangle, with $$3$$ lengths on one side, $$4$$ on the next and
$$5$$ on the last. Pulled tight, the rope makes a square corner between the $$3$$ and the $$4$$,
with no set square in sight. Builders and gardeners still lay out corners this way.

The trick works because $$3^2 + 4^2 = 5^2$$. Three whole numbers that fit the Pythagoras theorem
like this are called a Pythagorean triple. In this guide we'll prove that the rope's corner is
square, and then find every whole-number right triangle by drawing lines through one point of a
circle.

## Why the rope makes a square corner

The Pythagoras theorem starts from a square corner and ends with an equation: if the angle is right,
then $$a^2 + b^2 = c^2$$. The rope runs the other way. It starts from three lengths that fit the
equation and claims a square corner. A statement read backwards like this is called its
**converse**, and a converse needs its own proof: every square has four equal sides, but a shape
with four equal sides can be a leaning diamond.

<BuilderRope caption="A loop of rope with 12 equal lengths, pegged out as 3, 4 and 5 lengths. The corner between the 3 and the 4 is a right angle." />

The proof builds a second triangle that certainly has a right angle: one with legs $$a$$ and $$b$$
along the two axes. The distance formula shows that its third side is $$c$$. The two triangles then
have the same three sides, and three sides fix a triangle, so the right angle carries over.

:::proposition[The converse of Pythagoras]{#pythagoras-converse}

Let $$\triangle PQM$$ be a triangle with $$d(Q, P) = a$$, $$d(Q, M) = b$$ and $$d(P, M) = c$$. If

```math
a^2 + b^2 = c^2
```

then the angle at $$Q$$, opposite the side $$PM$$, is a right angle.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_theorem#Converse"}

**A right triangle with the same legs.** Let $$O = (0, 0)$$, $$A = (a, 0)$$ and $$B = (0, b)$$. The
point $$A$$ lies on the $$x$$-axis and $$B$$ on the $$y$$-axis, and the axes are perpendicular
(:ref{#coordinate-rules}), so the angle $$\angle AOB$$ is a right angle
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/right-angle}). By :ref{#distance-formula}, $$d(O, A) = \sqrt{a^2} = a$$
and $$d(O, B) = \sqrt{b^2} = b$$, since $$a$$ and $$b$$ are positive. The third side is

```math
d(A, B) = \sqrt{(0 - a)^2 + (b - 0)^2} = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2} = \sqrt{c^2} = c
```

where the last step uses $$c > 0$$ (:ref{#real-numbers/square-root}).

**Match the sides.** Pair $$Q$$, $$P$$, $$M$$ with $$O$$, $$A$$, $$B$$. Then
$$d(Q, P) = a = d(O, A)$$, $$d(Q, M) = b = d(O, B)$$ and $$d(P, M) = c = d(A, B)$$. By
:ref{#isometries/sss} there is an isometry $$F$$ with $$F(Q) = O$$, $$F(P) = A$$ and $$F(M) = B$$.

**The angle carries over.** An isometry keeps angles (:ref{#isometries/isometry-angles}), and $$F$$
sends the angle $$\angle PQM$$ to $$\angle AOB$$. So $$\angle PQM$$ is a right angle too.

:::

```plane
point Q 0 0 sw
point P 2.4 -1.8 se
point M 2.4 3.2 n
point O 6 -1.5 sw
point A 9 -1.5 se
point B 6 2.5 nw
ray O A color=muted extend=0.6
ray O B color=muted extend=0.4
polygon Q P M color=blue
polygon O A B color=orange
segment Q P label="a" side=right
segment Q M label="b"
segment P M label="c" side=right
segment O A label="a" side=right
segment O B label="b"
segment A B label="c" side=right
right A O B
caption "A triangle PQM with a² + b² = c², and the right triangle OAB on the axes with the same sides"
```

Together with :ref{#distance-and-angles/pythagoras}, this gives an exact test. The angle opposite
the side $$c$$ is a right angle exactly when $$a^2 + b^2 = c^2$$. You never need to measure the
angle: three lengths are enough.

:::example[Checking a frame]

A carpenter joins two lengths of wood, $$60$$ cm and $$80$$ cm long, at a corner, and measures the
distance between their far ends. Since

```math
60^2 + 80^2 = 3600 + 6400 = 10\,000 = 100^2
```

a distance of $$100$$ cm means the corner is square, by :ref{#pythagoras-converse}.

If the distance comes out at $$101$$ cm instead, the corner is not square. A square corner would
make it $$100$$ cm, by :ref{#distance-and-angles/pythagoras-sides}.

:::

## Pythagorean triples

The rope uses $$3$$, $$4$$ and $$5$$, and
[The Pythagoras theorem](../distance-and-angles/pythagoras.mdx) met $$5$$, $$12$$ and $$13$$.
Doubling the rope's numbers gives $$6$$, $$8$$ and $$10$$, which fit too: $$36 + 64 = 100$$. But
that is the same triangle, zoomed by $$2$$. So the real question is which shapes of right triangle
have whole-number sides.

:::definition[Pythagorean triple]{#pythagorean-triple}

A **Pythagorean triple** is a list of three positive integers $$a$$, $$b$$ and $$c$$ with

```math
a^2 + b^2 = c^2
```

:::

Every triple gives a right triangle with whole-number sides: the triangle with corners $$(0, 0)$$,
$$(a, 0)$$ and $$(0, b)$$ has legs $$a$$ and $$b$$, and its third side is $$c$$, as in the proof of
:ref{#pythagoras-converse}. In the other direction, every right triangle with whole-number sides
gives a triple, by :ref{#distance-and-angles/pythagoras}. Multiplying a triple by a positive integer
$$k$$ always gives another one, since

```math
(ka)^2 + (kb)^2 = k^2(a^2 + b^2) = k^2 c^2 = (kc)^2
```

## Triples as points on a circle

Shrink a right triangle with sides $$a$$, $$b$$ and $$c$$ until its hypotenuse is $$1$$, by dividing
every side by $$c$$. Lay it with its right angle at the origin, one leg along the $$x$$-axis and the
other straight up. Its top corner is then at $$(\frac{a}{c}, \frac{b}{c})$$, at distance $$1$$ from
the origin, so it lies on the unit circle, the circle with centre $$O$$ and radius $$1$$.

For $$3$$, $$4$$, $$5$$ the corner is $$(\frac{3}{5}, \frac{4}{5})$$. For $$6$$, $$8$$, $$10$$ it is
$$(\frac{6}{10}, \frac{8}{10})$$, which is the same point. So zoomed copies of one triangle all land
on one point, and the point is what tells different shapes apart. These points have fractions for
both coordinates.

:::definition[Rational point]{#rational-point}

A point $$(x, y)$$ is a **rational point** if both of its coordinates are rational numbers
(:ref{#numbers/rational}).

:::

The unit circle is the set of points with $$x^2 + y^2 = 1$$, by :ref{#circle-equation} with centre
$$(0, 0)$$ and radius $$1$$.

:::proposition[Triples and points on the circle]{#triples-points}

1. If $$a$$, $$b$$, $$c$$ is a Pythagorean triple, then $$(\frac{a}{c}, \frac{b}{c})$$ is a rational
   point on the unit circle, with both coordinates positive.
2. If $$(x, y)$$ is a rational point on the unit circle with $$x > 0$$ and $$y > 0$$, then there is
   a Pythagorean triple $$a$$, $$b$$, $$c$$ with $$x = \frac{a}{c}$$ and $$y = \frac{b}{c}$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_triple#Rational_points_on_a_unit_circle"}

**Part 1.** The coordinates $$\frac{a}{c}$$ and $$\frac{b}{c}$$ are fractions of positive integers,
so they are positive rational numbers. By :ref{#numbers/fraction-powers} and
:ref{#numbers/fraction-addition},

```math
\left(\frac{a}{c}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{b}{c}\right)^2 = \frac{a^2 + b^2}{c^2} = \frac{c^2}{c^2} = 1
```

so the point is on the unit circle.

**Part 2.** Since $$x$$ and $$y$$ are positive, write $$x = \frac{r}{s}$$ and $$y = \frac{u}{v}$$
with $$r$$, $$s$$, $$u$$ and $$v$$ positive integers (:ref{#numbers/positive-rational}). Put both
over the common denominator $$sv$$:

```math
x = \frac{rv}{sv}, \qquad y = \frac{us}{sv}
```

Let $$a = rv$$, $$b = us$$ and $$c = sv$$, all positive integers. Then $$a = cx$$ and $$b = cy$$, so

```math
a^2 + b^2 = c^2 x^2 + c^2 y^2 = c^2(x^2 + y^2) = c^2
```

since $$x^2 + y^2 = 1$$. So $$a$$, $$b$$, $$c$$ is a Pythagorean triple.

:::

So finding triples is the same job as finding rational points on the unit circle, in the part where
both coordinates are positive.

## Lines through one point of the circle

Rational points on the circle are hard to spot directly, but one is in plain view: $$(-1, 0)$$, at
the left end of the circle. Draw a line through it with a fraction for its slope. The line cuts
through the circle and comes out at one more point, and that point turns out to be rational too.
Turning it round, every rational point joins up with $$(-1, 0)$$ by a line whose slope is a
fraction.

The reason is algebra. Where the line meets the circle comes down to a quadratic equation, and one
of its solutions, $$x = -1$$, is already known. The product of the two solutions is a fraction built
from the slope, so the second solution is a fraction as well.

<RationalPoints caption="Step through slopes t between 0 and 1. The line through (−1, 0) with slope t meets the circle again at a point with fractions for coordinates, and their common denominator gives a Pythagorean triple." />

The line through $$(-1, 0)$$ with slope $$t$$ is the graph of $$y = t(x + 1)$$, which is
$$y = tx + t$$ and so has slope $$t$$ (:ref{#linear-equations/slope}). Putting $$x = -1$$ gives
$$y = 0$$, so it does pass through $$(-1, 0)$$.

:::theorem[Rational points on the circle]{#rational-points}

1. For every real number $$t$$, the line $$y = t(x + 1)$$ meets the unit circle at $$(-1, 0)$$ and
   at exactly one other point,

   ```math
   \left(\frac{1 - t^2}{1 + t^2}, \frac{2t}{1 + t^2}\right)
   ```

2. Every point $$(x, y)$$ of the unit circle other than $$(-1, 0)$$ is this second point for exactly
   one $$t$$, namely $$t = \frac{y}{x + 1}$$.
3. The second point is a rational point exactly when $$t$$ is rational.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangent_half-angle_substitution#Geometry"}

**Part 1.** A point $$(x, y)$$ is on both the line and the circle exactly when $$y = t(x + 1)$$ and
$$x^2 + y^2 = 1$$. Putting the first into the second gives $$x^2 + t^2(x + 1)^2 = 1$$. Multiplying
out and collecting terms,

```math
(1 + t^2)x^2 + 2t^2 x + (t^2 - 1) = 0
```

The number in front of $$x^2$$ is $$1 + t^2$$, which is at least $$1$$, so this is a quadratic
equation. It has the solution $$x = -1$$, since $$(1 + t^2) - 2t^2 + (t^2 - 1) = 0$$. So its
discriminant is not negative (:ref{#quadratics/root-count}), and by :ref{#quadratics/vieta} its
solutions $$x_1$$ and $$x_2$$ have

```math
x_1 x_2 = \frac{t^2 - 1}{1 + t^2}
```

and $$(1 + t^2)x^2 + 2t^2 x + (t^2 - 1) = (1 + t^2)(x - x_1)(x - x_2)$$ for every $$x$$. Putting
$$x = -1$$ makes the left side $$0$$, and $$1 + t^2$$ is not $$0$$, so $$(-1 - x_1)(-1 - x_2) = 0$$,
and one of the solutions, say $$x_1$$, is $$-1$$. Then $$-x_2 = \frac{t^2 - 1}{1 + t^2}$$, so

```math
x_2 = \frac{1 - t^2}{1 + t^2}
```

This is not $$-1$$, since that would need $$1 - t^2 = -1 - t^2$$, that is, $$1 = -1$$. Each solution
$$x$$ gives one point of the line, with $$y = t(x + 1)$$. For $$x = -1$$ that is $$(-1, 0)$$, and
for $$x = x_2$$ it is

```math
y = t\left(\frac{1 - t^2}{1 + t^2} + 1\right) = t \cdot \frac{(1 - t^2) + (1 + t^2)}{1 + t^2} = \frac{2t}{1 + t^2}
```

**Part 2.** Let $$(x, y)$$ be on the unit circle. If $$x = -1$$, then $$y^2 = 1 - 1 = 0$$, so
$$y = 0$$ and the point is $$(-1, 0)$$. So every other point has $$x + 1 \neq 0$$. Let
$$t = \frac{y}{x + 1}$$. Then $$y = t(x + 1)$$, so the point lies on the line with slope $$t$$. It
is on the circle and is not $$(-1, 0)$$, so by part 1 it is that line's second point. No other slope
works: if $$y = s(x + 1)$$ as well, then $$s = \frac{y}{x + 1} = t$$.

**Part 3.** If $$t$$ is rational, so are $$1 - t^2$$, $$1 + t^2$$ and $$2t$$, since sums and
products of fractions are fractions (:ref{#numbers/fraction-addition},
:ref{#numbers/fraction-multiplication}). Dividing by the fraction $$1 + t^2$$, which is not $$0$$,
means multiplying by its inverse (:ref{#numbers/inverse-exists}), so both coordinates of the second
point are rational. If instead the point $$(x, y)$$ is rational, then $$t = \frac{y}{x + 1}$$ is a
fraction divided by a non-zero fraction, so it is rational in the same way.

:::

:::example[The slope two thirds]

Take $$t = \frac{2}{3}$$, so $$t^2 = \frac{4}{9}$$. Then

```math
\frac{1 - t^2}{1 + t^2} = \frac{5/9}{13/9} = \frac{5}{13}, \qquad
\frac{2t}{1 + t^2} = \frac{4/3}{13/9} = \frac{4}{3} \cdot \frac{9}{13} = \frac{12}{13}
```

So the line meets the circle again at $$(\frac{5}{13}, \frac{12}{13})$$. Both coordinates are
positive, so by :ref{#triples-points} the tops and the common bottom give the triple $$5$$, $$12$$,
$$13$$. Check: $$25 + 144 = 169 = 13^2$$.

:::

The slopes from $$0$$ to $$1$$ give the quarter of the circle where both coordinates are positive.
At $$t = 0$$ the line is the $$x$$-axis and meets the circle again at $$(1, 0)$$. As $$t$$ grows the
second point climbs round the circle, and at $$t = 1$$ it reaches $$(0, 1)$$. In between, both
coordinates are positive.

## A formula for every triple

Fractions are awkward to carry around, so write the slope as $$t = \frac{m}{n}$$ and clear them.
Multiplying the top and bottom of each coordinate by $$n^2$$ gives the second point as

```math
\left(\frac{n^2 - m^2}{n^2 + m^2}, \frac{2mn}{n^2 + m^2}\right)
```

The three whole numbers $$n^2 - m^2$$, $$2mn$$ and $$n^2 + m^2$$ are then a triple, and every triple
comes from one of these, up to a zoom.

:::corollary[A formula for triples]{#triple-formula}

1. For positive integers $$m < n$$, the numbers

   ```math
   a = n^2 - m^2, \qquad b = 2mn, \qquad c = n^2 + m^2
   ```

   are a Pythagorean triple.

2. Every Pythagorean triple $$a$$, $$b$$, $$c$$ is a multiple of one of these: there are positive
   integers $$m < n$$ and a positive rational number $$k$$ with

   ```math
   a = k(n^2 - m^2), \qquad b = k \cdot 2mn, \qquad c = k(n^2 + m^2)
   ```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_triple#Generating_a_triple"}

**Part 1.** The three numbers are integers. They are positive: $$m < n$$ gives $$m^2 < n^2$$
(:ref{#real-numbers/power-order}), so $$n^2 - m^2 > 0$$. Multiplying out with
:ref{#numbers/square-formulas},

```math
(n^2 - m^2)^2 + (2mn)^2 = n^4 - 2m^2n^2 + m^4 + 4m^2n^2 = n^4 + 2m^2n^2 + m^4 = (n^2 + m^2)^2
```

**Part 2.** By :ref{#triples-points}, $$(\frac{a}{c}, \frac{b}{c})$$ is a rational point on the unit
circle with positive coordinates, so it is not $$(-1, 0)$$. By :ref{#rational-points} it is the
second point for the slope

```math
t = \frac{b/c}{a/c + 1} = \frac{b}{a + c}
```

which is a positive rational number. It is less than $$1$$: from $$a^2 + b^2 = c^2$$ we get
$$b^2 < c^2$$, so $$b < c$$ (:ref{#real-numbers/power-order-converse}), and so $$b < a + c$$. Write
$$t = \frac{m}{n}$$ with $$m$$ and $$n$$ positive integers (:ref{#numbers/positive-rational}). Then
$$\frac{m}{n} < 1$$ gives $$m < n$$.

By part 1 of :ref{#rational-points}, with the top and bottom multiplied by $$n^2$$,

```math
\frac{a}{c} = \frac{n^2 - m^2}{n^2 + m^2}, \qquad \frac{b}{c} = \frac{2mn}{n^2 + m^2}
```

Let $$k = \frac{c}{n^2 + m^2}$$, a positive rational number. Then $$c = k(n^2 + m^2)$$, and
multiplying the two equations by $$c$$ gives $$a = k(n^2 - m^2)$$ and $$b = k \cdot 2mn$$.

:::

The table lists the triples that the first few slopes give. Each line is a different shape of right
triangle, and the interactive figure under
[Lines through one point of the circle](#lines-through-one-point-of-the-circle) shows each one as a
point on the circle.

| Slope $$t$$     | $$n^2 - m^2$$ | $$2mn$$ | $$n^2 + m^2$$ | Check               |
| --------------- | ------------- | ------- | ------------- | ------------------- |
| $$\frac{1}{2}$$ | 3             | 4       | 5             | $$9 + 16 = 25$$     |
| $$\frac{2}{3}$$ | 5             | 12      | 13            | $$25 + 144 = 169$$  |
| $$\frac{1}{4}$$ | 15            | 8       | 17            | $$225 + 64 = 289$$  |
| $$\frac{3}{4}$$ | 7             | 24      | 25            | $$49 + 576 = 625$$  |
| $$\frac{2}{5}$$ | 21            | 20      | 29            | $$441 + 400 = 841$$ |

The number $$k$$ in part 2 need not be a whole number. The triple $$8$$, $$15$$, $$17$$ has
$$t = \frac{15}{8 + 17} = \frac{15}{25} = \frac{3}{5}$$, and the formula with $$m = 3$$ and
$$n = 5$$ gives $$16$$, $$30$$, $$34$$. So here $$k = \frac{1}{2}$$: the triple is half of the one
the formula gives.

There are infinitely many slopes between $$0$$ and $$1$$, such as $$\frac{1}{2}$$, $$\frac{1}{3}$$,
$$\frac{1}{4}$$ and so on, and by part 2 of :ref{#rational-points} different slopes give different
points. A triangle and its zoomed copies give a single point, or two if you also swap which leg is
$$a$$. So the infinitely many points give infinitely many different shapes of whole-number right
triangle.

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on whole-number right triangles, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-pythagorean-triples-which-right}

Which of these triangles have a right angle, and where is it?

1. Sides $$9$$, $$40$$ and $$41$$
2. Sides $$10$$, $$24$$ and $$27$$
3. Sides $$1.5$$, $$2$$ and $$2.5$$

:::solution

In a right triangle, the square of the hypotenuse is the sum of the other two squares
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/pythagoras}), so the hypotenuse is the longest side. So only the angle
opposite the longest side can be right. Compare the sum of the two smaller squares with the largest
square, and use :ref{#pythagoras-converse}.

1. $$81 + 1600 = 1681 = 41^2$$. There is a right angle, between the sides $$9$$ and $$40$$.
2. $$100 + 576 = 676$$, but $$27^2 = 729$$. If the angle opposite the side $$27$$ were right,
   :ref{#distance-and-angles/pythagoras} would make these equal, so it is not right. The other two
   angles face shorter sides, so none of the angles is right.
3. $$2.25 + 4 = 6.25 = 2.5^2$$. There is a right angle, between the sides $$1.5$$ and $$2$$. The
   converse works for any lengths, not only whole numbers.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-pythagorean-triples-picture-frame}

A picture frame is $$21$$ cm wide and $$28$$ cm tall. How long should its diagonal be if its corners
are square? The diagonal measures $$34.5$$ cm. What does that tell you?

:::solution

If the corner is square, the diagonal is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs $$21$$ and
$$28$$, so by :ref{#distance-and-angles/pythagoras-sides} it is

```math
\sqrt{21^2 + 28^2} = \sqrt{441 + 784} = \sqrt{1225} = 35
```

A diagonal of $$34.5$$ cm is not $$35$$ cm, so the corner between those sides is not square: the
frame has been pushed out of true. Had it measured $$35$$ cm, :ref{#pythagoras-converse} would show
the corner is square.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-pythagorean-triples-one-third}

Find the second point where the line through $$(-1, 0)$$ with slope $$\frac{1}{3}$$ meets the unit
circle, and the triple it gives. Compare with the triple from the slope $$\frac{1}{2}$$, and with
the formula in :ref{#triple-formula} for $$m = 1$$ and $$n = 3$$.

:::solution

With $$t = \frac{1}{3}$$, $$t^2 = \frac{1}{9}$$, so by :ref{#rational-points}

```math
\frac{1 - t^2}{1 + t^2} = \frac{8/9}{10/9} = \frac{4}{5}, \qquad
\frac{2t}{1 + t^2} = \frac{2/3}{10/9} = \frac{2}{3} \cdot \frac{9}{10} = \frac{3}{5}
```

The point is $$(\frac{4}{5}, \frac{3}{5})$$, giving the triple $$4$$, $$3$$, $$5$$
(:ref{#triples-points}). The slope $$\frac{1}{2}$$ gives $$(\frac{3}{5}, \frac{4}{5})$$ and the
triple $$3$$, $$4$$, $$5$$. It is the same triangle with its legs swapped: the point has been
reflected in the line $$y = x$$.

The formula gives $$9 - 1 = 8$$, $$2 \cdot 1 \cdot 3 = 6$$ and $$9 + 1 = 10$$. That is
$$2 \cdot 4$$, $$2 \cdot 3$$, $$2 \cdot 5$$, so here the formula gives twice the smallest triple,
and $$k = \frac{1}{2}$$ in part 2 of :ref{#triple-formula}.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-pythagorean-triples-find-slope}

Check that $$20$$, $$21$$, $$29$$ is a Pythagorean triple. Find the slope $$t$$ of the line through
$$(-1, 0)$$ that gives it, and the triple the formula in :ref{#triple-formula} gives for that slope.

:::solution

$$20^2 + 21^2 = 400 + 441 = 841 = 29^2$$, so it is a triple. Its point is
$$(\frac{20}{29}, \frac{21}{29})$$ (:ref{#triples-points}), and by part 2 of :ref{#rational-points}

```math
t = \frac{21/29}{20/29 + 1} = \frac{21/29}{49/29} = \frac{21}{49} = \frac{3}{7}
```

With $$m = 3$$ and $$n = 7$$ the formula gives $$49 - 9 = 40$$, $$2 \cdot 3 \cdot 7 = 42$$ and
$$49 + 9 = 58$$. That is twice $$20$$, $$21$$, $$29$$, as part 2 of :ref{#triple-formula} allows,
with $$k = \frac{1}{2}$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-pythagorean-triples-other-slopes}

Find the second points for the slopes $$t = 2$$ and $$t = -\frac{1}{2}$$. How are they related to
the point $$(\frac{3}{5}, \frac{4}{5})$$ from $$t = \frac{1}{2}$$?

:::solution

For $$t = 2$$, $$t^2 = 4$$, so the point is
$$(\frac{1 - 4}{5}, \frac{4}{5}) = (-\frac{3}{5}, \frac{4}{5})$$ (:ref{#rational-points}). For
$$t = -\frac{1}{2}$$, $$t^2 = \frac{1}{4}$$, so the point is

```math
\left(\frac{3/4}{5/4}, \frac{-1}{5/4}\right) = \left(\frac{3}{5}, -\frac{4}{5}\right)
```

The first is $$(\frac{3}{5}, \frac{4}{5})$$ with the sign of $$x$$ changed, reflected in the
$$y$$-axis. The second has the sign of $$y$$ changed, reflected in the $$x$$-axis. Both are still
rational points, but they are not in the quarter where both coordinates are positive, so they give
no new triple. That is why the slopes between $$0$$ and $$1$$ are enough.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-pythagorean-triples-missing-point}

No slope $$t$$ gives the point $$(-1, 0)$$ itself as the second point. Explain this in two ways:
from the formula in :ref{#rational-points}, and from the line through $$(-1, 0)$$ that has no slope.

:::solution

**From the formula.** The second point has $$x = \frac{1 - t^2}{1 + t^2}$$. For this to be $$-1$$
you would need $$1 - t^2 = -1 - t^2$$, that is, $$1 = -1$$. So $$x$$ is never $$-1$$.

**From the lines.** Every line through $$(-1, 0)$$ except one has a slope, and is $$y = t(x + 1)$$
for some $$t$$. The one without a slope is the vertical line $$x = -1$$. On it, a point of the
circle has $$1 + y^2 = 1$$, so $$y = 0$$: the vertical line touches the circle at $$(-1, 0)$$ and
nowhere else. So it has no second point to give.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-pythagorean-triples-largest}

In the formula of :ref{#triple-formula}, show that $$c$$ is always larger than $$a$$ and than $$b$$.
Explain why this had to happen.

:::solution

$$c - a = (n^2 + m^2) - (n^2 - m^2) = 2m^2$$, which is positive, so $$c > a$$. And

```math
c - b = n^2 + m^2 - 2mn = (n - m)^2
```

by :ref{#numbers/square-formulas}, which is positive since $$m < n$$. So $$c > b$$.

This had to happen because $$c$$ is the hypotenuse. Its square is $$a^2 + b^2$$, which is more than
$$a^2$$ and more than $$b^2$$, and a larger square means a larger positive number
(:ref{#real-numbers/power-order-converse}).

:::
