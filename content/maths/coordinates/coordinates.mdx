title: Coordinates
description: Naming every point of the plane by a pair of numbers, read off two perpendicular axes.

Imagine you've pitched a tent on a hillside and want to tell a friend where it is, on a walking map
you both have. "Near the wood" leaves them searching. But the map is covered by a grid of numbered
lines, so you can say "4 along the bottom, then 3 up the side", and your friend's finger lands on
the same spot as yours. Two numbers, read in an agreed order, pin down one place. Mathematicians
call such numbers coordinates. In this guide we'll set them up on the plane, and see how they change
when someone draws the grid differently.

<GridReference caption="The tent sits where the grid line 4 along the bottom meets the grid line 3 up the side" />

## Two numbers for every point

A map grid works because everyone using it agrees on three things: where the counting starts, which
way the numbers grow, and how long one step is. The plane needs the same agreements. So start at a
point, draw two perpendicular lines through it, and mark each line as a number line. Then any point
is described by where it sits along one line and where it sits along the other.

The [Equations as lines](../linear-equations/lines.mdx) guide used this picture already, going $$x$$
steps across and then $$y$$ steps up. Here we set it up with more care, because the next guides
prove facts about distance from it. The plane, perpendicular lines and the foot of a perpendicular
are as in the [Lines](../distance-and-angles/lines.mdx) guide.

:::definition[Coordinates]{#coordinates}

Choose a point $$O$$, called the **origin**, and two perpendicular lines through it, called the
**axes**. Mark each axis as a number line with $$0$$ at $$O$$, using the unit of length fixed for
distances. One axis, the **$$x$$-axis**, is drawn across the page with its numbers growing to the
right. The other, the **$$y$$-axis**, is drawn up the page with its numbers growing upwards.

Each point $$P$$ then has two numbers:

- **The $$x$$-coordinate**: the number marked at the foot of the perpendicular from $$P$$ to the
  $$x$$-axis
- **The $$y$$-coordinate**: the number marked at the foot of the perpendicular from $$P$$ to the
  $$y$$-axis

They are written as a pair, $$P = (x, y)$$, with the $$x$$-coordinate first. So the $$x$$-coordinate
is also called the **first coordinate**, and the $$y$$-coordinate the **second coordinate**.

:::

Take a point $$P$$ whose perpendicular to the $$x$$-axis lands at $$3$$, and whose perpendicular to
the $$y$$-axis lands at $$2$$. Then $$P = (3, 2)$$: from $$O$$, go $$3$$ to the right and then $$2$$
up. The origin itself is $$O = (0, 0)$$, since both perpendiculars from $$O$$ land at $$O$$.

```plane
point X1 -1.5 0 hidden
point X2 5 0 hidden
point Y1 0 -1.2 hidden
point Y2 0 3.4 hidden
point O 0 0 sw
point P 3 2 ne
point A 3 0 hidden
point B 0 2 hidden
segment X1 X2
segment Y1 Y2
segment P A color=orange dashed
segment P B color=green dashed
right X2 A P
right P B Y2
text 3 -0.45 "3" color=orange
text -0.35 2 "2" color=green
text 5.55 0 "x-axis"
text 0 3.75 "y-axis"
caption "The perpendiculars from P land at 3 on the x-axis and at 2 on the y-axis, so P = (3, 2)"
```

Negative numbers point the other way. A point to the left of the $$y$$-axis has a negative
$$x$$-coordinate, and a point below the $$x$$-axis has a negative $$y$$-coordinate. So $$(-4, -1)$$
is the point $$4$$ to the left of $$O$$ and $$1$$ down.

The definition gives every point a pair of numbers. For coordinates to be any use, the pairs and the
points have to match up one for one, and the grid lines have to behave as they do on a map. These
facts are taken as given.

:::remark[Rules for coordinates]{#coordinate-rules}

Fix an origin and axes as in :ref{#coordinates}.

1. **One pair, one point**: each point has exactly one pair of coordinates, and each pair of real
   numbers $$(a, b)$$ is the pair of exactly one point
2. **Distance along an axis**: the points $$(a, 0)$$ and $$(c, 0)$$ of the $$x$$-axis are at
   distance $$|a - c|$$, as on the number line (:ref{#real-numbers/distance}). The same goes for
   $$(0, a)$$ and $$(0, c)$$ on the $$y$$-axis.
3. **Grid lines**: the points with $$x$$-coordinate $$a$$ make up the line through $$(a, 0)$$
   perpendicular to the $$x$$-axis, called the **vertical line** $$x = a$$. The points with
   $$y$$-coordinate $$b$$ make up the line through $$(0, b)$$ perpendicular to the $$y$$-axis,
   called the **horizontal line** $$y = b$$. Every vertical line is perpendicular to every
   horizontal line.

These are taken as given.

::see-also{src="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinate_system#Two_dimensions"}

:::

Rule 3 is what makes the grid a grid. The vertical line $$x = 3$$ runs straight up and down through
$$3$$ on the $$x$$-axis, and every point on it has $$x$$-coordinate $$3$$, whatever its height. It
is the graph of the equation $$x = 3$$ (:ref{#linear-equations/graph}). The axes are grid lines too:
the $$x$$-axis is the line $$y = 0$$ and the $$y$$-axis is the line $$x = 0$$.

The order of the pair matters. The point $$(2, 3)$$ is $$2$$ across and $$3$$ up, while $$(3, 2)$$
is $$3$$ across and $$2$$ up, and these are different points. By rule 1, two pairs name the same
point exactly when their first coordinates are equal and their second coordinates are equal.

## Quadrants

The two axes cross at $$O$$ and cut the rest of the plane into four pieces, like the bars of a
window dividing it into four panes. Inside each piece the signs of the coordinates never change. In
the top right piece both coordinates are positive, and in the bottom left piece both are negative.
So the two signs tell you which piece a point is in, without drawing anything.

:::definition[Quadrants]{#quadrants}

The axes split the points that lie on neither axis into four **quadrants**, numbered anticlockwise
starting from the top right:

1. **First quadrant**: $$x > 0$$ and $$y > 0$$
2. **Second quadrant**: $$x < 0$$ and $$y > 0$$
3. **Third quadrant**: $$x < 0$$ and $$y < 0$$
4. **Fourth quadrant**: $$x > 0$$ and $$y < 0$$

A point on an axis is in no quadrant.

:::

```plane
point X1 -4 0 hidden
point X2 4 0 hidden
point Y1 0 -3 hidden
point Y2 0 3 hidden
point O 0 0 sw
segment X1 X2
segment Y1 Y2
text 2 1.8 "first"
text 2 1.2 "x > 0, y > 0"
text -2 1.8 "second"
text -2 1.2 "x < 0, y > 0"
text -2 -1.2 "third"
text -2 -1.8 "x < 0, y < 0"
text 2 -1.2 "fourth"
text 2 -1.8 "x > 0, y < 0"
text 4.5 0 "x"
text 0 3.35 "y"
caption "The four quadrants, numbered anticlockwise from the top right, with the signs of x and y in each"
```

For example, $$(-3, 5)$$ has a negative $$x$$-coordinate and a positive $$y$$-coordinate, so it is
in the second quadrant. The point $$(2, -7)$$ is in the fourth. The point $$(0, -4)$$ lies on the
$$y$$-axis, so it is in no quadrant.

Move the sliders below to place a point $$P$$, and watch the dashed perpendiculars carry it to its
two coordinates. The button swaps the two numbers. Unless they are equal, that moves $$P$$ to a
different point, often in a different quadrant.

## Choosing the axes

Coordinates describe a point only together with the axes they were read from. Picture two people in
the same field, one measuring from the gate and the other from an oak tree. They give the same
molehill different numbers, neither of them is wrong, and the molehill hasn't moved. Changing the
origin or the directions of the axes gives every point new coordinates, but leaves the points, and
the distances between them, exactly as they were.

:::example[Moving the origin]

Move the origin to the point $$N$$ that had coordinates $$(2, 1)$$, and keep both axes pointing the
same ways as before. Counting grid squares, the point that was $$(5, 4)$$ is now $$5 - 2 = 3$$
across and $$4 - 1 = 3$$ up from the new origin. So its new coordinates are $$(3, 3)$$.

Every point changes in the same way: subtract the old coordinates of the new origin. The point that
was $$(x, y)$$ becomes $$(x - 2, y - 1)$$.

:::

```plane
point O 0 0 sw
point N 2 1 sw
point P 5 4 ne
point X1 -0.8 0 hidden
point X2 6 0 hidden
point Y1 0 -0.8 hidden
point Y2 0 4.8 hidden
point U1 1.2 1 hidden
point U2 6 1 hidden
point V1 2 0.2 hidden
point V2 2 4.8 hidden
point A 5 1 hidden
point B 2 4 hidden
segment X1 X2 color=muted
segment Y1 Y2 color=muted
segment U1 U2 color=blue
segment V1 V2 color=blue
segment P A color=orange dashed
segment P B color=green dashed
text 5 0.6 "3" color=orange
text 1.6 4 "3" color=green
text 3.5 0.6 "new x-axis" color=blue
caption "With the origin moved to N, the old point (5, 4) is 3 across and 3 up from N"
```

:::example[Turning the axes]

Keep the origin, but turn both axes a quarter turn anticlockwise about it. The new $$x$$-axis now
points up the page, where the old $$y$$-axis pointed. The new $$y$$-axis points to the left.

The point that was $$(5, 4)$$ lies $$4$$ up the page, which is $$4$$ along the new $$x$$-axis. It
also lies $$5$$ to the right, which is the new negative $$y$$ direction. So its new coordinates are
$$(4, -5)$$.

:::

However the axes are placed, one convention is kept: the positive $$y$$ direction is the positive
$$x$$ direction turned a quarter turn anticlockwise. With the $$x$$-axis pointing right, this puts
the $$y$$-axis pointing up. The other choice, a quarter turn clockwise, would make every figure
drawn from its coordinates come out as a mirror image.

This freedom to choose is useful. A problem about a rectangle gets tidier with the origin at one
corner and the axes along two sides. Then a rectangle with sides $$a$$ and $$b$$ has corners
$$(0, 0)$$, $$(a, 0)$$, $$(a, b)$$ and $$(0, b)$$.

## Coordinates in the world

Anything laid out on a grid uses coordinates. Each one makes its own choice of origin and
directions, and the choices aren't always the ones made in these notes.

The grid on British walking maps gives a place by two numbers: how far east it is, then how far
north. Walkers remember the order with the phrase "along the corridor, then up the stairs". The
origin sits out at sea, south-west of the country, so that every place in Britain has positive
coordinates.

A computer screen counts pixels from its top left corner instead. The first coordinate grows to the
right, as usual, but the second grows downwards. So a screen's $$y$$-axis is its $$x$$-axis turned a
quarter turn clockwise, the opposite of the convention, and a character in a game moves up the
screen when its $$y$$-coordinate gets smaller. A spreadsheet counts its rows downwards in the same
way, and names cell C5 by its column first and then its row.

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on coordinates, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-coordinates-quadrants}

Say which quadrant each point is in, or which axis it lies on:

1. $$(-3, 4)$$
2. $$(5, -2)$$
3. $$(0, -7)$$
4. $$(-1, -1)$$
5. $$(6, 0)$$

:::solution

Look at the signs, using :ref{#quadrants}:

1. $$x < 0$$ and $$y > 0$$: the second quadrant
2. $$x > 0$$ and $$y < 0$$: the fourth quadrant
3. $$x = 0$$: on the $$y$$-axis, so in no quadrant
4. $$x < 0$$ and $$y < 0$$: the third quadrant
5. $$y = 0$$: on the $$x$$-axis, so in no quadrant

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-coordinates-signs}

The point $$(a, b)$$ is in the second quadrant. Which quadrant is each of these points in?

1. $$(b, a)$$
2. $$(-a, b)$$
3. $$(a, -b)$$
4. $$(-a, -b)$$

:::solution

In the second quadrant $$a < 0$$ and $$b > 0$$ (:ref{#quadrants}). So $$-a > 0$$ and $$-b < 0$$.

1. $$(b, a)$$ has first coordinate $$b > 0$$ and second $$a < 0$$: the fourth quadrant
2. $$(-a, b)$$ has both coordinates positive: the first quadrant
3. $$(a, -b)$$ has both coordinates negative: the third quadrant
4. $$(-a, -b)$$ has first coordinate positive and second negative: the fourth quadrant

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-coordinates-swap}

For which points $$(a, b)$$ is $$(b, a)$$ the same point as $$(a, b)$$? Give three examples.

:::solution

By rule 1 of :ref{#coordinate-rules}, $$(b, a)$$ and $$(a, b)$$ are the same point exactly when
their first coordinates match and their second coordinates match. That says $$b = a$$ and $$a = b$$,
so it happens exactly when $$a = b$$. Examples: $$(0, 0)$$, $$(2, 2)$$ and $$(-1.5, -1.5)$$. These
points make up the graph of the equation $$y = x$$ (:ref{#linear-equations/graph}).

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-coordinates-rectangle}

The lines $$x = 1$$, $$x = 5$$, $$y = -1$$ and $$y = 2$$ enclose a shape.

1. Find the four points where a vertical one of these lines meets a horizontal one.
2. Explain why these four points are the corners of a rectangle.

:::solution

1. A point on $$x = 1$$ and $$y = -1$$ has both coordinates fixed, so it is $$(1, -1)$$. In the same
   way the others are $$(5, -1)$$, $$(5, 2)$$ and $$(1, 2)$$.
2. Go round in that order. The sides from $$(1, -1)$$ to $$(5, -1)$$ and from $$(5, 2)$$ to
   $$(1, 2)$$ lie on the horizontal lines $$y = -1$$ and $$y = 2$$. The other two sides lie on the
   vertical lines $$x = 5$$ and $$x = 1$$. By rule 3 of :ref{#coordinate-rules}, every vertical line
   is perpendicular to every horizontal line, so each side is perpendicular to the two next to it.
   That is a rectangle (:ref{#distance-and-angles/rectangle}).

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-coordinates-new-origin}

The origin is moved to the point that had coordinates $$(-2, 3)$$, and the axes keep their
directions.

1. What are the new coordinates of the point that was $$(1, 1)$$?
2. A point has new coordinates $$(4, 5)$$. What were its old coordinates?

:::solution

As in the example under [Choosing the axes](#choosing-the-axes), subtract the old coordinates of the
new origin.

1. $$(1 - (-2), 1 - 3) = (3, -2)$$
2. Going back means adding instead: $$(4 + (-2), 5 + 3) = (2, 8)$$. Check: $$2 - (-2) = 4$$ and
   $$8 - 3 = 5$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-coordinates-turned}

The axes are turned a quarter turn anticlockwise about the origin, as in the example under
[Choosing the axes](#choosing-the-axes).

1. Explain why the point that was $$(x, y)$$ gets the new coordinates $$(y, -x)$$.
2. Find the new coordinates of the points that were $$(3, 1)$$ and $$(-2, 5)$$.

:::solution

1. The new $$x$$-axis points up the page, so the new first coordinate measures how far up the point
   is: that is $$y$$. The new $$y$$-axis points left, so the new second coordinate measures how far
   left the point is. A point $$x$$ to the right is $$-x$$ to the left, so the new second coordinate
   is $$-x$$.
2. $$(3, 1)$$ becomes $$(1, -3)$$, and $$(-2, 5)$$ becomes $$(5, 2)$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-coordinates-screen}

A drawing program uses a canvas $$800$$ wide and $$600$$ tall, with its origin at the top left
corner and its second coordinate growing downwards. A dot sits at $$(200, 150)$$ on the canvas.

1. Is the dot nearer the top or the bottom of the canvas?
2. Put the origin at the bottom left corner instead, with the axes pointing right and up. What are
   the dot's coordinates now?

:::solution

1. It is $$150$$ down from the top, and the canvas is $$600$$ tall, so it is in the top quarter:
   nearer the top.
2. The first coordinate still counts from the left edge, so it stays $$200$$. The second now counts
   up from the bottom edge, which is $$600 - 150 = 450$$. The dot is at $$(200, 450)$$.

:::
