Imagine a dog on a lead $$5$$ metres long, tied to a post in a field. Mark the field in metres, with
the post $$3$$ metres east and $$2$$ metres north of the corner, so the post is at $$(3, 2)$$. When
the dog runs round with the lead pulled tight, its collar traces a circle, and every point on it is
exactly $$5$$ metres from the post.

That one fact about distance can be written as an equation in $$x$$ and $$y$$, called the **equation
of the circle**. In this guide we'll find that equation, learn to read a circle back off it, and use
it to say where a circle meets a line.

## From distance to an equation

A point is on the circle exactly when its distance from the centre is the radius
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/circle}). The distance formula (:ref{#distance-formula}) turns that
distance into numbers, but it has a square root in it. Squaring both sides removes the root and
leaves an equation with no roots at all.

Take the collar at $$(6, 6)$$. It is $$3$$ east and $$4$$ north of the post, so its distance from
the post is $$\sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{25} = 5$$, and it is on the circle. In general a point
$$(x, y)$$ is $$x - 3$$ east and $$y - 2$$ north of the post, and it is on the circle exactly when

```math
(x - 3)^2 + (y - 2)^2 = 25
```

:::theorem[Equation of a circle]{#circle-equation}

Let $$r > 0$$. The point $$(x, y)$$ is on the circle with centre $$(a, b)$$ and radius $$r$$ exactly
when

```math
(x - a)^2 + (y - b)^2 = r^2
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle#Cartesian_coordinates"}

Write $$C = (a, b)$$ and $$P = (x, y)$$, and $$S = (x - a)^2 + (y - b)^2$$. By the distance formula,
$$d(C, P) = \sqrt{S}$$, so $$d(C, P)^2 = S$$ (:ref{#real-numbers/square-root}). By
:ref{#distance-and-angles/circle}, $$P$$ is on the circle exactly when $$d(C, P) = r$$.

If $$d(C, P) = r$$, squaring both sides gives $$S = r^2$$. Conversely, if $$S = r^2$$, then
$$d(C, P)^2 = r^2$$. Both $$d(C, P)$$ and $$r$$ are not negative, so they are equal
(:ref{#real-numbers/equal-squares}).

:::

The circle with centre at the origin and radius $$1$$ comes up often enough to have a name, the
**unit circle**. Its equation is $$x^2 + y^2 = 1$$. The points $$(1, 0)$$ and $$(0, -1)$$ are on it,
and so is $$(0.6, 0.8)$$, since $$0.36 + 0.64 = 1$$.

## Reading a circle off its equation

The equation works backwards too. Given $$(x - a)^2 + (y - b)^2 = r^2$$, the centre is $$(a, b)$$
and the radius is the square root of the number on the right. So the equation and the circle carry
the same information, and you can move between them in either direction.

Two things need care. The first is the sign. The bracket $$(y + 5)$$ is $$\big(y - (-5)\big)$$, so
it belongs to a centre with $$b = -5$$, not $$5$$. The number in each bracket is the centre's
coordinate with its sign changed. The second is the right-hand side, which is the radius squared. An
equation ending in $$= 7$$ has radius $$\sqrt{7}$$, not $$7$$.

:::example[Centres and radii]

- **$$(x - 2)^2 + (y + 5)^2 = 7$$**: the centre is $$(2, -5)$$ and the radius is
  $$\sqrt{7} \approx 2.65$$
- **$$x^2 + (y - 1)^2 = 9$$**: the first bracket is $$(x - 0)^2$$, so the centre is $$(0, 1)$$, and
  the radius is $$3$$
- **$$(x + 4)^2 + y^2 = 0.25$$**: the centre is $$(-4, 0)$$ and the radius is $$0.5$$

:::

the centre with its sign changed, and the right-hand side grows as the square of the radius.

## Inside and outside

A point closer to the centre than the radius is inside the circle, and a point further away is
outside. Squaring keeps that comparison the right way round, because distances and radii are never
negative. So you can test a point by putting its coordinates into the left side of the equation and
comparing the answer with $$r^2$$, with no square roots needed.

:::proposition[Discs as inequalities]{#disc-inequality}

Let $$C = (a, b)$$, let $$r > 0$$, and let $$P = (x, y)$$. Write $$S = (x - a)^2 + (y - b)^2$$.

1. $$P$$ is in the disc with centre $$C$$ and radius $$r$$ exactly when $$S \le r^2$$
2. $$d(C, P) < r$$, so that $$P$$ is inside the circle, exactly when $$S < r^2$$
3. $$d(C, P) > r$$, so that $$P$$ is outside the circle, exactly when $$S > r^2$$

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disk_(mathematics)#Formulas"}

As in the proof of :ref{#circle-equation}, $$d(C, P)^2 = S$$, and both $$d(C, P)$$ and $$r$$ are not
negative.

**Part 2.** If $$d(C, P) < r$$, then $$d(C, P)^2 < r^2$$ by :ref{#real-numbers/power-order}, that
is, $$S < r^2$$. If $$S < r^2$$, then $$d(C, P)^2 < r^2$$, so $$d(C, P) < r$$ by
:ref{#real-numbers/power-order-converse}.

**Part 3** is the same, with the roles of $$d(C, P)$$ and $$r$$ swapped.

**Part 1.** By :ref{#distance-and-angles/circle}, $$P$$ is in the disc exactly when $$d(C, P) < r$$
or $$d(C, P) = r$$. By part 2 and :ref{#circle-equation}, that happens exactly when $$S < r^2$$ or
$$S = r^2$$, that is, when $$S \le r^2$$.

:::

:::example[A house and a phone mast]

A phone mast stands at $$(2, 3)$$ on a map marked in kilometres, and it reaches $$5$$ km. A house at
$$(6, 5)$$ gives

```math
(6 - 2)^2 + (5 - 3)^2 = 16 + 4 = 20
```

which is less than $$5^2 = 25$$, so the house is inside the circle and in range. A house at
$$(-2, -1)$$ gives $$16 + 16 = 32$$, which is more than $$25$$, so it is out of range. A house at
$$(5, 7)$$ gives $$9 + 16 = 25$$, so it sits exactly on the edge of the range.

:::

## Equations that have been multiplied out

Multiply out the brackets in a circle's equation and it changes shape. For example,
$$(x - 3)^2 + (y + 1)^2 = 4$$ becomes $$x^2 - 6x + 9 + y^2 + 2y + 1 = 4$$, which tidies up to

```math
x^2 + y^2 - 6x + 2y + 6 = 0
```

This is the same circle, but the centre and radius are no longer on show. To get them back, you
complete the square (:ref{#quadratics/complete-square}) twice: once for the terms in $$x$$ and once
for the terms in $$y$$.

:::proposition[Expanded equations]{#expanded-circle}

Let $$D$$, $$E$$ and $$F$$ be real numbers. The points $$(x, y)$$ with

```math
x^2 + y^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0
```

are exactly the points with

```math
\left(x + \frac{D}{2}\right)^2 + \left(y + \frac{E}{2}\right)^2 = c, \qquad \text{where } c = \frac{D^2 + E^2}{4} - F
```

So the points form:

1. the circle with centre $$\left(-\frac{D}{2}, -\frac{E}{2}\right)$$ and radius $$\sqrt{c}$$, when
   $$c > 0$$
2. the single point $$\left(-\frac{D}{2}, -\frac{E}{2}\right)$$, when $$c = 0$$
3. no points at all, when $$c < 0$$

:::

:::proof{source="https://www.mathsisfun.com/algebra/circle-equations.html"}

By :ref{#quadratics/complete-square}, for every $$x$$ and $$y$$,

```math
x^2 + Dx = \left(x + \frac{D}{2}\right)^2 - \frac{D^2}{4} \qquad y^2 + Ey = \left(y + \frac{E}{2}\right)^2 - \frac{E^2}{4}
```

Replacing $$x^2 + Dx$$ and $$y^2 + Ey$$ in the equation by these equal amounts gives

```math
\left(x + \frac{D}{2}\right)^2 + \left(y + \frac{E}{2}\right)^2 - \frac{D^2}{4} - \frac{E^2}{4} + F = 0
```

and adding $$\frac{D^2 + E^2}{4} - F$$ to both sides gives the second form. Each step can be undone,
so the two equations have exactly the same solutions. Now take the three cases.

**Case $$c > 0$$.** Then $$c = \left(\sqrt{c}\right)^2$$, and the second form is the equation of the
circle with centre $$\left(-\frac{D}{2}, -\frac{E}{2}\right)$$ and radius $$\sqrt{c}$$, by
:ref{#circle-equation}.

**Case $$c = 0$$.** A sum of two squares is $$0$$ exactly when both numbers being squared are $$0$$
(:ref{#real-numbers/squares-positive}). So $$x + \frac{D}{2} = 0$$ and $$y + \frac{E}{2} = 0$$, and
the only solution is $$\left(-\frac{D}{2}, -\frac{E}{2}\right)$$.

**Case $$c < 0$$.** The left side is a sum of two squares, so it is never negative
(:ref{#real-numbers/squares-positive}). It can't equal a negative number, so there are no solutions.

:::

:::example[A circle, a point, and nothing]

- **$$x^2 + y^2 - 6x + 2y + 6 = 0$$**: here $$D = -6$$, $$E = 2$$ and $$F = 6$$, so
  $$c = \frac{36 + 4}{4} - 6 = 4$$. This is the circle with centre $$(3, -1)$$ and radius $$2$$, the
  one we started from.
- **$$x^2 + y^2 + 4x - 10y + 29 = 0$$**: here $$c = \frac{16 + 100}{4} - 29 = 0$$. The only point is
  $$(-2, 5)$$.
- **$$x^2 + y^2 - 2x + 8y + 20 = 0$$**: here $$c = \frac{4 + 64}{4} - 20 = -3$$. No point fits.

:::

You don't need to remember the formula for $$c$$. Completing the square in each example by hand
gives the same answer, and shows where each number comes from.

## Where a circle meets a horizontal line

Draw a horizontal line across a circle. It can miss the circle, touch it at the top or the bottom,
or cut straight through it at two points. Putting the line's equation into the circle's equation
leaves a quadratic in $$x$$, and its discriminant tells the three cases apart.

Take the circle $$(x - 2)^2 + (y - 1)^2 = 25$$, with centre $$(2, 1)$$ and radius $$5$$. On the line
$$y = 4$$, every point has $$y = 4$$, so the meeting points have $$(x - 2)^2 + 9 = 25$$. That gives
$$(x - 2)^2 = 16$$, so $$x - 2 = 4$$ or $$x - 2 = -4$$, and the line meets the circle at $$(6, 4)$$
and $$(-2, 4)$$. The line $$y = 6$$ gives $$(x - 2)^2 + 25 = 25$$, so $$x = 2$$ and it touches the
circle at $$(2, 6)$$ only. The line $$y = 7$$ gives $$(x - 2)^2 = -11$$, which has no solution, so
it misses.

:::proposition[A circle and a horizontal line]{#circle-horizontal-line}

Let $$r > 0$$. The circle with centre $$(a, b)$$ and radius $$r$$ meets the line $$y = k$$ at the
points $$(x, k)$$ for which

```math
x^2 - 2ax + \left(a^2 + (k - b)^2 - r^2\right) = 0
```

This quadratic has discriminant $$4\left(r^2 - (k - b)^2\right)$$. So the line and the circle meet:

1. at two points, when $$|k - b| < r$$
2. at the one point $$(a, k)$$, when $$|k - b| = r$$
3. nowhere, when $$|k - b| > r$$

:::

:::proof{source="https://mathworld.wolfram.com/Circle-LineIntersection.html"}

The points of the line are the points $$(x, k)$$. By :ref{#circle-equation}, such a point is on the
circle exactly when $$(x - a)^2 + (k - b)^2 = r^2$$. Expanding $$(x - a)^2$$ with
:ref{#numbers/square-formulas} and taking $$r^2$$ from both sides gives the quadratic in the
statement.

Its discriminant (:ref{#quadratics/discriminant}) is

```math
(-2a)^2 - 4\left(a^2 + (k - b)^2 - r^2\right) = 4a^2 - 4a^2 - 4(k - b)^2 + 4r^2 = 4\left(r^2 - (k - b)^2\right)
```

This has the same sign as $$r^2 - (k - b)^2$$, and $$(k - b)^2 = |k - b|^2$$ by part 4 of
:ref{#real-numbers/abs-basics}. Both $$|k - b|$$ and $$r$$ are not negative, so $$|k - b|^2$$ and
$$r^2$$ come in the same order as $$|k - b|$$ and $$r$$ (:ref{#real-numbers/power-order} and
:ref{#real-numbers/power-order-converse}). So the discriminant is positive, zero or negative exactly
when $$|k - b|$$ is less than, equal to or more than $$r$$. By :ref{#quadratics/root-count}, that
gives two solutions, one solution or none.

In the middle case the one solution is $$x = -\frac{-2a}{2} = a$$, so the meeting point is
$$(a, k)$$.

:::

The number $$|k - b|$$ is how far the line is above or below the centre. So the proposition says
what the picture suggests: a horizontal line crosses the circle when it passes closer to the centre
than the radius, touches it when it is exactly the radius away, and misses it when it is further.
Vertical lines $$x = h$$ work the same way, with the roles of $$x$$ and $$y$$ swapped. Lines that
slope need the equations of lines, and
[Lines and circles](../segments-rays-and-lines/lines-and-circles.mdx) handles them in a later
chapter.

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on the equation of a circle, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-circles-read}

Give the centre and the radius of each circle:

1. $$(x - 1)^2 + (y + 3)^2 = 16$$
2. $$(x + 2)^2 + y^2 = 5$$
3. $$x^2 + y^2 = 0.49$$

:::solution

By :ref{#circle-equation}, each number in a bracket is the centre's coordinate with its sign
changed, and the radius is the square root of the right-hand side:

1. Centre $$(1, -3)$$, radius $$\sqrt{16} = 4$$
2. Centre $$(-2, 0)$$, radius $$\sqrt{5}$$
3. Centre $$(0, 0)$$, radius $$\sqrt{0.49} = 0.7$$

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-circles-write}

Write down the equation of the circle with centre $$(-3, 4)$$ and radius $$2$$. Is the point
$$(-3, 6)$$ on it? Is $$(-1, 5)$$ inside it, on it, or outside it?

:::solution

By :ref{#circle-equation}, the equation is

```math
(x + 3)^2 + (y - 4)^2 = 4
```

For $$(-3, 6)$$, the left side is $$0^2 + 2^2 = 4$$, so the point is on the circle. For $$(-1, 5)$$,
it is $$2^2 + 1^2 = 5$$, which is more than $$4$$, so the point is outside (:ref{#disc-inequality}).

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-circles-through}

A circle has centre $$(1, -2)$$ and passes through the point $$(4, 2)$$. Find its equation.

:::solution

The radius is the distance from the centre to $$(4, 2)$$. The steps are $$3$$ across and $$4$$ up,
so $$r^2 = 3^2 + 4^2 = 25$$. There is no need to take the square root, since the equation uses
$$r^2$$:

```math
(x - 1)^2 + (y + 2)^2 = 25
```

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-circles-expanded}

Find the centre and the radius of the circle $$x^2 + y^2 + 8x - 6y = 0$$. Explain why it passes
through the origin.

:::solution

Complete the square in $$x$$ and in $$y$$ (:ref{#quadratics/complete-square}):

```math
x^2 + 8x = (x + 4)^2 - 16 \qquad y^2 - 6y = (y - 3)^2 - 9
```

So the equation becomes $$(x + 4)^2 + (y - 3)^2 - 25 = 0$$, that is, $$(x + 4)^2 + (y - 3)^2 = 25$$.
The centre is $$(-4, 3)$$ and the radius is $$5$$ (:ref{#expanded-circle}).

Putting $$x = 0$$ and $$y = 0$$ into the original equation gives $$0 = 0$$, so the origin is on the
circle. That happens because the equation has no number term: $$F = 0$$. Check with distances: the
origin is $$\sqrt{16 + 9} = 5$$ from the centre.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-circles-scaled}

Find the centre and the radius of the circle $$2x^2 + 2y^2 - 4x + 12y + 2 = 0$$.

:::solution

Divide both sides by $$2$$ first, so that $$x^2$$ and $$y^2$$ each have a $$1$$ in front:

```math
x^2 + y^2 - 2x + 6y + 1 = 0
```

Now $$D = -2$$, $$E = 6$$ and $$F = 1$$, so by :ref{#expanded-circle},
$$c = \frac{4 + 36}{4} - 1 = 9$$. The centre is $$(1, -3)$$ and the radius is $$\sqrt{9} = 3$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-circles-which}

For which numbers $$F$$ is $$x^2 + y^2 - 6x + 4y + F = 0$$ the equation of a circle? What happens
for the other values of $$F$$?

:::solution

Here $$D = -6$$ and $$E = 4$$, so by :ref{#expanded-circle},

```math
c = \frac{36 + 16}{4} - F = 13 - F
```

- **$$F < 13$$**: $$c > 0$$, so the equation is a circle with centre $$(3, -2)$$ and radius
  $$\sqrt{13 - F}$$
- **$$F = 13$$**: $$c = 0$$, so the only point is $$(3, -2)$$
- **$$F > 13$$**: $$c < 0$$, so no point fits

As $$F$$ grows towards $$13$$, the circle shrinks onto its centre.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-circles-vertical}

Find where the circle $$x^2 + y^2 = 25$$ meets each vertical line: $$x = 3$$, $$x = 5$$ and
$$x = -6$$.

:::solution

Every point of the line $$x = h$$ has first coordinate $$h$$, so the meeting points solve
$$h^2 + y^2 = 25$$ (:ref{#circle-equation}).

- **$$x = 3$$**: $$y^2 = 16$$, so $$y = 4$$ or $$y = -4$$. The points are $$(3, 4)$$ and
  $$(3, -4)$$.
- **$$x = 5$$**: $$y^2 = 0$$, so $$y = 0$$. The line touches the circle at $$(5, 0)$$ only.
- **$$x = -6$$**: $$y^2 = -11$$, which has no solution, so the line misses the circle.

The centre is $$(0, 0)$$ and the radius $$5$$. The three lines are $$3$$, $$5$$ and $$6$$ away from
the centre, which is less than, equal to and more than the radius.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-circles-masts}

A phone at $$(1, 1)$$, on a map marked in kilometres, can pick up two masts. One stands at
$$(4, 5)$$ and reaches $$5$$ km. The other stands at $$(-3, -2)$$ and reaches $$4$$ km. Which masts
is the phone in range of? Use no square roots.

:::solution

By :ref{#disc-inequality}, compare each squared distance with the squared range.

- **First mast**: $$(1 - 4)^2 + (1 - 5)^2 = 9 + 16 = 25$$, and $$5^2 = 25$$. The phone is on the
  edge of the range, which still counts as in the disc, so it is in range.
- **Second mast**: $$(1 + 3)^2 + (1 + 2)^2 = 16 + 9 = 25$$, and $$4^2 = 16$$. Since $$25 > 16$$, the
  phone is out of range.

So the phone is in range of the first mast, right on the edge, and out of range of the second.

:::
