---
title: The distance between two points
description:
  A formula for the distance between two points from their coordinates, found with Pythagoras.
---

Imagine a taxi and a delivery drone setting off from the same street corner, in a city laid out in
square blocks. Both are heading for a café $$3$$ blocks east and $$4$$ blocks north. The taxi has to
keep to the streets, so it covers $$7$$ blocks however it turns. The drone flies straight over the
rooftops and covers only $$5$$. The length of the drone's route is the distance between the two
places, and the coordinates of the two ends are enough to work it out. In this guide we'll find a
formula for it with the Pythagoras theorem, and get used to using it.

## Points level with each other

Two points at the same height sit on one horizontal line, which is a copy of the $$x$$-axis lifted
up or down. Measuring along it works as on the number line: subtract the $$x$$-coordinates and
ignore the sign. So $$(1, 3)$$ and $$(6, 3)$$ are $$5$$ apart, and $$(-2, 3)$$ and $$(4, 3)$$ are
$$6$$ apart, since $$4 - (-2) = 6$$. Points one above the other work the same way with their
$$y$$-coordinates.

The same distance can also be written as the square root of a square. That looks roundabout, but it
is the form the distance formula uses.

:::proposition[Distance along a grid line]{#line-distance}

1. For any real numbers $$a$$ and $$b$$,

   ```math
   \sqrt{(a - b)^2} = |a - b| = \sqrt{(b - a)^2}
   ```

2. Two points with the same $$y$$-coordinate, $$(x_1, c)$$ and $$(x_2, c)$$, are at distance
   $$|x_1 - x_2|$$. Two points with the same $$x$$-coordinate, $$(c, y_1)$$ and $$(c, y_2)$$, are at
   distance $$|y_1 - y_2|$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_distance#One_dimension"}

**Part 1.** By part 4 of :ref{#real-numbers/abs-basics}, $$\sqrt{(a - b)^2} = |a - b|$$, and in the
same way $$\sqrt{(b - a)^2} = |b - a|$$. Since $$b - a = -(a - b)$$, part 2 of
:ref{#real-numbers/abs-basics} gives $$|b - a| = |a - b|$$.

**Part 2.** Let $$P = (x_1, c)$$ and $$Q = (x_2, c)$$. If $$x_1 = x_2$$, they are the same point by
rule 1 of :ref{#coordinate-rules}, and their distance is $$0 = |x_1 - x_2|$$. If $$c = 0$$, both lie
on the $$x$$-axis, and rule 2 of :ref{#coordinate-rules} gives the distance $$|x_1 - x_2|$$.

Otherwise, take the points $$A = (x_1, 0)$$ and $$B = (x_2, 0)$$ on the $$x$$-axis. Go round $$A$$,
$$B$$, $$Q$$, $$P$$ in that order. The sides $$AB$$ and $$QP$$ lie on the horizontal lines $$y = 0$$
and $$y = c$$, and the sides $$BQ$$ and $$PA$$ lie on the vertical lines $$x = x_2$$ and
$$x = x_1$$. By rule 3 of :ref{#coordinate-rules}, each side is perpendicular to the two next to it,
so $$ABQP$$ is a rectangle (:ref{#distance-and-angles/rectangle}). Its opposite sides are equal
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/rectangle-sides}), so by rule 2 of :ref{#coordinate-rules},

```math
d(P, Q) = d(A, B) = |x_1 - x_2|
```

Points with the same $$x$$-coordinate are handled the same way, with the $$y$$-axis in place of the
$$x$$-axis.

:::

## The distance formula

Now take two points that are neither level nor one above the other. You can get from one to the
other in two moves: straight across until you are directly below or above the second point, then
straight up or down to it. The two moves meet at a square corner, so together with the direct path
they make a right triangle. The moves are its legs and the direct path is its hypotenuse, so the
Pythagoras theorem gives the distance.

For example, take $$P = (1, 1)$$ and $$Q = (4, 5)$$. The corner is $$R = (4, 1)$$, level with $$P$$
and straight below $$Q$$. The across move has length $$4 - 1 = 3$$ and the up move has length
$$5 - 1 = 4$$, so

```math
d(P, Q) = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5
```


The same steps work for any two points: subtract the $$x$$-coordinates and square, subtract the
$$y$$-coordinates and square, add the two squares, and take the square root.

:::theorem[The distance formula]{#distance-formula}

The distance between the points $$P = (x_1, y_1)$$ and $$Q = (x_2, y_2)$$ is

```math
d(P, Q) = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_distance#Two_dimensions"}

**Level points, or one above the other.** If $$y_1 = y_2$$, the formula gives
$$\sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + 0} = |x_2 - x_1|$$, by part 1 of :ref{#line-distance}. That is $$d(P, Q)$$,
by part 2. If $$x_1 = x_2$$, the formula gives $$|y_2 - y_1|$$, which is $$d(P, Q)$$ in the same
way. This case includes $$P = Q$$.

**Any other two points.** Now $$x_1 \ne x_2$$ and $$y_1 \ne y_2$$. Let $$R = (x_2, y_1)$$. The
points $$P$$ and $$R$$ both lie on the horizontal line $$y = y_1$$, which is the only line through
them (:ref{#distance-and-angles/line-rules}), and $$Q$$ is not on it since $$y_2 \ne y_1$$. So
$$P$$, $$R$$ and $$Q$$ are the corners of a triangle (:ref{#distance-and-angles/triangle}). The
points $$R$$ and $$Q$$ lie on the vertical line $$x = x_2$$. By rule 3 of :ref{#coordinate-rules},
the vertical line is perpendicular to the horizontal one, so the triangle has a right angle at $$R$$
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/right-triangle}).

By part 2 of :ref{#line-distance}, the legs have lengths $$d(P, R) = |x_2 - x_1|$$ and
$$d(R, Q) = |y_2 - y_1|$$. The hypotenuse is $$PQ$$, so :ref{#distance-and-angles/pythagoras-sides}
gives

```math
d(P, Q) = \sqrt{|x_2 - x_1|^2 + |y_2 - y_1|^2} = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}
```

since $$|a|^2 = a^2$$ for every number $$a$$ (:ref{#real-numbers/abs-basics}).

:::

Move $$P$$ and $$Q$$ below. The corner $$R$$ follows them, and the readout works through the formula
one step at a time. When you put the two points level, the triangle flattens into a single segment,
but the formula still gives the right answer.

<DistanceTriangle p={[-2, -1]} q={[2, 2]} />

The formula treats the two points alike. Swapping them changes the sign of each difference, so
$$x_1 - x_2$$ appears in place of $$x_2 - x_1$$, but squaring removes the sign. So
$$d(Q, P) = d(P, Q)$$, as rule 2 of :ref{#distance-and-angles/distance-rules} says. It also means
you can call either point $$P$$.

## Working with the formula

The formula is mostly subtraction, and the place to take care is the minus signs. When a negative
coordinate is subtracted, put it in brackets, so that the two minus signs visibly make a plus. A
negative difference needs brackets when it is squared too, since its square is positive.

:::example[Points on opposite sides of the origin]

Take $$P = (-2, 3)$$ and $$Q = (4, -5)$$. The differences are

```math
x_2 - x_1 = 4 - (-2) = 6 \qquad y_2 - y_1 = -5 - 3 = -8
```

So

```math
d(P, Q) = \sqrt{6^2 + (-8)^2} = \sqrt{36 + 64} = \sqrt{100} = 10
```

Leaving out the first pair of brackets would give $$4 - 2 = 2$$, which is wrong. And $$(-8)^2$$ is
$$64$$, while $$-8^2$$, without brackets, means $$-64$$. Taken the other way round, the differences
are $$-6$$ and $$8$$, which have the same squares.

:::

The answer is often not a whole number. Then it can be left as a square root, or tidied by taking
out a square factor with :ref{#real-numbers/root-product}.

:::example[An answer that stays a square root]

Take $$P = (1, -2)$$ and $$Q = (-2, 4)$$. The differences are $$-2 - 1 = -3$$ and $$4 - (-2) = 6$$,
so

```math
d(P, Q) = \sqrt{(-3)^2 + 6^2} = \sqrt{9 + 36} = \sqrt{45}
```

Since $$45 = 9 \cdot 5$$, this is $$\sqrt{9}\,\sqrt{5} = 3\sqrt{5}$$, which is about $$6.71$$. The
exact form is the answer, and the decimal is useful for a measurement.

:::

The distance of a point from the origin comes out especially tidy. With $$O = (0, 0)$$ and
$$P = (x, y)$$, the differences are $$x$$ and $$y$$ themselves, so $$d(O, P) = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$$.
For example, $$(5, -12)$$ is $$\sqrt{25 + 144} = \sqrt{169} = 13$$ from the origin.

## As the crow flies

People say "as the crow flies" for the straight-line distance, the one a bird or a drone covers, to
set it apart from the distance by road. The distance formula gives the crow's distance. On a grid of
streets the taxi's distance is $$|x_2 - x_1| + |y_2 - y_1|$$ instead: the two legs added, with no
squaring. It is never shorter than the crow's, since two sides of a triangle together are at least
as long as the third (:ref{#distance-and-angles/distance-rules}).

Often you need only to compare a distance with a fixed number, and then no square root is needed.
Both the distance and the number are at least $$0$$, so the distance is at most $$5$$ exactly when
its square is at most $$25$$ (:ref{#real-numbers/power-order},
:ref{#real-numbers/power-order-converse}).

:::example[Checking whether a phone is in range]

A phone mast stands at $$(2, 3)$$, with coordinates in kilometres, and reaches phones up to $$5$$ km
away. A phone at $$(6, 5)$$ has squared distance

```math
(6 - 2)^2 + (5 - 3)^2 = 16 + 4 = 20
```

which is less than $$25$$, so it is in range. A phone at $$(-2, 7)$$ has squared distance
$$(-4)^2 + 4^2 = 32$$, which is more than $$25$$, so it is out of range.

:::

A program checking thousands of phones can use the same shortcut. Comparing squares saves working
out a square root for each phone.

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on the distance between two points, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-distance-formula-practice}

Find the distance between each pair of points. Leave an answer that isn't a whole number as a square
root, with any square factor taken out.

1. $$(2, 1)$$ and $$(7, 13)$$
2. $$(-3, -1)$$ and $$(3, 7)$$
3. $$(0, -4)$$ and $$(-5, 0)$$
4. $$(-1.5, 2)$$ and $$(1.5, -2)$$
5. $$(3, -1)$$ and $$(-1, -3)$$

:::solution

By :ref{#distance-formula}:

1. $$\sqrt{5^2 + 12^2} = \sqrt{25 + 144} = \sqrt{169} = 13$$
2. $$\sqrt{6^2 + 8^2} = \sqrt{36 + 64} = \sqrt{100} = 10$$
3. $$\sqrt{(-5)^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{25 + 16} = \sqrt{41}$$
4. $$\sqrt{3^2 + (-4)^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5$$
5. $$\sqrt{(-4)^2 + (-2)^2} = \sqrt{16 + 4} = \sqrt{20} = 2\sqrt{5}$$, using
   :ref{#real-numbers/root-product} with $$20 = 4 \cdot 5$$

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-distance-formula-mistake}

A student works out the distance between $$(-1, 4)$$ and $$(3, -2)$$ as

```math
\sqrt{(3 - 1)^2 + (-2 - 4)^2} = \sqrt{4 + 36} = \sqrt{40}
```

Find the mistake and give the right answer.

:::solution

The first difference should be $$3 - (-1) = 4$$, not $$3 - 1 = 2$$: the minus sign of $$-1$$ was
lost. The second difference, $$-2 - 4 = -6$$, is right. So the distance is

```math
\sqrt{4^2 + (-6)^2} = \sqrt{16 + 36} = \sqrt{52} = 2\sqrt{13}
```

using $$52 = 4 \cdot 13$$ and :ref{#real-numbers/root-product}.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-distance-formula-missing}

The points $$(1, 2)$$ and $$(x, 6)$$ are $$5$$ apart. Find every possible value of $$x$$, and
explain with a picture why there are two.

:::solution

By :ref{#distance-formula}, $$\sqrt{(x - 1)^2 + 4^2} = 5$$. Both sides are at least $$0$$, so this
holds exactly when the squares are equal (:ref{#real-numbers/equal-squares}):
$$(x - 1)^2 + 16 = 25$$, that is, $$(x - 1)^2 = 9$$. So $$x - 1 = 3$$ or $$x - 1 = -3$$
(:ref{#real-numbers/square-root-count}), which gives $$x = 4$$ or $$x = -2$$.

The point $$(x, 6)$$ lies on the horizontal line $$y = 6$$, which is $$4$$ above $$(1, 2)$$. A right
triangle with height $$4$$ and hypotenuse $$5$$ has base $$3$$, and the base can stretch $$3$$ to
the right of $$x = 1$$ or $$3$$ to the left. Check: $$(4 - 1)^2 + 4^2 = 25$$ and
$$(-2 - 1)^2 + 4^2 = 25$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-distance-formula-isosceles}

Show that the triangle with corners $$A = (1, 1)$$, $$B = (5, 3)$$ and $$C = (3, 7)$$ has two sides
of the same length. Which side is the odd one out?

:::solution

By :ref{#distance-formula}:

- $$d(A, B) = \sqrt{4^2 + 2^2} = \sqrt{20}$$
- $$d(B, C) = \sqrt{(-2)^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{20}$$
- $$d(A, C) = \sqrt{2^2 + 6^2} = \sqrt{40}$$

So $$AB$$ and $$BC$$ both have length $$\sqrt{20} = 2\sqrt{5}$$, and $$AC$$, of length
$$\sqrt{40} = 2\sqrt{10}$$, is the odd one out.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-distance-formula-range}

A mast at $$(-2, 1)$$ reaches phones up to $$6$$ km away, with coordinates in kilometres. Which of
the phones at $$(3, 4)$$, $$(2, -3)$$ and $$(-6, 6)$$ are in range? Don't work out any square roots.

:::solution

As in the example under [As the crow flies](#as-the-crow-flies), compare each squared distance with
$$6^2 = 36$$:

- $$(3, 4)$$: $$5^2 + 3^2 = 25 + 9 = 34$$, which is at most $$36$$, so in range
- $$(2, -3)$$: $$4^2 + (-4)^2 = 16 + 16 = 32$$, so in range
- $$(-6, 6)$$: $$(-4)^2 + 5^2 = 16 + 25 = 41$$, which is more than $$36$$, so out of range

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-distance-formula-equal}

Find the point on the $$x$$-axis that is the same distance from $$(0, 4)$$ as from $$(6, 2)$$.

:::solution

A point on the $$x$$-axis has the form $$(x, 0)$$. Its squared distances from the two points are
$$x^2 + 16$$ and $$(x - 6)^2 + 4$$ (:ref{#distance-formula}). The distances are equal exactly when
their squares are, since both are at least $$0$$ (:ref{#real-numbers/equal-squares}). So

```math
x^2 + 16 = x^2 - 12x + 36 + 4
```

Taking $$x^2$$ from both sides leaves $$16 = 40 - 12x$$, so $$12x = 24$$ and $$x = 2$$. The point is
$$(2, 0)$$. Check: both squared distances are $$4 + 16 = 20$$ and $$16 + 4 = 20$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-distance-formula-taxi}

The taxi distance between $$P = (x_1, y_1)$$ and $$Q = (x_2, y_2)$$ is $$|a| + |b|$$, where
$$a = x_2 - x_1$$ and $$b = y_2 - y_1$$.

1. Show by squaring that $$\sqrt{a^2 + b^2} \le |a| + |b|$$, so the crow's distance is never more
   than the taxi's.
2. When are the two distances equal? Describe the points $$P$$ and $$Q$$.

:::solution

1. Both sides are at least $$0$$, so it is enough to compare their squares. Using $$|a|^2 = a^2$$
   and $$|b|^2 = b^2$$ (:ref{#real-numbers/abs-basics}),

   ```math
   (|a| + |b|)^2 = a^2 + 2|a|\,|b| + b^2 \ge a^2 + b^2
   ```

   since $$2|a|\,|b| \ge 0$$. So the square of $$\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$$, which is $$a^2 + b^2$$, is at
   most the square of $$|a| + |b|$$, and :ref{#real-numbers/power-order-converse} gives
   $$\sqrt{a^2 + b^2} \le |a| + |b|$$.

2. The squares are equal exactly when $$2|a|\,|b| = 0$$, that is, when $$a = 0$$ or $$b = 0$$. So
   the two distances agree exactly when $$P$$ and $$Q$$ lie on one vertical or one horizontal line,
   where a taxi can drive straight there.

:::
