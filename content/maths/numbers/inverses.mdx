---
title: Inverses and division
description: Multiplicative inverses, dividing by rational numbers, and solving equations.
---

Every integer has an opposite, which undoes adding it: $$a + (-a) = 0$$. Fractions give us the same
thing for multiplication. This is the one big way rational numbers improve on the integers. For
example, no integer multiplied by $$2$$ gives $$1$$, but $$\frac{1}{2} \cdot 2 = 1$$.

This page uses that idea to make division precise. It explains why every number except $$0$$ can be
divided by, why dividing by a fraction means multiplying by its flip, and how to solve equations
that need dividing. Along the way, the rule for equal fractions, which was a definition on the
previous page, turns out to be something we can prove.

## Inverses

Adding a number can be undone by adding its opposite: add $$5$$, then add $$-5$$, and you are back
where you started. Multiplying can be undone in a similar way. Multiply by $$3$$ and then by
$$\frac{1}{3}$$, and you are back where you started, because $$3 \cdot \frac{1}{3} = 1$$ and
multiplying by $$1$$ changes nothing. A number that multiplies with another to give $$1$$ is called
its inverse. Among the integers only $$1$$ and $$-1$$ have inverses, but every fraction except $$0$$
has one.

:::definition[Multiplicative inverse]{#inverse}

Let $$a$$ be a rational number. A **multiplicative inverse** of $$a$$ is a rational number $$b$$
with

```math
ab = ba = 1
```

:::

:::proposition[Every non-zero fraction has an inverse]{#inverse-exists}

Let $$m$$ and $$n$$ be non-zero integers. Then $$\frac{n}{m}$$ is an inverse of $$\frac{m}{n}$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraction#Reciprocals_and_the_invisible_denominator"}

First, $$\frac{n}{m}$$ is a genuine fraction, because its denominator $$m$$ is not $$0$$. Now we
just multiply and check that we get $$1$$. By :ref{#fraction-multiplication},

```math
\frac{m}{n} \cdot \frac{n}{m} = \frac{mn}{nm} = \frac{mn}{mn} = \frac{mn \cdot 1}{mn \cdot 1}
= \frac{1}{1} = 1
```

using $$nm = mn$$ in the second step. The next step cancels the common factor $$mn$$ with
:ref{#fraction-cancel}, which is allowed because $$mn \neq 0$$: a product of two non-zero integers
is never $$0$$, as in the proof of :ref{#fraction-cancel}. The product the other way round,
$$\frac{n}{m} \cdot \frac{m}{n}$$, is the same, since order doesn't matter in a product.

For example, $$\frac{3}{7} \cdot \frac{7}{3} = \frac{21}{21} = 1$$.

:::

So every non-zero rational number has an inverse, and the next result shows it has only one. We
write it $$a^{-1}$$ or $$\frac{1}{a}$$. To invert a fraction, flip it upside down. The inverse of
$$\frac{3}{7}$$ is $$\frac{7}{3}$$, the inverse of $$5 = \frac{5}{1}$$ is $$\frac{1}{5}$$, and the
inverse of $$-\frac{2}{9}$$ is $$-\frac{9}{2}$$.

The condition $$m \neq 0$$ matters: $$0$$ has **no** inverse, because $$0 \cdot b = 0$$ for every
$$b$$, so it is never $$1$$. That is the real reason we can't divide by zero.

:::proposition[Inverses are unique]{#inverse-unique}

Let $$a$$ and $$b$$ be rational numbers with $$a \neq 0$$. If $$ab = 1$$, then $$b = a^{-1}$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_(mathematics)#Uniqueness_of_inverses"}

This mirrors the fact that an opposite is the only way back to zero (:ref{#opposite-unique}).

First, $$a$$ really does have an inverse to compare $$b$$ with. Write $$a = \frac{m}{n}$$. The
numerator $$m$$ is not $$0$$, because $$\frac{0}{n} = 0$$ and $$a \neq 0$$. So by
:ref{#inverse-exists}, $$\frac{n}{m}$$ is an inverse of $$a$$; call it $$a^{-1}$$.

The idea is now to start from $$b$$ and slip in a factor $$a^{-1}a = 1$$, so that the given
$$ab = 1$$ can be used:

```math
b = 1 \cdot b = (a^{-1}a)b = a^{-1}(ab) = a^{-1} \cdot 1 = a^{-1}
```

Step by step: multiplying by $$1$$ changes nothing; $$a^{-1}a = 1$$ because $$a^{-1}$$ is an inverse
of $$a$$; the grouping of a product doesn't matter; $$ab = 1$$ is what we were told; and multiplying
by $$1$$ changes nothing again. So $$b = a^{-1}$$: any number that multiplies with $$a$$ to give
$$1$$ is the same as the inverse we already had, so there is only one.

:::

This gives a quick way to check that something is an inverse: just check that the product is $$1$$.

## Negative powers

Look at the powers of $$2$$ going down: $$16$$, $$8$$, $$4$$, $$2$$, $$1$$. Each time the exponent
drops by one, the answer is halved. Carrying the pattern on past $$2^0 = 1$$ gives $$\frac{1}{2}$$,
then $$\frac{1}{4}$$, then $$\frac{1}{8}$$, so $$2^{-1}$$ should be $$\frac{1}{2}$$ and $$2^{-3}$$
should be $$\frac{1}{8}$$. Inverses let powers have negative exponents in exactly this way. Since
$$a^{-1}$$ undoes one factor of $$a$$, it makes sense for $$a^{-n}$$ to undo $$n$$ of them.

:::definition[Negative powers]{#negative-powers}

For a non-zero rational number $$a$$ and a positive integer $$n$$,

```math
a^{-n} = \left(a^{-1}\right)^n = \underbrace{a^{-1} \cdot a^{-1} \cdots a^{-1}}_{n \text{ times}}
```

:::

For example, $$2^{-3} = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^3 = \frac{1}{8}$$, and
$$\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{-2} = \left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{9}{4}$$. With this choice, the
rules in :ref{#power-rules} work for **every** whole-number exponent, positive, negative or zero.
Each $$a^{-1}$$ cancels one $$a$$, since $$a^{-1}a = 1$$, so for example

```math
a^5 a^{-2} = (a \cdot a \cdot a \cdot a \cdot a)(a^{-1} \cdot a^{-1}) = a \cdot a \cdot a = a^3
= a^{5 + (-2)}
```

and $$a^n a^{-n} = a^0 = 1$$, which is where the choice $$a^0 = 1$$ from the
[Multiplication](./multiplication.mdx) page fits in.

## Products equal to zero

Two numbers that are not zero never multiply to give zero. For whole numbers this is no surprise,
but with fractions you might wonder whether two very small numbers could multiply to exactly $$0$$.
They can't: $$\frac{1}{1000} \cdot \frac{1}{1000} = \frac{1}{1000000}$$ is smaller still, but not
zero. The reason is that multiplying by a non-zero number can always be undone, by multiplying by
its inverse. If multiplying $$b$$ by a non-zero number gave $$0$$, undoing it would turn $$0$$ back
into $$b$$. But undoing it means multiplying $$0$$ by the inverse, and anything times $$0$$ is
$$0$$. So $$b$$ must have been $$0$$ all along.

Inverses also give us the most useful fact about products that equal zero.

:::theorem[Products equal to zero]{#zero-product}

Let $$a$$ and $$b$$ be rational numbers. If $$ab = 0$$, then $$a = 0$$ or $$b = 0$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Field_has_no_Proper_Zero_Divisors#Proof_2"}

There are two cases. Either $$a = 0$$, in which case we are done, or $$a \neq 0$$. In the second
case we show that $$b$$ must be $$0$$.

Since $$a \neq 0$$, it has an inverse $$a^{-1}$$, as in the previous proof (:ref{#inverse-exists}).
Multiplying both sides of $$ab = 0$$ by it gives

```math
a^{-1}(ab) = a^{-1} \cdot 0 = 0
```

where the right-hand side is $$0$$ because anything times $$0$$ is $$0$$ (:ref{#zero-times}). On the
left, regrouping gives $$a^{-1}(ab) = (a^{-1}a)b = 1 \cdot b = b$$, just as in the previous proof.
So $$b = 0$$.

:::

This is how equations like $$(x - 3)(2x + 1) = 0$$ are solved. The product is zero, so one of the
factors must be: either $$x - 3 = 0$$, giving $$x = 3$$, or $$2x + 1 = 0$$, giving
$$x = -\frac{1}{2}$$.

## Dividing by a rational number

Dividing asks how many times one number fits into another. How many halves fit into $$3$$? Each
whole holds $$2$$ halves, so $$3$$ holds $$3 \cdot 2 = 6$$ of them. Dividing by $$\frac{1}{2}$$ gave
the same answer as multiplying by $$2$$, its flip. That is no accident. Dividing by $$b$$ should
undo multiplying by $$b$$, and that is exactly what multiplying by $$b^{-1}$$ does.

:::definition[Quotient]{#quotient}

For rational numbers $$a$$ and $$b$$ with $$b \neq 0$$, the quotient $$a$$ divided by $$b$$ is

```math
\frac{a}{b} = a b^{-1}
```

:::

This agrees with the fractions we already have: for integers $$m$$ and $$n$$,
$$m n^{-1} = \frac{m}{1} \cdot \frac{1}{n} = \frac{m}{n}$$. It also explains the rule "to divide by
a fraction, multiply by its flip". For example,

```math
\frac{2/3}{4/5} = \frac{2}{3} \cdot \frac{5}{4} = \frac{10}{12} = \frac{5}{6}
```

and a fraction with fractions inside can be simplified the same way:

```math
\frac{1 + \frac{1}{2}}{1 - \frac{1}{4}} = \frac{3/2}{3/4} = \frac{3}{2} \cdot \frac{4}{3} = 2
```

Earlier, cross-multiplying was simply the meaning of equality for fractions of integers. Now it can
be proved, and for quotients of any rational numbers.

:::theorem[Cross-multiplying quotients]{#cross-multiplying-rational}

Let $$a$$, $$b$$, $$c$$, $$d$$ be rational numbers with $$b \neq 0$$ and $$d \neq 0$$. Then

```math
\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d} \qquad \text{exactly when} \qquad ad = bc
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-multiplication#Procedure"}

"Exactly when" means two things to prove: each statement leads to the other
(:ref{#basic-proofs/exactly-when}). Both directions work by multiplying both sides by the same
number, chosen so that each $$b$$ meets a $$b^{-1}$$ and each $$d$$ meets a $$d^{-1}$$, which cancel
to $$1$$. The inverses $$b^{-1}$$ and $$d^{-1}$$ exist because $$b$$ and $$d$$ are not $$0$$.

Suppose first that $$\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d}$$, which by :ref{#quotient} says
$$ab^{-1} = cd^{-1}$$. Multiplying both sides by $$bd$$, the $$b^{-1}$$ on the left meets the $$b$$,
and the $$d^{-1}$$ on the right meets the $$d$$:

```math
ab^{-1} \cdot bd = a \cdot (b^{-1}b) \cdot d = ad,
\qquad
cd^{-1} \cdot bd = c \cdot (d^{-1}d) \cdot b = cb
```

using that the order and grouping of a product don't matter. So $$ad = bc$$.

Now suppose $$ad = bc$$. This time we multiply both sides by $$b^{-1}d^{-1}$$ to remove the $$b$$
and the $$d$$:

```math
ad \cdot b^{-1}d^{-1} = a b^{-1} \cdot (d d^{-1}) = ab^{-1},
\qquad
bc \cdot b^{-1}d^{-1} = (b b^{-1}) \cdot c d^{-1} = cd^{-1}
```

So $$ab^{-1} = cd^{-1}$$, which is $$\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d}$$.

For example, $$\frac{1/2}{3} = \frac{1}{6}$$, and cross-multiplying checks it:
$$\frac{1}{2} \cdot 6 = 3 = 3 \cdot 1$$.

:::

## Solving equations

Inverses mean that equations like $$3x = 8$$ now have an answer. Multiplying both sides by
$$3^{-1} = \frac{1}{3}$$, which is what dividing both sides by $$3$$ means, gives
$$x = \frac{8}{3}$$. Combined with :ref{#rearranging}, this solves any equation in which $$x$$
appears only multiplied by numbers.

:::example

Solve $$3(x + 4) = 20$$. Expanding the bracket gives $$3x + 12 = 20$$. Subtracting $$12$$ from both
sides gives $$3x = 8$$, and dividing by $$3$$ gives $$x = \frac{8}{3}$$.

:::

:::example

Solve $$\frac{x}{3} + 1 = \frac{x}{2}$$. Fractions in an equation are easiest to clear first, by
multiplying both sides by a common denominator. Multiplying by $$6$$ gives $$2x + 6 = 3x$$, and
subtracting $$2x$$ from both sides gives $$x = 6$$. Check: $$\frac{6}{3} + 1 = 3$$ and
$$\frac{6}{2} = 3$$.

:::

When the unknown appears in a denominator, cross-multiplying turns the equation into one without
fractions.

:::example

Solve $$\frac{2x + 3}{x - 1} = 5$$. The left side only makes sense when $$x \neq 1$$.
Cross-multiplying (with $$5 = \frac{5}{1}$$) gives $$2x + 3 = 5(x - 1) = 5x - 5$$. Rearranging,
$$3 + 5 = 5x - 2x$$, so $$3x = 8$$ and $$x = \frac{8}{3}$$. Check: $$2x + 3 = \frac{25}{3}$$ and
$$x - 1 = \frac{5}{3}$$, and $$\frac{25/3}{5/3} = 5$$.

:::

## Cancelling

If $$3$$ times one number equals $$3$$ times another, the two numbers must be the same, because
multiplying by $$3$$ can be undone by multiplying by $$\frac{1}{3}$$. The same goes for any non-zero
factor. Zero is the exception: multiplying by $$0$$ turns every number into $$0$$, so it wipes out
any difference and can't be undone.

Inverses also let us cancel a common factor from both sides of an equation.

:::proposition[Cancelling a non-zero factor]{#cancel-product}

Let $$a$$, $$b$$, $$c$$ be rational numbers with $$a \neq 0$$. If $$ab = ac$$, then $$b = c$$. Also,
if $$c \neq 0$$ too,

```math
\frac{ab}{ac} = \frac{b}{c}
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Cancellation_Law_for_Field_Product"}

**First part.** Since $$a \neq 0$$, it has an inverse $$a^{-1}$$ (:ref{#inverse-exists}). Multiply
both sides of $$ab = ac$$ by it, and regroup:

```math
a^{-1}(ab) = (a^{-1}a)b = 1 \cdot b = b,
\qquad
a^{-1}(ac) = (a^{-1}a)c = 1 \cdot c = c
```

The two left-hand sides are equal, because they come from multiplying equal numbers by the same
$$a^{-1}$$. So $$b = c$$.

**Second part.** First, $$\frac{ab}{ac}$$ makes sense: $$a$$ and $$c$$ are both non-zero, so their
product $$ac$$ is not $$0$$ by :ref{#zero-product}. Now cross-multiply
(:ref{#cross-multiplying-rational}). The two quotients are equal exactly when $$(ab)c = (ac)b$$, and
both sides are the product of $$a$$, $$b$$ and $$c$$ in a different order, so they are equal.

:::

The condition $$a \neq 0$$ cannot be dropped: $$0 \cdot 2 = 0 \cdot 5$$, but $$2 \neq 5$$.

As :ref{#no-cancel} showed, cancelling can fail in other settings too: for congruences it can fail
even when the factor is not zero.

## Adding quotients

A quotient like $$\frac{1/2}{3}$$ is a fraction whose top and bottom happen to be fractions
themselves. The idea behind adding fractions still applies: rewrite both so they share a
denominator, then add the tops. So quotients of rational numbers add in exactly the same way as
fractions of integers.

:::proposition[Adding quotients]{#adding-quotients}

Let $$a$$, $$b$$, $$c$$, $$d$$ be rational numbers with $$b \neq 0$$ and $$d \neq 0$$. Then

```math
\frac{a}{b} + \frac{c}{b} = \frac{a + c}{b}
\qquad \text{and} \qquad
\frac{a}{b} + \frac{c}{d} = \frac{ad + bc}{bd}
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraction#Adding_unlike_quantities"}

**Same denominator.** By :ref{#quotient}, both quotients contain the factor $$b^{-1}$$, so
distributivity lets us take it out:

```math
\frac{a}{b} + \frac{c}{b} = ab^{-1} + cb^{-1} = (a + c)b^{-1} = \frac{a + c}{b}
```

**Different denominators.** The plan is to rewrite both quotients with the same denominator $$bd$$,
and then use the first formula. Note that $$bd \neq 0$$ by :ref{#zero-product}, since $$b$$ and
$$d$$ are both non-zero.

The second part of :ref{#cancel-product} says that a non-zero common factor of the top and bottom
can be cancelled. Read backwards, multiplying the top and bottom by the same non-zero number doesn't
change a quotient. Multiplying $$\frac{a}{b}$$ through by $$d$$, and $$\frac{c}{d}$$ through by
$$b$$, gives

```math
\frac{a}{b} = \frac{da}{db} = \frac{ad}{bd} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \frac{c}{d} = \frac{bc}{bd}
```

Now both have the denominator $$bd$$, and the first formula adds them to give
$$\frac{ad + bc}{bd}$$.

:::

These rules work for expressions with letters in them too, as long as the denominators are not zero.

:::remark[Hidden conditions]

An expression like $$\frac{1}{x - 2}$$ only makes sense when $$x \neq 2$$. From here on, whenever a
letter appears in a denominator, we assume without saying so that it takes only values that keep
every denominator non-zero.

:::

:::example

Show that $$\frac{1}{x} - \frac{1}{x + 1} = \frac{1}{x(x + 1)}$$. Using the formula for adding
quotients,

```math
\frac{1}{x} - \frac{1}{x + 1} = \frac{1 \cdot (x + 1) - x \cdot 1}{x(x + 1)}
= \frac{x + 1 - x}{x(x + 1)} = \frac{1}{x(x + 1)}
```

For instance, with $$x = 3$$ this says $$\frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{12}$$.

:::

Cross-multiplying is often the quickest way to prove that two quotients are equal, because it turns
the question into one about products, which can simply be expanded.

:::proposition[Sum of consecutive powers]{#sum-of-powers}

For a rational number $$x \neq 1$$ and a positive integer $$n$$,

```math
\frac{x^n - 1}{x - 1} = x^{n - 1} + x^{n - 2} + \cdots + x + 1
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://www.mathsisfun.com/algebra/sequences-sums-geometric.html"}

Since $$x \neq 1$$, the denominator $$x - 1$$ is not $$0$$, so the left side makes sense. The right
side is the same as $$\frac{x^{n-1} + \cdots + x + 1}{1}$$, so by :ref{#cross-multiplying-rational}
it is enough to show that

```math
(x - 1)(x^{n - 1} + x^{n - 2} + \cdots + x + 1) = x^n - 1
```

To see the idea, take $$n = 3$$ first:

```math
(x - 1)(x^2 + x + 1) = (x^3 + x^2 + x) - (x^2 + x + 1) = x^3 - 1
```

The $$x^2$$ and $$x$$ appear once with each sign and cancel. In general, expanding the left side,
multiplying the bracket by $$x$$ and by $$-1$$ in turn, gives two rows:

```math
\begin{array}{ccccccccccc}
x^n & + & x^{n - 1} & + & \cdots & + & x^2 & + & x & & \\
    & - & x^{n - 1} & - & \cdots & - & x^2 & - & x & - & 1
\end{array}
```

Every power from $$x^{n-1}$$ down to $$x$$ appears once with a plus sign and once with a minus sign,
so they all cancel. Only the first and last terms survive, leaving $$x^n - 1$$.

:::

For example, with $$n = 3$$ this says $$\frac{x^3 - 1}{x - 1} = x^2 + x + 1$$, and with $$x = 2$$ it
says $$1 + 2 + 4 + 8 = \frac{2^4 - 1}{2 - 1} = 15$$.

## Using quotients in problems

Many formulas in science relate three quantities by a product, and quotients let us solve for any of
them. For something moving at a steady speed $$s$$, the distance $$d$$ covered in time $$t$$ is

```math
d = st, \qquad \text{so} \qquad s = \frac{d}{t} \quad \text{and} \quad t = \frac{d}{s}
```

:::example[Speed]

A cyclist rides $$18$$ km in $$\frac{3}{4}$$ of an hour. Their speed is

```math
s = \frac{18}{3/4} = 18 \cdot \frac{4}{3} = 24 \text{ km per hour}
```

:::

:::example[A journey in two parts]

A driver covers $$150$$ km in $$3$$ hours, partly on a motorway at $$70$$ km per hour and partly
through towns at $$30$$ km per hour. How long were they in towns?

Let $$x$$ be the time in towns, in hours. Then the time on the motorway is $$3 - x$$. The distances
are $$30x$$ and $$70(3 - x)$$, and together they make $$150$$:

```math
30x + 70(3 - x) = 150
```

Expanding gives $$30x + 210 - 70x = 150$$, so $$210 - 150 = 40x$$, that is $$40x = 60$$ and
$$x = \frac{60}{40} = \frac{3}{2}$$. The driver spent an hour and a half in towns. Check: $$45$$ km
in towns plus $$70 \cdot \frac{3}{2} = 105$$ km on the motorway is $$150$$ km.

:::

:::example[Mixtures]

A $$10$$ litre jug holds a drink that is $$20\%$$ juice. How much should be poured out and replaced
with pure juice to make it $$50\%$$ juice?

Let $$x$$ be the number of litres replaced. After pouring out $$x$$ litres, $$10 - x$$ litres are
left, of which $$\frac{20}{100} = \frac{1}{5}$$ is juice. Adding $$x$$ litres of juice must give
$$\frac{1}{2}$$ of $$10$$, which is $$5$$ litres of juice:

```math
\frac{1}{5}(10 - x) + x = 5
```

Expanding, $$2 - \frac{1}{5}x + x = 5$$, so $$\frac{4}{5}x = 3$$. Multiplying both sides by
$$\frac{5}{4}$$, the inverse of $$\frac{4}{5}$$, gives $$x = \frac{15}{4}$$. So
$$\frac{15}{4} = 3.75$$ litres should be replaced.

:::
