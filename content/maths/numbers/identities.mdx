title: Algebraic identities

Distributivity says how to multiply out a bracket. A handful of products come up so often that it is
worth knowing their expansions by heart: they save work, they make mental arithmetic easier, and
read backwards they let us **factorise**, turning a sum into a product. Every one of them is proved
by multiplying out, so nothing here is new; it is a toolkit built from the rules on the
[Multiplication](./multiplication.mdx) page. They hold for any integers, and later for any rational
numbers too.

## Squares

Multiplying out a bracket means every part of the first bracket meets every part of the second. When
the two brackets are the same, as in $$(a + b)^2$$, each pair of different parts meets twice: once
as $$a$$ times $$b$$, and once as $$b$$ times $$a$$. That is why the square of a sum has a middle
term on top of the two squares. For example, $$(3 + 2)^2 = 25$$, while $$3^2 + 2^2$$ is only $$13$$;
the missing $$12$$ is two lots of $$3 \cdot 2$$.

In the difference of squares, the two brackets differ by one sign, so the two middle terms come out
with opposite signs and cancel. Only the squares are left: $$7 \cdot 3 = (5 + 2)(5 - 2) = 25 - 4$$.

:::proposition[Formulas for squares]{#square-formulas}

For any $$a$$ and $$b$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
(a + b)^2 &= a^2 + 2ab + b^2 &&\qquad \text{square of a sum} \\
(a - b)^2 &= a^2 - 2ab + b^2 &&\qquad \text{square of a difference} \\
(a + b)(a - b) &= a^2 - b^2 &&\qquad \text{difference of squares}
\end{aligned}
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://www.mathsisfun.com/algebra/special-binomial-products.html"}

Each one comes from distributivity (:ref{#multiplication-rules}), multiplying every part of the
first bracket by every part of the second:

```math
\begin{aligned}
(a + b)^2 &= (a + b)(a + b) = a(a + b) + b(a + b) = a^2 + ab + ba + b^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2 \\
(a - b)^2 &= (a - b)(a - b) = a(a - b) - b(a - b) = a^2 - ab - ba + b^2 = a^2 - 2ab + b^2 \\
(a + b)(a - b) &= a(a - b) + b(a - b) = a^2 - ab + ba - b^2 = a^2 - b^2
\end{aligned}
```

In each line $$ab$$ and $$ba$$ are equal, since order doesn't matter, so they either add up or
cancel.

:::

The first one can be seen as a square of side $$a + b$$ cut into four pieces: one $$a^2$$, one
$$b^2$$, and **two** rectangles of area $$ab$$. That is where the $$2ab$$ comes from, and why
$$(a + b)^2$$ is not just $$a^2 + b^2$$.

:::area-model{row-sizes="3 2" col-sizes="3 2"}

|     | a   | b   |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a   | a²  | ab  |
| b   | ab  | b²  |

:::

Some examples:

```math
\begin{aligned}
(x + 4)^2 &= x^2 + 2 \cdot 4x + 4^2 = x^2 + 8x + 16 \\
(5 - 2y)^2 &= 5^2 - 2 \cdot 5 \cdot 2y + (2y)^2 = 25 - 20y + 4y^2 \\
(3a + 1)(3a - 1) &= (3a)^2 - 1^2 = 9a^2 - 1
\end{aligned}
```

They are also handy for mental arithmetic: $$21^2 = (20 + 1)^2 = 400 + 40 + 1 = 441$$, and
$$19 \cdot 21 = (20 - 1)(20 + 1) = 400 - 1 = 399$$.

The same method works for a sum of three terms. Each term meets every other term twice, once from
each bracket, giving

```math
(a + b + c)^2 = a^2 + b^2 + c^2 + 2ab + 2bc + 2ca
```

For example, $$(x + y + 1)^2 = x^2 + y^2 + 1 + 2xy + 2y + 2x$$.

## Cubes

A cube is just one more multiplication. Multiplying the square of a sum by the sum once more makes
the middle terms pile up in the same way, so the cube of a sum has four terms instead of three, with
the numbers in front growing to $$3$$. For example, $$(2 + 1)^3 = 27$$, which splits as
$$8 + 12 + 6 + 1$$.

The difference and sum of cubes work like the difference of squares, read backwards. They say a cube
minus a cube, or plus a cube, can always be split into two factors. The reason is the same
cancelling trick: when the right side is multiplied out, all the middle terms cancel in pairs and
only the two cubes are left.

:::proposition[Formulas for cubes]{#cube-formulas}

For any $$a$$ and $$b$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
(a + b)^3 &= a^3 + 3a^2 b + 3ab^2 + b^3 &&\qquad \text{cube of a sum} \\
(a - b)^3 &= a^3 - 3a^2 b + 3ab^2 - b^3 &&\qquad \text{cube of a difference} \\
a^3 - b^3 &= (a - b)(a^2 + ab + b^2) &&\qquad \text{difference of cubes} \\
a^3 + b^3 &= (a + b)(a^2 - ab + b^2) &&\qquad \text{sum of cubes}
\end{aligned}
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sum_of_two_cubes#Proof"}

The cube of a sum is the square of the sum (:ref{#square-formulas}), multiplied once more by
$$a + b$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
(a + b)^3 &= (a^2 + 2ab + b^2)(a + b) \\
&= a^3 + a^2 b + 2a^2 b + 2ab^2 + ab^2 + b^3 \\
&= a^3 + 3a^2 b + 3ab^2 + b^3
\end{aligned}
```

The cube of a difference needs no new work. The cube of a sum holds for **any** $$b$$, so we may use
it with $$-b$$ in place of $$b$$, since $$a + (-b)$$ is just $$a - b$$. Since $$(-b)^2 = b^2$$ but
$$(-b)^3 = -b^3$$, the terms with an odd power of $$b$$ change sign and the others stay the same:

```math
(a - b)^3 = a^3 + 3a^2 (-b) + 3a(-b)^2 + (-b)^3 = a^3 - 3a^2 b + 3ab^2 - b^3
```

For the difference of cubes, expand the right side. Multiplying by $$a$$ and by $$-b$$ gives two
rows whose middle terms cancel, leaving only $$a^3$$ and $$-b^3$$:

```math
\begin{array}{rcrrrr}
a(a^2 + ab + b^2) &=& a^3 & {} + a^2 b & {} + ab^2 & \\
-b(a^2 + ab + b^2) &=& & {} - a^2 b & {} - ab^2 & {} - b^3 \\
\hline
(a - b)(a^2 + ab + b^2) &=& a^3 & & & {} - b^3
\end{array}
```

The sum of cubes then follows by putting $$-b$$ in place of $$b$$ again: the left side becomes
$$a^3 - (-b)^3 = a^3 + b^3$$, and the right side becomes $$(a + b)(a^2 - ab + b^2)$$.

:::

A few things help to remember these:

- In the cube of a sum the numbers in front are $$1, 3, 3, 1$$, just as they are $$1, 2, 1$$ in the
  square of a sum. Each term has total power $$3$$: $$a^3$$, then $$a^2 b$$, then $$ab^2$$, then
  $$b^3$$. These numbers are rows of Pascal's triangle, and the [Factorials](./factorials.mdx) page
  explains why and gives the pattern for every power.
- In the difference and sum of cubes, the short bracket has the same sign as the left side, and the
  middle term of the long bracket has the opposite sign.
- A _sum_ of squares $$a^2 + b^2$$ has no formula like these. Only the difference of squares
  factors.

Some examples:

```math
\begin{aligned}
(x + 2)^3 &= x^3 + 3 \cdot 2x^2 + 3 \cdot 4x + 8 = x^3 + 6x^2 + 12x + 8 \\
x^3 - 8 &= x^3 - 2^3 = (x - 2)(x^2 + 2x + 4) \\
27y^3 + 1 &= (3y)^3 + 1^3 = (3y + 1)(9y^2 - 3y + 1)
\end{aligned}
```

They help with arithmetic too:

```math
\begin{aligned}
9^3 &= (10 - 1)^3 = 1000 - 300 + 30 - 1 = 729 \\
1001 &= 10^3 + 1^3 = (10 + 1)(100 - 10 + 1) = 11 \cdot 91
\end{aligned}
```

## Expanding longer products

A product of three or more brackets is expanded two at a time. For example, to expand
$$(x + 3)(x - 1)(2x + 1)$$, first multiply out the first two brackets:

```math
(x + 3)(x - 1) = x^2 - x + 3x - 3 = x^2 + 2x - 3
```

Then multiply that result by the last bracket, and collect terms with the same power of $$x$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
(x^2 + 2x - 3)(2x + 1) &= (x^2 + 2x - 3) \cdot 2x + (x^2 + 2x - 3) \cdot 1 \\
&= 2x^3 + 4x^2 - 6x + x^2 + 2x - 3 \\
&= 2x^3 + 5x^2 - 4x - 3
\end{aligned}
```

A quick way to check an expansion is to try a value. With $$x = 2$$, the original is
$$5 \cdot 1 \cdot 5 = 25$$, and the expansion gives $$16 + 20 - 8 - 3 = 25$$.

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on algebraic identities, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-identities-expand}

Expand $$(3 - 4t)^2$$ and $$(x^2 + 1)(x^2 - 1)$$.

:::solution

The first is the square of a difference (:ref{#square-formulas}), with $$a = 3$$ and $$b = 4t$$:

```math
(3 - 4t)^2 = 3^2 - 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 4t + (4t)^2 = 9 - 24t + 16t^2
```

The second is a difference of squares, with $$a = x^2$$ and $$b = 1$$:

```math
(x^2 + 1)(x^2 - 1) = (x^2)^2 - 1^2 = x^4 - 1
```

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-identities-factorise}

Factorise each one by reading a formula for squares backwards:

1. $$x^2 - 49$$
2. $$a^2 + 10a + 25$$
3. $$9y^2 - 12y + 4$$

:::solution

1. This is a difference of squares, $$x^2 - 7^2$$, so it is $$(x - 7)(x + 7)$$.
2. The first and last terms are $$a^2$$ and $$5^2$$, and the middle term is $$2 \cdot a \cdot 5$$.
   So it is the square of a sum: $$(a + 5)^2$$.
3. The first and last terms are $$(3y)^2$$ and $$2^2$$, and the middle term is
   $$-2 \cdot 3y \cdot 2 = -12y$$. So it is the square of a difference: $$(3y - 2)^2$$.

Expanding each answer gives back the starting expression, which is a good check.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-identities-mental}

Use the formulas for squares to work out $$45^2$$ and $$103 \cdot 97$$ in your head.

:::solution

For $$45^2$$, use the square of a sum with $$45 = 40 + 5$$:

```math
45^2 = 40^2 + 2 \cdot 40 \cdot 5 + 5^2 = 1600 + 400 + 25 = 2025
```

For $$103 \cdot 97$$, both numbers are $$3$$ away from $$100$$, so use the difference of squares:

```math
103 \cdot 97 = (100 + 3)(100 - 3) = 100^2 - 3^2 = 10000 - 9 = 9991
```

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-identities-cubes}

Expand $$(x - 3)^3$$, and factorise $$y^3 + 64$$ and $$8a^3 - 1$$.

:::solution

By the cube of a difference (:ref{#cube-formulas}) with $$b = 3$$:

```math
(x - 3)^3 = x^3 - 3 \cdot 3x^2 + 3 \cdot 9x - 27 = x^3 - 9x^2 + 27x - 27
```

Since $$64 = 4^3$$, the sum of cubes gives $$y^3 + 64 = (y + 4)(y^2 - 4y + 16)$$.

Since $$8a^3 = (2a)^3$$ and $$1 = 1^3$$, the difference of cubes gives

```math
8a^3 - 1 = (2a - 1)(4a^2 + 2a + 1)
```

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-identities-three-terms}

Expand $$(a + b - c)^2$$, and check your answer with $$a = 1$$, $$b = 2$$ and $$c = 3$$.

:::solution

Use the square of a sum of three terms, with $$-c$$ as the third term. Each term is squared, and
each pair of different terms appears twice:

```math
(a + b - c)^2 = a^2 + b^2 + c^2 + 2ab - 2bc - 2ca
```

The terms with $$c$$ in them once change sign, because $$b(-c) = -bc$$ and $$(-c)a = -ca$$, while
$$(-c)^2 = c^2$$.

Check: the left side is $$(1 + 2 - 3)^2 = 0$$, and the right side is $$1 + 4 + 9 + 4 - 12 - 6 = 0$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-identities-sum-of-squares-trap}

Find integers $$a$$ and $$b$$ with $$(a + b)^2 \neq a^2 + b^2$$. Then show that the two are equal
exactly when $$a = 0$$ or $$b = 0$$. (This uses :ref{#zero-product} from the
[Inverses](./inverses.mdx) page.)

:::solution

For $$a = b = 1$$, $$(1 + 1)^2 = 4$$ but $$1^2 + 1^2 = 2$$.

By :ref{#square-formulas}, $$(a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$$. So the two are equal exactly when the
extra term is zero, that is, when $$2ab = 0$$. A product is zero exactly when one of its factors is
(:ref{#zero-product}), and $$2$$ isn't zero, so this happens exactly when $$a = 0$$ or $$b = 0$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-identities-odd-differences}

Show that every odd number $$2k + 1$$ is the difference of two consecutive squares. Write $$15$$ and
$$39$$ this way.

:::solution

By the square of a sum, $$(k + 1)^2 = k^2 + 2k + 1$$. Subtracting $$k^2$$ leaves

```math
(k + 1)^2 - k^2 = 2k + 1
```

For $$15 = 2 \cdot 7 + 1$$, take $$k = 7$$: $$8^2 - 7^2 = 64 - 49 = 15$$. For
$$39 = 2 \cdot 19 + 1$$, take $$k = 19$$: $$20^2 - 19^2 = 400 - 361 = 39$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-identities-check-by-value}

Expand $$(x - 1)(x + 2)(x - 3)$$, and check your answer by putting $$x = 2$$ into both sides.

:::solution

Multiply out the first two brackets, then multiply the result by the last one:

```math
\begin{aligned}
(x - 1)(x + 2) &= x^2 + 2x - x - 2 = x^2 + x - 2 \\
(x^2 + x - 2)(x - 3) &= x^3 - 3x^2 + x^2 - 3x - 2x + 6 = x^3 - 2x^2 - 5x + 6
\end{aligned}
```

Check with $$x = 2$$: the product is $$1 \cdot 4 \cdot (-1) = -4$$, and the expansion gives
$$8 - 8 - 10 + 6 = -4$$.

:::
