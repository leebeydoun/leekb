---
title: Rational numbers
description: Fractions, when they are equal, and how to add and multiply them.
---

Integers are good for counting whole things, but not for sharing them. Splitting $$3$$ pizzas
between $$4$$ people gives each person a piece of pizza that is not a whole number. Fractions fill
this gap. This page defines them, decides when two fractions are the same number, and sets up
adding, multiplying and comparing them. Dividing gets its own page next, because it needs one more
idea.

## Fractions

:::definition[Rational numbers]{#rational}

A **rational number** is a number written as a fraction of two integers,

```math
\frac{m}{n}, \qquad \text{also written } m/n,
```

where $$m$$ and $$n$$ are integers and $$n \neq 0$$. The top number $$m$$ is the **numerator** and
the bottom number $$n$$ is the **denominator**.

:::

For example, $$\frac{3}{4}$$, $$\frac{-5}{2}$$ and $$\frac{7}{3}$$ are rational numbers. The
fraction $$\frac{m}{n}$$ answers the question "what number, multiplied by $$n$$, gives $$m$$?" That
is why the denominator can never be $$0$$: no number multiplied by $$0$$ gives, say, $$5$$, so
$$\frac{5}{0}$$ has no meaning.

Rational numbers have places on the number line between the integers. To find $$\frac{2}{3}$$, cut
the stretch from $$0$$ to $$1$$ into $$3$$ equal pieces and count $$2$$ of them from $$0$$. To find
$$\frac{3}{2}$$, cut each whole step into $$2$$ pieces and count $$3$$ of them:

::number-line{from=0 to=2 divisions=6 points="1/2, 2/3, 3/2" labelled}

As with the integers, a negative rational number sits on the other side of $$0$$, the same distance
away:

::number-line{from=-2 to=2 divisions=4 points="-3/2, -3/4, 3/4, 3/2" labelled}

## When are two fractions equal?

The same rational number can be written as a fraction in many ways. Cutting the stretch from $$0$$
to $$1$$ into $$2$$ pieces and taking $$1$$ lands on the same point as cutting it into $$4$$ pieces
and taking $$2$$, or into $$6$$ pieces and taking $$3$$:

::number-line{from=0 to=1 divisions=4 points="1/2" labels="1/2: 1/2 = 2/4"}

::number-line{from=0 to=1 divisions=6 points="1/2" labels="1/2: 1/2 = 3/6"}

We need a test that decides when two fractions name the same number, without drawing pictures. If
$$\frac{m}{n} = \frac{r}{s}$$, then multiplying both by $$ns$$ should give the same answer, and
$$\frac{m}{n} \cdot ns = ms$$ while $$\frac{r}{s} \cdot ns = rn$$. We turn this into the rule that
says what equality of fractions means.

:::definition[Equal fractions]{#cross-multiplying}

For integers $$m$$, $$n$$, $$r$$, $$s$$ with $$n \neq 0$$ and $$s \neq 0$$,

```math
\frac{m}{n} = \frac{r}{s} \qquad \text{exactly when} \qquad ms = rn
```

Checking this is called **cross-multiplying**, because each numerator is multiplied by the other
fraction's denominator.

:::

For now this rule is simply what "equal" means for fractions. Once division is set up properly on
the [Inverses and division](./inverses.mdx) page, :ref{#cross-multiplying-rational} shows that it is
not a choice at all: no other rule would be consistent with multiplying.

For example:

- $$\frac{2}{3} = \frac{10}{15}$$, because $$2 \cdot 15 = 30$$ and $$10 \cdot 3 = 30$$.
- $$\frac{6}{8} = \frac{9}{12}$$, because both $$6 \cdot 12$$ and $$9 \cdot 8$$ are $$72$$.
- $$\frac{-3}{4} = \frac{6}{-8}$$, because $$(-3)(-8) = 24$$ and $$6 \cdot 4 = 24$$.
- $$\frac{3}{5} \neq \frac{5}{8}$$, because $$3 \cdot 8 = 24$$ but $$5 \cdot 5 = 25$$.

An integer $$m$$ is treated as the same thing as the fraction $$\frac{m}{1}$$, so
$$4 = \frac{4}{1}$$ and $$-7 = \frac{-7}{1}$$. This way every integer is also a rational number.
Cross-multiplying against an integer is often a quick way to solve for an unknown:
$$\frac{x}{5} = 3$$ means $$\frac{x}{5} = \frac{3}{1}$$, so $$x \cdot 1 = 3 \cdot 5$$ and
$$x = 15$$.

## Cancelling and minus signs

The first consequence of cross-multiplying is that a common factor of the numerator and denominator
can be cancelled.

:::proposition[Cancelling a common factor]{#fraction-cancel}

Let $$a$$, $$m$$, $$n$$ be integers with $$a \neq 0$$ and $$n \neq 0$$. Then

```math
\frac{am}{an} = \frac{m}{n}
```

:::

:::proof

By :ref{#cross-multiplying}, the two fractions are equal exactly when

```math
(am)n = m(an)
```

So all we need to check is that these two products of integers are the same. Both are the product of
the three integers $$a$$, $$m$$ and $$n$$, just written in a different order and grouping. Since the
order and grouping of a product don't matter, they are equal. The condition $$a \neq 0$$ is needed
so that $$an$$ is not $$0$$, which keeps the left-hand side a genuine fraction.

:::

For example, $$\frac{18}{24} = \frac{6 \cdot 3}{6 \cdot 4} = \frac{3}{4}$$. Read backwards, the same
rule lets us multiply the top and bottom by the same number, which is how $$\frac{1}{2}$$ becomes
$$\frac{2}{4}$$ and $$\frac{3}{6}$$.

:::proposition[Minus signs in fractions]{#fraction-signs}

For integers $$m$$ and $$n$$ with $$n \neq 0$$,

```math
\frac{-m}{n} = \frac{m}{-n} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \frac{-m}{-n} = \frac{m}{n}
```

:::

:::proof

Both are checked by cross-multiplying, using the rules for minus signs in products of integers.

For the first, we need $$(-m)(-n) = mn$$, which is true because two minus signs in a product cancel.

For the second, we need $$(-m)n = m(-n)$$. Both sides equal $$-(mn)$$, because a minus sign can be
moved to either factor of a product.

:::

So a single minus sign can sit on the top, on the bottom, or in front of the whole fraction, and two
minus signs cancel. We usually move it to the top or the front: $$\frac{5}{-2} = \frac{-5}{2}$$.

## Positive and negative fractions

With the minus sign rules in hand, every fraction can be written with a positive denominator, and
then the sign of the numerator decides which side of $$0$$ it is on.

:::definition[Positive and negative rational numbers]{#positive-rational}

A rational number is **positive** if it can be written as $$\frac{m}{n}$$ with $$m$$ and $$n$$ both
positive integers, and **negative** if it can be written as $$-\frac{m}{n}$$ with $$m$$ and $$n$$
positive.

:::

So $$\frac{-3}{-4} = \frac{3}{4}$$ is positive and $$\frac{5}{-2} = -\frac{5}{2}$$ is negative.
Every rational number is positive, negative or $$0$$.

## Lowest form

Cancelling makes a fraction smaller and simpler, and it is natural to keep going until nothing more
can be cancelled.

:::definition[Lowest form]{#lowest-form}

A positive rational number is in **lowest form** when it is written as $$\frac{r}{s}$$ with $$r$$
and $$s$$ positive integers whose only positive common divisor is $$1$$ (:ref{#gcd}).

:::

For example, $$\frac{36}{60}$$ has the common divisor $$12$$, and
$$\frac{36}{60} = \frac{12 \cdot 3}{12 \cdot 5} = \frac{3}{5}$$, which is in lowest form since $$3$$
and $$5$$ have no common divisor other than $$1$$.

:::theorem[Every fraction has a lowest form]{#lowest-form-exists}

Every positive rational number can be written as a fraction in lowest form.

:::

:::proof

The plan is to cancel the biggest possible factor in one go, and then check that nothing more can be
cancelled.

Write the number as $$\frac{m}{n}$$ with $$m$$ and $$n$$ positive integers, and let $$d$$ be their
greatest common divisor (:ref{#gcd}). Writing $$m = dr$$ and $$n = ds$$ with $$r$$ and $$s$$
positive integers, :ref{#fraction-cancel} gives

```math
\frac{m}{n} = \frac{dr}{ds} = \frac{r}{s}
```

It remains to show that $$\frac{r}{s}$$ is in lowest form, meaning $$r$$ and $$s$$ have no common
divisor bigger than $$1$$. We argue by contradiction (:ref{#contradiction}): we suppose they do, and
show this leads to something impossible.

So suppose $$e > 1$$ divides both $$r$$ and $$s$$, say $$r = ex$$ and $$s = ey$$. Substituting,

```math
m = dr = (de)x \qquad \text{and} \qquad n = ds = (de)y
```

so $$de$$ is a common divisor of $$m$$ and $$n$$. But $$e > 1$$ and $$d > 0$$, so $$de > d$$ by
:ref{#order-multiply}. That contradicts our choice of $$d$$ as the greatest common divisor. So no
such $$e$$ exists, and $$\frac{r}{s}$$ is in lowest form.

:::

:::remark[Not both even]{#not-both-even}

A fraction in lowest form never has both its numerator and denominator even, since then $$2$$ would
be a common divisor. So any positive rational number can be written as $$\frac{m}{n}$$ with at least
one of $$m$$ and $$n$$ odd. This fact is the key to the proof on the
[Irrational numbers](./irrational-numbers.mdx) page that no fraction squares to $$2$$.

:::

## Adding fractions

Adding fractions with the same denominator is like adding amounts of the same unit: $$2$$ sevenths
and $$3$$ sevenths make $$5$$ sevenths, so $$\frac{2}{7} + \frac{3}{7} = \frac{5}{7}$$. When the
denominators are different, we first rewrite both fractions so they share one. Multiplying the top
and bottom of each by the other's denominator does this:

```math
\frac{m}{n} = \frac{ms}{ns} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \frac{r}{s} = \frac{rn}{ns}
```

Now both are counted in pieces of size $$\frac{1}{ns}$$, and we can add the numerators.

:::definition[Adding fractions]{#fraction-addition}

For integers $$m$$, $$n$$, $$r$$, $$s$$ with $$n \neq 0$$ and $$s \neq 0$$,

```math
\frac{m}{n} + \frac{r}{n} = \frac{m + r}{n}
\qquad \text{and} \qquad
\frac{m}{n} + \frac{r}{s} = \frac{ms + rn}{ns}
```

:::

For example,

```math
\frac{1}{4} + \frac{2}{3} = \frac{1 \cdot 3 + 2 \cdot 4}{4 \cdot 3} = \frac{3 + 8}{12}
= \frac{11}{12}
```

and with a negative fraction,

```math
\frac{5}{6} + \frac{-3}{4} = \frac{5 \cdot 4 + (-3) \cdot 6}{24} = \frac{20 - 18}{24} = \frac{2}{24}
= \frac{1}{12}
```

The product of the denominators always works as a common denominator, but a smaller one is often
easier. Here $$12$$ would do: $$\frac{10}{12} - \frac{9}{12} = \frac{1}{12}$$.

:::remark[The answer doesn't depend on how the fractions are written]

The rule uses particular numerators and denominators, but the same rational number can be written in
many ways. Fortunately, the answer comes out the same whichever way we write the fractions. For
example $$\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3} = \frac{5}{6}$$, and writing $$\frac{1}{2}$$ as $$\frac{2}{4}$$
instead gives $$\frac{2}{4} + \frac{1}{3} = \frac{10}{12}$$, which is again $$\frac{5}{6}$$. This
can be proved in general by cross-multiplying, and the same is true for multiplication below.

:::

Zero and opposites behave just as they did for integers. Any fraction with numerator $$0$$ equals
$$0$$, since $$\frac{0}{n} = \frac{0}{1}$$ by cross-multiplying ($$0 \cdot 1 = 0 \cdot n$$). So
adding $$0$$ changes nothing:

```math
\frac{m}{n} + 0 = \frac{m}{n} + \frac{0}{n} = \frac{m + 0}{n} = \frac{m}{n}
```

The opposite of $$\frac{m}{n}$$ is $$\frac{-m}{n}$$, because
$$\frac{m}{n} + \frac{-m}{n} = \frac{m - m}{n} = \frac{0}{n} = 0$$. Putting this together with the
minus sign rules in :ref{#fraction-signs} shows that

```math
-\frac{m}{n} = \frac{-m}{n} = \frac{m}{-n}
```

Addition of rational numbers is also commutative and associative, which we take as basic facts, as
we did for integers. Every rule in [Addition](./addition.mdx) was proved from just these facts
together with $$a + 0 = a$$ and $$a + (-a) = 0$$, so all of them hold for rational numbers as well.
For instance, if $$a + b = 0$$ then $$b = -a$$, and $$-(a + b) = -a - b$$.

:::proposition[Sums of positive fractions]{#positive-sum}

The sum of two positive rational numbers is positive.

:::

:::proof

Write the two numbers as $$\frac{m}{n}$$ and $$\frac{r}{s}$$ with $$m$$, $$n$$, $$r$$, $$s$$ all
positive integers, which we can do because they are positive. By :ref{#fraction-addition}, their sum
is

```math
\frac{ms + rn}{ns}
```

Products and sums of positive integers are positive, so $$ms + rn$$ and $$ns$$ are both positive
integers. A fraction with positive numerator and denominator is positive by definition.

:::

## Multiplying fractions

Taking $$\frac{2}{3}$$ of $$\frac{4}{5}$$ of something is easiest to see with a square. Cut it into
$$5$$ columns and keep $$4$$ of them; that is $$\frac{4}{5}$$. Then cut it into $$3$$ rows and keep
$$2$$ of them; that takes $$\frac{2}{3}$$ of what was kept. The square is now in $$3 \cdot 5 = 15$$
equal pieces, and $$2 \cdot 4 = 8$$ of them are kept:

:::area-model{caption="Two thirds of four fifths: 8 of the 15 pieces"}

|     | 1/5  | 1/5  | 1/5  | 1/5  | 1/5 |
| --- | ---- | ---- | ---- | ---- | --- |
| 1/3 | 1/15 | 1/15 | 1/15 | 1/15 |     |
| 1/3 | 1/15 | 1/15 | 1/15 | 1/15 |     |
| 1/3 |      |      |      |      |     |

:::

So the answer is $$\frac{8}{15}$$. In general the numbers of pieces multiply, so we multiply the
tops together and the bottoms together.

:::definition[Multiplying fractions]{#fraction-multiplication}

For integers $$m$$, $$n$$, $$r$$, $$s$$ with $$n \neq 0$$ and $$s \neq 0$$,

```math
\frac{m}{n} \cdot \frac{r}{s} = \frac{mr}{ns}
```

:::

For example, $$\frac{2}{3} \cdot \frac{4}{5} = \frac{8}{15}$$, and
$$\frac{-2}{3} \cdot \frac{9}{4} = \frac{-18}{12} = -\frac{3}{2}$$.

It often pays to look for cancellations **before** multiplying out. Splitting each number into
factors shows what cancels:

```math
\frac{6}{35} \cdot \frac{14}{9}
= \frac{2 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 \cdot 7}{5 \cdot 7 \cdot 3 \cdot 3}
= \frac{2 \cdot 2}{5 \cdot 3}
= \frac{4}{15}
```

Multiplying out first would give $$\frac{84}{315}$$, which takes more work to simplify.

Multiplication of rational numbers follows the same basic rules as for integers: it is commutative,
associative and distributive over addition, and $$1 \cdot a = a$$ and $$0 \cdot a = 0$$. As with
addition, every rule in [Multiplication](./multiplication.mdx) that was proved from these, including
the [algebraic identities](./identities.mdx), holds for rational numbers too.

:::proposition[Powers of fractions]{#fraction-powers}

For integers $$m$$ and $$n$$ with $$n \neq 0$$, and any positive integer $$k$$,

```math
\left(\frac{m}{n}\right)^k = \frac{m^k}{n^k}
```

:::

:::proof

A power is a repeated product, and :ref{#fraction-multiplication} says that multiplying fractions
multiplies the tops and the bottoms separately. So multiplying $$k$$ copies of $$\frac{m}{n}$$
together gives a numerator of $$k$$ copies of $$m$$ multiplied together, and a denominator of $$k$$
copies of $$n$$:

```math
\left(\frac{m}{n}\right)^k
= \underbrace{\frac{m}{n} \cdot \frac{m}{n} \cdots \frac{m}{n}}_{k \text{ times}}
= \frac{m \cdot m \cdots m}{n \cdot n \cdots n}
= \frac{m^k}{n^k}
```

:::

For example, $$\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^3 = \frac{8}{27}$$ and
$$\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)^4 = \frac{1}{16}$$.

:::example[Halving over time]

The amount of a medicine in the body halves every $$6$$ hours. Starting from $$80$$ mg, after $$6$$
hours there is $$\frac{1}{2} \cdot 80$$ mg left, after $$12$$ hours $$\frac{1}{2}$$ of that, which
is $$\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^2 \cdot 80$$ mg, and so on. After $$24$$ hours, which is $$4$$
halvings,

```math
\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^4 \cdot 80 = \frac{80}{16} = 5 \text{ mg}
```

and after $$30$$ hours there is $$\frac{80}{32} = \frac{5}{2}$$ mg.

:::

## Comparing fractions

Order works for rational numbers exactly as it did for integers (:ref{#order}): $$a < b$$ means that
$$b - a$$ is positive, which on the number line means $$b$$ sits to the right of $$a$$. To compare
two fractions in practice, cross-multiply.

:::proposition[Comparing fractions]{#compare-fractions}

For integers $$m$$, $$r$$ and positive integers $$n$$, $$s$$,

```math
\frac{m}{n} < \frac{r}{s} \qquad \text{exactly when} \qquad ms < rn
```

:::

:::proof

By :ref{#fraction-addition}, the difference is

```math
\frac{r}{s} - \frac{m}{n} = \frac{rn - ms}{ns}
```

Its denominator $$ns$$ is positive, so the difference is positive exactly when its numerator
$$rn - ms$$ is, that is, when $$ms < rn$$.

:::

For example, $$\frac{5}{8} < \frac{2}{3}$$, because $$5 \cdot 3 = 15 < 16 = 2 \cdot 8$$. The rules
for order carry over too: inequalities can be chained, survive adding the same number to both sides,
and reverse when both sides are multiplied by a negative number.

Unlike the integers, the rational numbers have no "next" number: between any two of them there is
always another, such as the one halfway between. So the fractions are packed densely along the
number line. The next pages show they still leave gaps.

## Decimals

A decimal that stops is a rational number, since each decimal place is a number of tenths,
hundredths and so on. A decimal with $$k$$ places is an integer divided by $$10^k$$:

```math
0.75 = \frac{75}{100} = \frac{3}{4}, \qquad 2.5 = \frac{25}{10} = \frac{5}{2},
\qquad 0.03 = \frac{3}{100}
```

The reverse is not always true. A fraction in lowest form has a stopping decimal only when its
denominator divides a power of $$10$$, so $$\frac{1}{3}$$ becomes $$0.333\ldots$$, which goes on
forever (:ref{#ex-one-third-decimal} proves this). But the digits of such a fraction always end up
**repeating**, because dividing by $$n$$ can only leave $$n$$ different remainders, so the long
division eventually repeats itself.

Going the other way, a repeating decimal can be turned back into a fraction by shifting it. For
$$x = 0.272727\ldots$$, the block $$27$$ repeats every two places, so multiplying by $$100$$ shifts
it along by one block:

```math
100x = 27.2727\ldots \qquad \text{so} \qquad 100x - x = 27
```

The endless tails cancel, leaving $$99x = 27$$, so by cross-multiplying $$x = \frac{27}{99}$$, which
is $$\frac{3}{11}$$ in lowest form. This treats an endless decimal as a number, which the rational
numbers alone don't quite justify; the idea is made precise when the real numbers are introduced. So
the rational numbers are exactly the decimals that stop or repeat. A decimal that never settles into
a repeating pattern, like the one for a square root of $$2$$, is not a fraction at all.

## Common mistakes

- **Adding tops and bottoms.** $$\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3}$$ is $$\frac{5}{6}$$, not
  $$\frac{2}{5}$$.
- **Cancelling across a sum.** $$\frac{2 + 3}{2}$$ is not $$3$$: only a common **factor** of the
  whole top and the whole bottom can be cancelled.
- **Comparing by size of the numbers.** $$\frac{1}{3}$$ is smaller than $$\frac{1}{2}$$, even though
  $$3$$ is bigger than $$2$$.

## In short

- A rational number is $$\frac{m}{n}$$ with $$m$$, $$n$$ integers and $$n \neq 0$$.
- $$\frac{m}{n} = \frac{r}{s}$$ exactly when $$ms = rn$$; common factors cancel, and minus signs can
  move between top, bottom and front.
- Every positive rational number has a lowest form, with no common factor but $$1$$.
- $$\frac{m}{n} + \frac{r}{s} = \frac{ms + rn}{ns}$$ and
  $$\frac{m}{n} \cdot \frac{r}{s} = \frac{mr}{ns}$$; all the rules for integers carry over.
- To compare fractions with positive denominators, cross-multiply.
- Rational numbers are exactly the decimals that stop or repeat.
