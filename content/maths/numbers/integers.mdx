The first numbers anyone learns are the ones used for counting: $$1, 2, 3, 4, \dots$$, going on
forever. We call these the **counting numbers**. Each one is one more than the one before, and there
is no last one, because you can always count one more.

Counting numbers are enough for counting things, but not for describing changes that can go either
way: a temperature that falls below freezing, a bank balance in overdraft, a lift going down past
the ground floor. For that we need zero, and numbers on the other side of it. This page sets them up
and fixes the words used for them on every later page.

## The number line

A good way to picture whole numbers is as evenly spaced marks along a straight line that goes on
forever in both directions. We pick one mark to be $$0$$, called the **origin**, and a fixed step
size. Every step to the right is one more, and every step to the left is one less.

::number-line{from=-6 to=6 points="0"}

The counting numbers $$1, 2, 3, \dots$$ sit to the right of $$0$$. Each of them has a mirror image
on the left, the same number of steps from $$0$$ but in the other direction. The mirror image of
$$3$$ is written $$-3$$, read "minus three".

## Integers

The marks on the line fall into three groups: the ones to the right of $$0$$, the ones to the left,
and $$0$$ itself in the middle. Each group needs a name, and so does the whole collection of marks.
The definition below simply gives them those names. A temperature of $$5$$ degrees above freezing is
positive, $$5$$ below is negative, and freezing point itself is neither.

:::definition[Integers]{#integers}

- The **positive integers** are the counting numbers $$1, 2, 3, 4, \dots$$
- The **negative integers** are their mirror images on the other side of $$0$$, written with a minus
  sign: $$-1, -2, -3, \dots$$
- **Zero**, $$0$$, is neither positive nor negative.

The **natural numbers** are the positive integers together with $$0$$. The **integers** are all of
these together: the positive integers, the negative integers and $$0$$.

:::

Integers are whole numbers: they have no fractional or decimal part. Some books leave $$0$$ out of
the natural numbers; in this guide it is always included.

The further right a number sits on the line, the bigger it is. So $$-1$$ is bigger than $$-4$$, even
though $$4$$ is bigger than $$1$$: on the left of $$0$$, the number further from $$0$$ is the
smaller one. The [Addition](./addition.mdx) page turns "bigger" into a precise definition.

## Opposites

The mirror image idea works for every integer, not just the positive ones. Imagine folding the
number line in half at $$0$$. Each mark lands on another one: $$3$$ lands on $$-3$$, $$-5$$ lands on
$$5$$, and $$0$$ stays where it is. The mark a number lands on is its opposite.

:::definition[Opposite]{#opposite}

The **opposite** of an integer $$a$$, written $$-a$$, is the integer the same distance from $$0$$ as
$$a$$, but on the other side. It is also called the **additive inverse** of $$a$$. The opposite of
$$0$$ is $$0$$ itself.

:::

So the opposite of $$5$$ is $$-5$$, and the opposite of $$-5$$ is $$5$$. Taking the opposite twice
flips a number to the other side and back again, so you end up where you started:

```math
-(-a) = a, \qquad \text{for example} \quad -(-7) = 7
```

## Addition as movement

Adding can be read as a walk along the number line.

- Adding a **positive** number moves you **right** by that many steps. Starting at $$-2$$ and adding
  $$6$$ lands on $$4$$:

::number-line{from=-4 to=6 points="-2, 4" jumps="-2>4"}

- Adding a **negative** number moves you **left** by that many steps. Starting at $$4$$ and adding
  $$-7$$ lands on $$-3$$:

::number-line{from=-5 to=6 points="4, -3" jumps="4>-3"}

- Adding $$0$$ means taking no steps at all, so the number stays where it is: $$a + 0 = a$$.

Adding a number to its opposite always walks you back to the origin, because the opposite is the
same distance from $$0$$ in the other direction:

```math
a + (-a) = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad -a + a = 0
```

::number-line{from=-5 to=5 points="-4, 4" jumps="4>0: +(−4)"}

This is where the name additive inverse comes from: adding $$-a$$ undoes adding $$a$$. Subtracting
works the same way: taking away $$7$$ from $$4$$ is the same walk as adding $$-7$$, and both land on
$$-3$$. The next page makes this the definition of subtraction.

## The minus sign does not mean "negative"

It is tempting to read $$-a$$ as "a negative number", but that is only true when $$a$$ itself is
positive. The minus sign means "the opposite of". If $$a = -2$$, then $$-a = -(-2) = 2$$, which is
positive. Reading $$-a$$ out loud as "minus $$a$$" or "the opposite of $$a$$" avoids the confusion.

## Common mistakes

- **Thinking $$-a$$ is always negative.** It is the opposite of $$a$$, which is positive when $$a$$
  is negative.
- **Comparing negatives by size alone.** $$-10$$ is further from $$0$$ than $$-7$$, but it is the
  **smaller** number, because it sits further left.

## In short

- The integers are the positive integers, their opposites, and $$0$$; the natural numbers are
  $$0, 1, 2, \dots$$
- On the number line, further right means bigger.
- The opposite $$-a$$ is the same distance from $$0$$ on the other side, and $$-(-a) = a$$.
- Adding is walking along the line: right for positive numbers, left for negative ones.
