Picture the buttons in a lift that goes below the ground floor. The floors above ground are
$$1, 2, 3$$ and so on, the numbers you first learned for counting. But the ground floor needs a
number too, and so do the car park levels underneath it. Counting numbers can't label those, because
they only go up.

The **counting numbers** are $$1, 2, 3, 4, \dots$$, going on forever, since you can always count one
more. Adding zero and the numbers below it gives the integers, which can describe changes in either
direction, like a lift going down or a temperature falling below freezing. In this guide we'll set
them up and fix the words used for them in the rest of the chapter.

## The number line

A good way to picture whole numbers is as evenly spaced marks along a straight line that goes on
forever in both directions. One mark is $$0$$, called the **origin**, and the marks are a fixed step
apart. Every step to the right is one more, and every step to the left is one less.

::number-line{from=-6 to=6 points="0"}

The counting numbers $$1, 2, 3, \dots$$ sit to the right of $$0$$. Each of them has a mirror image
on the left, the same number of steps from $$0$$ but in the other direction. The mirror image of
$$3$$ is written $$-3$$, read "minus three".

## Integers

The marks on the line fall into three groups: the ones to the right of $$0$$, the ones to the left,
and $$0$$ itself in the middle. Each group needs a name, and so does the whole collection of marks.
The definition below gives them those names. A temperature of $$5$$ degrees above freezing is
positive, $$5$$ below is negative, and freezing point itself is neither.

:::definition[Integers]{#integers}

- The **positive integers** are the counting numbers $$1, 2, 3, 4, \dots$$
- The **negative integers** are their mirror images on the other side of $$0$$, written with a minus
  sign: $$-1, -2, -3, \dots$$
- **Zero**, $$0$$, is neither positive nor negative.

The **natural numbers** are the positive integers together with $$0$$. The **integers** are all of
these together: the positive integers, the negative integers and $$0$$.

:::

Integers are whole numbers: they have no fractional or decimal part. Some books leave $$0$$ out of
the natural numbers; in this chapter it is always included.

The further right a number sits on the line, the bigger it is. So $$-1$$ is bigger than $$-4$$, even
though $$4$$ is bigger than $$1$$: on the left of $$0$$, the number further from $$0$$ is the
smaller one. The [Addition](./addition.mdx) page turns "bigger" into a precise definition.

## Opposites

The mirror image idea works for every integer, not only the positive ones. Imagine folding the
number line in half at $$0$$. Each mark lands on another one: $$3$$ lands on $$-3$$, $$-5$$ lands on
$$5$$, and $$0$$ stays where it is. The mark a number lands on is its opposite.

:::definition[Opposite of an integer]{#opposite}

The **opposite** of an integer $$a$$, written $$-a$$, is the integer the same distance from $$0$$ as
$$a$$, but on the other side. It is also called the **additive inverse** of $$a$$. The opposite of
$$0$$ is $$0$$ itself.

:::

So the opposite of $$5$$ is $$-5$$, and the opposite of $$-5$$ is $$5$$. Taking the opposite twice
flips a number to the other side and back again, so you end up where you started:

```math
-(-a) = a, \qquad \text{for example} \quad -(-7) = 7
```

## Addition as movement

Adding can be read as a walk along the number line.

- Adding a **positive** number moves you **right** by that many steps. Starting at $$-2$$ and adding
  $$6$$ lands on $$4$$:

::number-line{from=-4 to=6 points="-2, 4" jumps="-2>4"}

- Adding a **negative** number moves you **left** by that many steps. Starting at $$4$$ and adding
  $$-7$$ lands on $$-3$$:

::number-line{from=-5 to=6 points="4, -3" jumps="4>-3"}

- Adding $$0$$ means taking no steps at all, so the number stays where it is: $$a + 0 = a$$.

Adding a number to its opposite always walks you back to the origin, because the opposite is the
same distance from $$0$$ in the other direction:

```math
a + (-a) = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad -a + a = 0
```

::number-line{from=-5 to=5 points="-4, 4" jumps="4>0: +(−4)"}

This is where the name additive inverse comes from: adding $$-a$$ undoes adding $$a$$. Subtracting
works the same way: taking away $$7$$ from $$4$$ is the same walk as adding $$-7$$, and both land on
$$-3$$. The next page makes this the definition of subtraction.

Try it: pick a start and an amount, add or take away, and watch the walk.

## The minus sign does not mean "negative"

It is tempting to read $$-a$$ as "a negative number", but that is only true when $$a$$ itself is
positive. The minus sign means "the opposite of". If $$a = -2$$, then $$-a = -(-2) = 2$$, which is
positive. Reading $$-a$$ out loud as "minus $$a$$" or "the opposite of $$a$$" avoids the confusion.

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on integers, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-integers-classify}

Say whether each number is positive, negative or neither, and whether it is a natural number:

```math
7, \qquad -3, \qquad 0, \qquad -(-4), \qquad -(5)
```

:::solution

Work out what each number is first, then use :ref{#integers}:

- $$7$$ is a positive integer, so it is a natural number.
- $$-3$$ is negative, so it isn't a natural number.
- $$0$$ is neither positive nor negative, and it is a natural number.
- $$-(-4)$$ is the opposite of $$-4$$, which is $$4$$. It is positive and a natural number.
- $$-(5)$$ is the opposite of $$5$$, which is $$-5$$. It is negative.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-integers-opposites}

Write down the opposite of $$9$$, $$-12$$, $$0$$ and $$-(-3)$$.

:::solution

The opposite is the integer the same distance from $$0$$ on the other side (:ref{#opposite}):

- the opposite of $$9$$ is $$-9$$
- the opposite of $$-12$$ is $$12$$
- the opposite of $$0$$ is $$0$$
- $$-(-3)$$ is $$3$$, so its opposite is $$-3$$

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-integers-missing-step}

A walk along the number line starts at $$-4$$ and ends at $$3$$. What number was added? Another walk
adds $$-9$$ and ends at $$-6$$. Where did it start?

:::solution

From $$-4$$ to $$3$$ is $$7$$ steps to the right: $$4$$ steps to reach $$0$$, then $$3$$ more. So
$$7$$ was added, and indeed $$-4 + 7 = 3$$.

Adding $$-9$$ is a walk of $$9$$ steps to the left. To find the start, walk back $$9$$ steps to the
right from $$-6$$: $$6$$ steps reach $$0$$ and $$3$$ more reach $$3$$. So the walk started at $$3$$,
and $$3 + (-9) = -6$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-integers-order-line}

Which is bigger, $$-8$$ or $$-3$$? List every integer that is bigger than $$-3$$ and smaller than
$$2$$.

:::solution

On the number line, $$-3$$ sits to the right of $$-8$$, so $$-3$$ is bigger. It is closer to $$0$$,
and on the left of $$0$$ the number closer to $$0$$ is the bigger one.

The integers between them are the marks strictly between $$-3$$ and $$2$$: $$-2$$, $$-1$$, $$0$$ and
$$1$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-integers-temperature}

At midnight the temperature is $$-5$$ degrees. By noon it has risen by $$9$$ degrees, and by the
evening it has fallen by $$12$$ degrees from the noon value. Write the evening temperature as a sum
of integers and work it out.

:::solution

A rise is a step to the right and a fall is a step to the left, so the evening temperature is

```math
-5 + 9 + (-12)
```

The first walk goes from $$-5$$ to $$4$$. The second walks $$12$$ steps left from $$4$$, which
passes $$0$$ after $$4$$ steps and goes $$8$$ more. So the evening temperature is $$-8$$ degrees.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-integers-sign-of-opposite}

True or false? Give a reason or a counterexample for each.

1. For every integer $$a$$, the number $$-a$$ is negative.
2. If $$a$$ is negative, then $$-a$$ is positive.
3. There is exactly one integer $$a$$ with $$-a = a$$.

:::solution

1. False. For $$a = -2$$, $$-a = 2$$, which is positive. The minus sign means "the opposite of", not
   "negative".
2. True. A negative $$a$$ sits to the left of $$0$$, so its mirror image across $$0$$ sits to the
   right, which makes it positive.
3. True, and the integer is $$0$$. If $$a$$ isn't $$0$$, then $$a$$ and $$-a$$ are on opposite sides
   of $$0$$, so they are different. The opposite of $$0$$ is $$0$$ itself (:ref{#opposite}).

:::
