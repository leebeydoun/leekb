Multiplication has its own short list of basic rules, plus one rule that ties it to addition. From
these, everything else follows, including the sign rules that are usually just memorised. In
particular, this page answers the question everybody asks at some point: why is a negative times a
negative positive?

A product can be written with a dot, $$4 \cdot 9 = 36$$, or, when letters are involved, just by
writing them side by side: $$ab$$ means $$a \cdot b$$.

## The rules we assume

:::remark[Rules for multiplication]{#multiplication-rules}

For any integers $$a$$, $$b$$ and $$c$$:

1. **Order doesn't matter:** $$ab = ba$$.
2. **Grouping doesn't matter:** $$(ab)c = a(bc)$$.
3. **Multiplying by one:** $$1 \cdot a = a$$.
4. **Distributivity:** $$a(b + c) = ab + ac$$.
5. **Positive products:** if $$a$$ and $$b$$ are positive, so is $$ab$$.

These are taken as given, together with the [rules for addition](./addition.mdx). Everything else on
this page is proved from them.

:::

Rules 1 and 2 work just as they do for addition. For example, both groupings of
$$2 \cdot 5 \cdot (-3)$$ give the same answer:

```math
\begin{aligned}
(2 \cdot 5) \cdot (-3) &= 10 \cdot (-3) = -30 \\
2 \cdot (5 \cdot (-3)) &= 2 \cdot (-15) = -30
\end{aligned}
```

So a long product can be rearranged freely. A useful habit is to gather all the plain numbers at the
front and all the letters at the back:

```math
(4x)(-3y) = 4 \cdot (-3) \cdot xy = -12xy,
\qquad
(2a)(5b)(7c) = 2 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 \cdot abc = 70abc
```

## Distributivity

Rule 4 is the one that links multiplication and addition: multiplying a sum is the same as
multiplying each part and then adding. A good way to picture it is as the area of a rectangle. A
rectangle of height $$a$$ and width $$b + c$$ can be cut into two smaller rectangles, of areas
$$ab$$ and $$ac$$:

:::area-model{col-sizes="3 2"}

|     | b   | c   |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a   | ab  | ac  |

:::

This is what makes mental arithmetic like $$6 \cdot 13$$ easy: split $$13$$ into $$10 + 3$$, so
$$6 \cdot 13 = 6 \cdot 10 + 6 \cdot 3 = 60 + 18 = 78$$. It is also how brackets are expanded:

```math
\begin{aligned}
5(2x - 3y) &= 10x - 15y \\
-2(4a - b) &= -8a + 2b
\end{aligned}
```

The rule also works with the sum on the left, $$(b + c)a = ba + ca$$. This doesn't need to be
assumed separately, because the order of factors doesn't matter:
$$(b + c)a = a(b + c) = ab + ac = ba + ca$$.

## Multiplying by zero

It may be surprising that $$0 \cdot a = 0$$ isn't on the list of rules. It doesn't need to be,
because it follows from the others.

:::proposition[Multiplying by zero]{#zero-times}

For every integer $$a$$, $$0 \cdot a = 0$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Field_Product_with_Zero"}

The plan is to show that adding $$a$$ to $$0 \cdot a$$ gives back $$a$$, and then subtract $$a$$.

Using rule 3, then distributivity with the sum on the left (explained above), then $$0 + 1 = 1$$,
then rule 3 again:

```math
0 \cdot a + a = 0 \cdot a + 1 \cdot a = (0 + 1)a = 1 \cdot a = a
```

So $$0 \cdot a + a = a$$. Subtracting $$a$$ from both sides (:ref{#rearranging}) leaves
$$0 \cdot a = a - a$$, and $$a - a = a + (-a) = 0$$ by the rules for addition.

:::

## Minus signs in products

Distributivity also decides what minus signs do in a product. The trick each time is the same as on
the previous page: to show that something is the opposite of $$x$$, check that it adds to $$x$$ to
give $$0$$, and use :ref{#opposite-unique}.

:::proposition[Multiplying by minus one]{#minus-one}

For every integer $$a$$, $$(-1)a = -a$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E2%88%921#Algebraic_properties"}

By :ref{#opposite-unique}, it is enough to show that $$(-1)a$$ adds to $$a$$ to give $$0$$. Writing
$$a$$ as $$1 \cdot a$$ and using distributivity,

```math
(-1)a + a = (-1)a + 1 \cdot a = (-1 + 1)a = 0 \cdot a = 0
```

The last two steps use $$-1 + 1 = 0$$ and then :ref{#zero-times}. So $$(-1)a$$ is the opposite of
$$a$$.

:::

:::proposition[Moving a minus sign]{#minus-in-product}

For integers $$a$$ and $$b$$,

```math
-(ab) = (-a)b = a(-b)
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://math.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Analysis/Introduction_to_Mathematical_Analysis_I_(Lafferriere_Lafferriere_and_Nguyen)/01%3A_Tools_for_Analysis/1.4%3A_Ordered_Field_Axioms"}

Both use the same trick: to show that something is $$-(ab)$$, check that it adds to $$ab$$ to give
$$0$$, and use :ref{#opposite-unique}.

**$$(-a)b = -(ab)$$.** Taking out the common factor $$b$$ with distributivity,

```math
ab + (-a)b = (a + (-a))b = 0 \cdot b = 0
```

using $$a + (-a) = 0$$ and :ref{#zero-times}. So $$(-a)b$$ is the opposite of $$ab$$.

**$$a(-b) = -(ab)$$.** This time the common factor is $$a$$:

```math
ab + a(-b) = a(b + (-b)) = a \cdot 0 = 0
```

since $$a \cdot 0 = 0 \cdot a = 0$$. So $$a(-b)$$ is the opposite of $$ab$$ too.

:::

For example, $$-(4x) = (-4)x = 4(-x)$$. Now for the famous one. Before proving it, here is why it
**has** to be true. Watch what happens to multiples of $$-2$$ as the first factor goes down by one
each time:

| Product | $$3 \cdot (-2)$$ | $$2 \cdot (-2)$$ | $$1 \cdot (-2)$$ | $$0 \cdot (-2)$$ | $$(-1) \cdot (-2)$$ |
| ------- | ---------------- | ---------------- | ---------------- | ---------------- | ------------------- |
| Value   | $$-6$$           | $$-4$$           | $$-2$$           | $$0$$            | ?                   |

Each step down adds $$2$$ to the answer, because one fewer copy of $$-2$$ is being added. For the
pattern to continue, $$(-1)(-2)$$ must be $$0 + 2 = 2$$. Any other answer would break
distributivity.

:::theorem[Two minus signs]{#two-minus}

For integers $$a$$ and $$b$$, $$(-a)(-b) = ab$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_number#Multiplication"}

Move the minus signs out one at a time with :ref{#minus-in-product}, then use
:ref{#double-opposite}:

```math
(-a)(-b) = -(a(-b)) = -(-(ab)) = ab
```

In the first step, the minus sign on the first factor comes out to the front, leaving $$-(a(-b))$$.
In the second, the minus sign on $$b$$ comes out too, giving $$a(-b) = -(ab)$$. That leaves two
minus signs in front of $$ab$$, and they cancel.

:::

In particular $$(-1)(-1) = 1$$, and $$(-6)(-7) = 42$$. Together with rule 5, this gives the familiar
sign rules:

| Signs of the factors | Sign of the product | Example                |
| -------------------- | ------------------- | ---------------------- |
| positive × positive  | positive            | $$3 \cdot 8 = 24$$     |
| negative × negative  | positive            | $$(-3)(-8) = 24$$      |
| positive × negative  | negative            | $$3 \cdot (-8) = -24$$ |
| negative × positive  | negative            | $$(-3) \cdot 8 = -24$$ |

With more factors, count the minus signs: each pair cancels, so an even number of them gives a
positive product, and an odd number gives a negative one. For example,
$$(-2) \cdot 3 \cdot (-5) \cdot (-1)$$ has three minus signs, so it is
$$-(2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 1) = -30$$.

Minus signs are one of the most common sources of mistakes, so it pays to go slowly:

```math
-3(5a - 7b) = -15a + 21b
```

Here $$-3$$ times $$-7b$$ is $$+21b$$, not $$-21b$$.

## Multiplying inequalities

Adding the same number to both sides keeps an inequality true (:ref{#order-rules}). Multiplying is
more delicate: it depends on the sign of the number.

:::proposition[Multiplying an inequality]{#order-multiply}

Let $$a$$, $$b$$ and $$c$$ be integers with $$a < b$$.

1. If $$c > 0$$, then $$ac < bc$$.
2. If $$c < 0$$, then $$ac > bc$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Real_Number_Ordering_is_Compatible_with_Multiplication"}

By :ref{#order}, $$a < b$$ means $$b - a$$ is positive. In each part, we write the difference we
want to be positive as a product of $$b - a$$ and a positive number, then use rule 5.

1. Expanding with distributivity and moving the minus sign with :ref{#minus-in-product},

   ```math
   (b - a)c = bc + (-a)c = bc - ac
   ```

   This is a product of two positive integers, so it is positive by rule 5. So $$bc - ac$$ is
   positive, which means $$ac < bc$$.

2. This time $$c < 0$$, which means $$0 - c = -c$$ is positive. Expanding in the same way, and using
   :ref{#two-minus} for $$(-a)(-c) = ac$$,

   ```math
   (b - a)(-c) = b(-c) + (-a)(-c) = -(bc) + ac = ac - bc
   ```

   This is a product of two positive integers, so it is positive. So $$ac - bc$$ is positive, which
   means $$bc < ac$$, that is, $$ac > bc$$.

For example, $$2 < 5$$, and multiplying by $$c = -3$$ gives $$-6$$ and $$-15$$, with $$-6 > -15$$
because $$-6 - (-15) = 9$$ is positive.

:::

So multiplying both sides by a negative number **reverses** an inequality: $$2 < 5$$, but
$$-2 > -5$$. On the number line, multiplying by $$-1$$ swaps every number with its mirror image,
which turns left into right.

## Powers

Multiplying a number by itself several times is written with a small raised number.

:::definition[Powers]{#power}

For an integer $$a$$ and a positive integer $$n$$, the **$$n$$-th power** of $$a$$ is

```math
a^n = \underbrace{a \cdot a \cdots a}_{n \text{ times}}
```

read "$$a$$ to the power $$n$$". The number $$a$$ is the **base** and $$n$$ is the **exponent**. A
single copy is just the number itself, $$a^1 = a$$. We also set $$a^0 = 1$$ when $$a \neq 0$$, for a
reason explained below.

:::

So $$a^2 = aa$$ and $$a^3 = aaa$$. For example, $$2^5 = 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 = 32$$.

:::proposition[Rules for powers]{#power-rules}

For integers $$a$$ and $$b$$ and positive integers $$m$$ and $$n$$:

1. $$a^m a^n = a^{m+n}$$
2. $$(a^m)^n = a^{mn}$$
3. $$(ab)^n = a^n b^n$$

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponentiation#Positive_exponents"}

Each one comes from writing both sides out as products and counting copies. Since order and grouping
don't matter in a product, two products with the same factors, the same number of times each, are
equal.

1. $$a^m a^n$$ is $$m$$ copies of $$a$$ followed by $$n$$ more copies, which is $$m + n$$ copies,
   that is, $$a^{m+n}$$.
2. $$(a^m)^n$$ is $$n$$ copies of $$a^m$$. Each of those is $$m$$ copies of $$a$$, so altogether
   there are $$n$$ groups of $$m$$, which is $$mn$$ copies of $$a$$.
3. $$(ab)^n$$ is $$n$$ copies of $$ab$$, so it contains $$n$$ copies of $$a$$ and $$n$$ copies of
   $$b$$. Rearranging puts all the copies of $$a$$ first and all the copies of $$b$$ after them,
   giving $$a^n b^n$$. For example, $$(ab)^3 = ababab = aaabbb = a^3 b^3$$.

:::

For example, $$x^2 x^4 = (xx)(xxxx) = x^6$$, $$(x^2)^3 = x^2 x^2 x^2 = x^6$$ and $$(3y)^2 = 9y^2$$.
They also combine: $$(3x^2)^3 = 3^3 (x^2)^3 = 27x^6$$.

The choice $$a^0 = 1$$ is the only one that keeps rule 1 working when an exponent is $$0$$:
$$a^m a^0$$ should be $$a^{m+0} = a^m$$, which forces $$a^0 = 1$$.

Because of the sign rules, a negative base gives a positive result for an even power and a negative
result for an odd power: $$(-2)^4 = 16$$ but $$(-2)^3 = -8$$.

Powers appear naturally in repeated growth. Suppose a culture of $$500$$ bacteria triples every
hour. After one hour there are $$3 \cdot 500$$, after two hours $$3^2 \cdot 500$$, and after four
hours

```math
3^4 \cdot 500 = 81 \cdot 500 = 40{,}500 \text{ bacteria}
```

## Common mistakes

- **Losing a minus sign when expanding.** In $$-3(5a - 7b)$$, the $$-3$$ multiplies both terms, and
  $$(-3)(-7b) = +21b$$.
- **Confusing $$-2^4$$ with $$(-2)^4$$.** The power applies to what is directly below it:
  $$-2^4 = -(2^4) = -16$$, but $$(-2)^4 = 16$$.
- **Powering only part of a product.** $$(3x)^2 = 9x^2$$, not $$3x^2$$.
- **Forgetting to flip an inequality.** Multiplying both sides by a negative number reverses it.

## In short

- Five rules are assumed: order and grouping don't matter, $$1 \cdot a = a$$, distributivity, and
  positive times positive is positive.
- From them: $$0 \cdot a = 0$$, $$(-1)a = -a$$, and $$(-a)(-b) = ab$$.
- An even number of minus signs gives a positive product; an odd number gives a negative one.
- Multiplying an inequality by a positive number keeps it; by a negative number reverses it.
- $$a^m a^n = a^{m+n}$$, $$(a^m)^n = a^{mn}$$, $$(ab)^n = a^n b^n$$, and $$a^0 = 1$$.
