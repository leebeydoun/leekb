This guide doesn't just state rules; it shows why each one has to hold. The arguments that do this
are called **proofs**. Proofs can look intimidating at first, but almost all of them are built from
a handful of patterns. Once you can recognise the pattern a proof is using, it becomes much easier
to follow, and much easier to write one yourself.

This page goes through those patterns one at a time. Each section explains the idea, why it works,
and how to use it, with worked examples. The examples use a few ideas defined later in the guide,
like even and odd numbers, but only in ways that should already feel familiar. Later pages link back
here whenever a pattern is used, so this is also a page to come back to.

## What a proof is

A **proof** is a chain of steps that starts from things we already accept and ends at the statement
we want. Every step has to be justified by one of three things:

- a **definition**, which says exactly what a word means;
- a **basic rule** that we have agreed to take as given, such as $$a + b = b + a$$;
- a **result proved earlier**.

Nothing else is allowed: not "it looks true", not "it works for the examples I tried". In return, a
proof settles a question **for every case at once**, including the infinitely many cases nobody will
ever check by hand. That is what makes it so much stronger than checking examples.

In this guide a proof sits under the statement it proves, in a box marked _Proof_, and ends with the
symbol ∎ to show that the argument is finished.

### Letters stand for every number at once

Statements in this guide usually talk about letters, as in "for any integers $$a$$ and $$b$$,
$$a + b = b + a$$". The letter doesn't stand for one particular number that we have forgotten; it
stands for any integer at all. A proof about $$a$$ and $$b$$ may only use facts that are true of
**every** integer, and that is exactly why its conclusion holds for every integer.

Two phrases come up again and again, and it matters which one is meant:

- **"For every"** (or "for any", "for all") means the statement holds whichever number is chosen.
  "For every integer $$n$$, $$n + 0 = n$$" is a claim about all integers.
- **"For some"** (or "there is", "there exists") means at least one number makes it hold. "There is
  an integer $$n$$ with $$n + 3 = 1$$" is true because $$n = -2$$ works.

Definitions often hide a "for some". For example, "$$n$$ is even" means $$n = 2k$$ **for some**
integer $$k$$ (:ref{#even-odd}). Spotting these hidden phrases is the first step in most proofs.

### Saying the opposite

Several proof methods start by supposing that a statement is **false**, so it helps to know how to
say "not" carefully.

- The opposite of "**every** integer has property $$P$$" is "**some** integer does not have property
  $$P$$". To deny that all swans are white, you only need one swan that isn't.
- The opposite of "**some** integer has property $$P$$" is "**no** integer has property $$P$$", that
  is, "every integer fails to have it".
- The opposite of "$$P$$ and $$Q$$" is "not $$P$$, or not $$Q$$". The opposite of "$$m$$ and $$n$$
  are both even" is "at least one of $$m$$ and $$n$$ is odd".

A common slip is to think the opposite of "every number is positive" is "every number is negative".
It isn't: it is "some number is not positive".

## Direct proofs

The most common pattern simply starts from what we are told and works forward, step by step, to what
we want. A reliable way to find a direct proof is:

1. **Write down what you are given**, and unpack every definition in it into a formula.
2. **Write down what you want**, also unpacked into a formula. This is your target.
3. **Bridge the gap**: rearrange what you have until it matches the target.

Step 2 is the one people skip, and it is often the most useful. Once you can see the exact form the
answer must take, the algebra usually tells you what to do.

:::example[A sum of even numbers]

**Claim.** If $$a$$ and $$b$$ are even, then $$a + b$$ is even.

**Given.** $$a$$ and $$b$$ are even, so by :ref{#even-odd} there are integers $$k$$ and $$l$$ with
$$a = 2k$$ and $$b = 2l$$. (We use two different letters, because $$a$$ and $$b$$ needn't be equal.)

**Want.** $$a + b = 2 \times (\text{some integer})$$.

**Bridge.** Substitute and take out the common factor:

```math
a + b = 2k + 2l = 2(k + l)
```

Since $$k + l$$ is an integer, $$a + b$$ is $$2$$ times an integer, so it is even.

:::

:::example[Multiples of 6 are multiples of 3]

**Claim.** If $$n$$ is a multiple of $$6$$, then $$n$$ is a multiple of $$3$$.

**Given.** $$n = 6k$$ for some integer $$k$$.

**Want.** $$n = 3 \times (\text{some integer})$$.

**Bridge.** $$n = 6k = 3(2k)$$, and $$2k$$ is an integer.

:::

It is fine, and often wise, to work **backwards** on scrap paper, starting from the target and
asking "what would I need for this to be true?". But the written proof should run forwards, from
what is given to what is wanted, so that every step follows from the ones before it.

## Examples can't prove, but one example can disprove

Checking examples is an excellent way to guess what is true. It can never prove a "for every"
statement, though, because however many cases you check, there are always infinitely many left. On
the other hand, a single case where a statement fails is enough to show that it is false.

:::definition[Counterexample]{#counterexample}

A **counterexample** to a statement about every number is one particular number for which the
statement is false.

:::

This follows from the section on saying the opposite: "not every number has property $$P$$" means
"some number fails to have it", and a counterexample is that number.

:::example[Patterns can break]

Is $$n^2 + n + 11$$ never a multiple of $$11$$, for positive integers $$n$$? Checking the first few
values gives

```math
13,\ 17,\ 23,\ 31,\ 41,\ 53,\ 67,\ 83,\ 101
```

for $$n = 1, \dots, 9$$, and none of them is a multiple of $$11$$. But $$n = 10$$ gives
$$100 + 10 + 11 = 121 = 11 \cdot 11$$. Nine cases in a row looked convincing, and the tenth is a
counterexample.

:::

Counterexamples are also the quickest way to catch a tempting mistake. Is $$(a + b)^2$$ the same as
$$a^2 + b^2$$? Taking $$a = b = 1$$ gives $$4$$ on the left and $$2$$ on the right, so no.

When hunting for a counterexample, try the awkward cases first: $$0$$, $$1$$, negative numbers, two
equal numbers, and small numbers that are easy to check. Statements that are false usually fail
somewhere simple.

## If, then, and turning statements around

Many statements have the form "**if** $$P$$, **then** $$Q$$". Here $$P$$ is called the
**hypothesis** (what we assume) and $$Q$$ the **conclusion** (what we then get). Such a statement
promises only one thing: whenever $$P$$ is true, $$Q$$ is true too. It says nothing about what
happens when $$P$$ is false.

Two other statements are built from the same parts, and it is important to keep them apart.

:::definition[Converse and contrapositive]{#contrapositive}

For a statement "if $$P$$, then $$Q$$":

- its **converse** is "if $$Q$$, then $$P$$";
- its **contrapositive** is "if not $$Q$$, then not $$P$$".

:::

Take the true statement "if $$n$$ is a multiple of $$4$$, then $$n$$ is even".

| Version        | Statement                                                   | True? |
| -------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | ----- |
| Original       | if $$n$$ is a multiple of $$4$$, then $$n$$ is even         | yes   |
| Converse       | if $$n$$ is even, then $$n$$ is a multiple of $$4$$         | no    |
| Contrapositive | if $$n$$ is not even, then $$n$$ is not a multiple of $$4$$ | yes   |

The converse fails, with $$6$$ as a counterexample: it is even but not a multiple of $$4$$. A
statement and its converse are genuinely different claims, and one can be true while the other is
false. "If it is raining, the ground is wet" is true, but "if the ground is wet, it is raining" is
not: someone might have watered the garden.

The contrapositive, on the other hand, is always true or false along with the original.

:::proposition[A statement and its contrapositive]{#contrapositive-equivalent}

A statement "if $$P$$, then $$Q$$" and its contrapositive "if not $$Q$$, then not $$P$$" are either
both true or both false.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contraposition#Simple_proof_by_contradiction"}

There are two things to show: if the original is true then so is the contrapositive, and the other
way round.

**If the original is true, so is the contrapositive.** Suppose "if $$P$$, then $$Q$$" is true, and
suppose $$Q$$ fails. Could $$P$$ hold? If it did, the original statement would make $$Q$$ hold too,
but $$Q$$ fails. So $$P$$ doesn't hold. We started from "not $$Q$$" and reached "not $$P$$", which
is exactly what the contrapositive says.

**If the contrapositive is true, so is the original.** The contrapositive of "if not $$Q$$, then not
$$P$$" is "if not not $$P$$, then not not $$Q$$". Saying "not" twice cancels out, so this is just
"if $$P$$, then $$Q$$" again. Applying the first half to the statement "if not $$Q$$, then not
$$P$$" therefore shows that if it is true, so is the original.

:::

This gives a second way to prove an "if, then" statement: prove its contrapositive instead. It is
worth doing whenever "not $$Q$$" is easier to start from than $$P$$.

:::example[Proving the contrapositive]

**Claim.** If $$3n + 2$$ is odd, then $$n$$ is odd.

Trying this directly is awkward: knowing $$3n + 2 = 2k + 1$$ tells us $$3n = 2k - 1$$, and it is not
obvious how to get from there to $$n$$. The contrapositive is much easier.

**Contrapositive.** If $$n$$ is **not** odd, then $$3n + 2$$ is **not** odd. In other words: if
$$n$$ is even, then $$3n + 2$$ is even.

**Proof of the contrapositive.** Write $$n = 2k$$. Then

```math
3n + 2 = 6k + 2 = 2(3k + 1)
```

which is even. By :ref{#contrapositive-equivalent}, the original claim is true too.

:::

The guide uses this method to show that if $$3$$ divides $$a^2$$ then $$3$$ divides $$a$$: it is
much easier to start from a number that is **not** a multiple of $$3$$ and look at its square.

## "Exactly when"

Sometimes a statement and its converse are both true, and we want to say so in one go.

:::definition[Exactly when]{#exactly-when}

"$$P$$ **exactly when** $$Q$$" (also written "$$P$$ if and only if $$Q$$") means both "if $$P$$,
then $$Q$$" and "if $$Q$$, then $$P$$".

:::

So $$P$$ and $$Q$$ are true in exactly the same situations: they are two ways of saying the same
thing. Proving an "exactly when" statement therefore takes **two** proofs, one in each direction,
and a proof that only does one of them is only half finished. It helps to label the two halves
clearly.

:::example[Two directions]

**Claim.** An integer $$n$$ is even exactly when $$n + 1$$ is odd.

**If $$n$$ is even, then $$n + 1$$ is odd.** Write $$n = 2k$$. Then $$n + 1 = 2k + 1$$, which is
odd.

**If $$n + 1$$ is odd, then $$n$$ is even.** Write $$n + 1 = 2k + 1$$. Subtracting $$1$$ from both
sides gives $$n = 2k$$, which is even.

:::

Either direction can be proved by any method: one half might be direct and the other by
contrapositive.

## Proof by contradiction

To prove that something is true, we can suppose that it is **false**, and show that this leads to
something impossible.

:::remark[Proof by contradiction]{#contradiction}

To prove a statement $$S$$:

1. Suppose $$S$$ is false.
2. Using that supposition, together with definitions, rules and earlier results, reach something
   impossible: a statement that contradicts itself, or contradicts something already known.
3. Conclude that the supposition was wrong, so $$S$$ is true.

:::

Why does this work? Correct steps never lead from true statements to a false one. So if correct
steps have led to something impossible, one of the starting points must have been false, and the
only starting point we weren't sure of was the supposition that $$S$$ is false.

:::example[There is no largest integer]

**Claim.** There is no largest integer.

**Suppose not:** suppose there is a largest integer, and call it $$N$$. Then $$N + 1$$ is also an
integer, and $$N + 1 > N$$. So $$N$$ is not the largest after all, which contradicts the way we
chose it. So there is no largest integer.

:::

:::example[An equation with no solutions]

**Claim.** There are no integers $$m$$ and $$n$$ with $$2m + 4n = 7$$.

**Suppose not:** suppose $$2m + 4n = 7$$ for some integers $$m$$ and $$n$$. The left side is
$$2(m + 2n)$$, which is even. The right side, $$7 = 2 \cdot 3 + 1$$, is odd. So $$7$$ would be both
even and odd, which is impossible (:ref{#even-or-odd}). So there are no such integers.

:::

Proof by contradiction is especially useful for statements that say something **doesn't** exist,
like these two, because "it does exist" gives us something concrete to work with. The most famous
example in this guide shows that no fraction squares to $$2$$. It supposes that one does, writes it
in lowest form, and shows that its top and bottom would then both have to be even, which lowest form
rules out.

Proving the contrapositive is closely related: supposing "not $$Q$$" and reaching "not $$P$$" is the
same as supposing $$P$$ and "not $$Q$$" together and reaching a contradiction. When an argument
naturally ends in "not $$P$$", the contrapositive is the cleaner way to write it.

## Splitting into cases

When every number falls into one of a few kinds, we can prove a statement by checking each kind
separately. The important thing is that the cases must **cover every possibility**, or the proof has
a hole.

:::example[Two cases]

**Claim.** For every integer $$n$$, the number $$n^2 + n$$ is even.

Every integer is either even or odd (:ref{#even-or-odd}), so two cases cover everything.

**Case 1: $$n$$ is even.** Write $$n = 2k$$. Then $$n^2 + n = 4k^2 + 2k = 2(2k^2 + k)$$, which is
even.

**Case 2: $$n$$ is odd.** Write $$n = 2k + 1$$. Then

```math
n^2 + n = (4k^2 + 4k + 1) + (2k + 1) = 4k^2 + 6k + 2 = 2(2k^2 + 3k + 1)
```

which is even too.

:::

There is a neater proof here as well: $$n^2 + n = n(n + 1)$$ is a product of two consecutive
integers, one of which is even. Looking for a slicker argument after finding one that works is a
good habit, but a proof by cases that works is a perfectly good proof.

Cases can be split more than two ways. Dividing by $$3$$ leaves remainder $$0$$, $$1$$ or $$2$$
(:ref{#division-remainder}), so every integer can be written as $$3k$$, $$3k + 1$$ or $$3k + 2$$.

:::example[Three cases]

**Claim.** For every integer $$n$$, the number $$n(n + 1)(2n + 1)$$ is a multiple of $$3$$.

In each case we find one factor that is a multiple of $$3$$, which makes the whole product one.

- **$$n = 3k$$:** the factor $$n$$ is a multiple of $$3$$.
- **$$n = 3k + 1$$:** the factor $$2n + 1 = 6k + 3 = 3(2k + 1)$$ is a multiple of $$3$$.
- **$$n = 3k + 2$$:** the factor $$n + 1 = 3k + 3 = 3(k + 1)$$ is a multiple of $$3$$.

:::

## Proof by induction

Some statements are really an endless list of statements, one for each positive integer. For
example, "for every positive integer $$n$$, $$1 + 2 + \cdots + n = \frac{n(n + 1)}{2}$$" says

```math
1 = \frac{1 \cdot 2}{2}, \qquad 1 + 2 = \frac{2 \cdot 3}{2}, \qquad 1 + 2 + 3 = \frac{3 \cdot 4}{2},
\qquad \dots
```

and so on forever. We can check any one of these by hand, but never all of them. Induction is a way
to prove the whole endless list with just two arguments.

### The idea

Picture an endless row of dominoes standing on their ends. You want to be sure that every single one
falls over. You don't need to watch each one; it is enough to know two things:

1. **The first domino falls.** Someone pushes it.
2. **Each domino knocks over the next.** They are spaced so that whenever one falls, the one after
   it falls too.

Then the first falls, so the second falls, so the third falls, and so on: every domino falls,
however far along the row it is. Notice that the second fact on its own proves nothing; a row of
perfectly spaced dominoes that nobody pushes stays standing. And the first fact on its own only
knocks over one domino. It is the two together that do the work.

Induction is exactly this, with statements in place of dominoes.

:::remark[Induction]{#induction}

Suppose that for each positive integer $$n$$ there is a statement, which we call $$P(n)$$. To prove
that $$P(n)$$ is true for every positive integer $$n$$, it is enough to show two things:

1. **The base case:** $$P(1)$$ is true.
2. **The inductive step:** for every positive integer $$k$$, **if** $$P(k)$$ is true, **then**
   $$P(k + 1)$$ is true.

We take this as a basic fact about the positive integers: starting from $$1$$ and adding $$1$$ over
and over reaches every one of them, so nothing gets missed.

:::

### The confusing part: assuming $$P(k)$$

The inductive step says "suppose $$P(k)$$ is true; show that $$P(k + 1)$$ is true". This often feels
like cheating. Aren't we assuming exactly what we are trying to prove?

We aren't. The inductive step doesn't claim that $$P(k)$$ **is** true. It proves an "if, then"
statement: **if** $$P(k)$$ happens to be true, **then** so is $$P(k + 1)$$. That is the domino
spacing: it says nothing about whether domino $$k$$ actually falls, only what happens to domino
$$k + 1$$ if it does. Whether it falls is settled by the chain reaction from the base case.

So in the inductive step, $$k$$ is any positive integer, and $$P(k)$$ is something we are allowed to
use, called the **inductive hypothesis**. The job is to get from $$P(k)$$ to $$P(k + 1)$$. In
practice that means:

1. Write out exactly what $$P(k)$$ says. This is what you know.
2. Write out exactly what $$P(k + 1)$$ says. This is what you want.
3. Find $$P(k)$$ hiding inside $$P(k + 1)$$, and use it.

Step 3 is the heart of every induction proof. The case $$k + 1$$ is usually the case $$k$$ plus one
more piece, and the trick is to spot the case $$k$$ inside it.

### A first example, slowly

:::example[Adding up 1 to n]

**Claim.** For every positive integer $$n$$,

```math
1 + 2 + \cdots + n = \frac{n(n + 1)}{2}
```

It is worth checking a few cases first, both to believe the claim and to see what $$P(n)$$ means:

| $$n$$ | $$1 + \cdots + n$$ | $$\frac{n(n + 1)}{2}$$       |
| ----- | ------------------ | ---------------------------- |
| $$1$$ | $$1$$              | $$\frac{1 \cdot 2}{2} = 1$$  |
| $$2$$ | $$3$$              | $$\frac{2 \cdot 3}{2} = 3$$  |
| $$3$$ | $$6$$              | $$\frac{3 \cdot 4}{2} = 6$$  |
| $$4$$ | $$10$$             | $$\frac{4 \cdot 5}{2} = 10$$ |

Here $$P(n)$$ is the statement "$$1 + 2 + \cdots + n = \frac{n(n + 1)}{2}$$".

**Base case.** $$P(1)$$ says $$1 = \frac{1 \cdot 2}{2}$$, which is true.

**Inductive step.** Let $$k$$ be a positive integer and suppose $$P(k)$$ is true.

- **Know:** $$1 + 2 + \cdots + k = \frac{k(k + 1)}{2}$$.
- **Want:** $$1 + 2 + \cdots + k + (k + 1) = \frac{(k + 1)(k + 2)}{2}$$, which is $$P(k + 1)$$: the
  same statement with $$k + 1$$ in place of $$n$$.

The sum we want is the sum we know, plus one more number. So we replace the first $$k$$ terms using
what we know, then simplify:

```math
\begin{aligned}
1 + 2 + \cdots + k + (k + 1) &= \frac{k(k + 1)}{2} + (k + 1) \\
&= \frac{k(k + 1)}{2} + \frac{2(k + 1)}{2} \\
&= \frac{(k + 1)(k + 2)}{2}
\end{aligned}
```

The last step takes out the common factor $$k + 1$$. That is exactly $$P(k + 1)$$.

**Conclusion.** By induction, $$P(n)$$ is true for every positive integer $$n$$.

:::

So, for example, the numbers from $$1$$ to $$100$$ add up to $$\frac{100 \cdot 101}{2} = 5050$$,
without adding a single one of them.

### A second example: divisibility

The same shape works for statements about divisibility. The trick of finding $$P(k)$$ inside
$$P(k + 1)$$ just looks different.

:::example[Powers of 4]

**Claim.** For every positive integer $$n$$, $$3$$ divides $$4^n - 1$$.

**Base case.** $$4^1 - 1 = 3$$, which $$3$$ divides.

**Inductive step.** Suppose $$3 \mid 4^k - 1$$.

- **Know:** $$4^k - 1 = 3m$$ for some integer $$m$$.
- **Want:** $$4^{k + 1} - 1 = 3 \times (\text{some integer})$$.

To find $$4^k - 1$$ inside $$4^{k + 1} - 1$$, write $$4^{k + 1}$$ as $$4 \cdot 4^k$$ and then
rearrange so that $$4^k - 1$$ appears:

```math
4^{k + 1} - 1 = 4 \cdot 4^k - 1 = 4(4^k - 1) + 4 - 1 = 4(4^k - 1) + 3
```

Now use what we know: $$4(4^k - 1) + 3 = 4 \cdot 3m + 3 = 3(4m + 1)$$, which is a multiple of $$3$$.

**Conclusion.** By induction, $$3 \mid 4^n - 1$$ for every positive integer $$n$$.

:::

The step "$$- 1 = - 4 + 3$$" in the middle is the kind of move that looks like magic the first time.
It came from asking: "I have $$4 \cdot 4^k - 1$$, and I want $$4^k - 1$$ to appear. What do I need?"
Answering that question is most of the work in an inductive step.

### Starting somewhere else

Some statements are false for the first few numbers and only become true from some point on. For
example, "$$2^n > n^2$$" fails for $$n = 2, 3, 4$$ but holds for every $$n$$ from $$5$$ onwards:

| $$n$$   | $$1$$ | $$2$$ | $$3$$ | $$4$$  | $$5$$  | $$6$$  | $$7$$   |
| ------- | ----- | ----- | ----- | ------ | ------ | ------ | ------- |
| $$2^n$$ | $$2$$ | $$4$$ | $$8$$ | $$16$$ | $$32$$ | $$64$$ | $$128$$ |
| $$n^2$$ | $$1$$ | $$4$$ | $$9$$ | $$16$$ | $$25$$ | $$36$$ | $$49$$  |
| True?   | yes   | no    | no    | no     | yes    | yes    | yes     |

So the claim to prove is "$$2^n > n^2$$ for every $$n \ge 5$$". Induction still works; the chain
reaction just starts further along.

Think of the dominoes again, but this time push domino number $$5$$ instead of domino number $$1$$.
Domino $$5$$ knocks over $$6$$, which knocks over $$7$$, and so on forever. Dominoes $$1$$ to $$4$$
stay standing, but that's fine: we never claimed anything about them.

This changes the two parts of the proof in two small ways.

1. **The base case is the starting number.** Here we check $$P(5)$$ instead of $$P(1)$$.
2. **The inductive step only has to work from the starting number on.** The chain reaction only ever
   uses the step to go from $$5$$ to $$6$$, from $$6$$ to $$7$$, and so on. It never goes from $$2$$
   to $$3$$. So in the step we may assume $$k \ge 5$$, and use that fact if it helps.

The second point is easy to miss, and it is often exactly what makes the step work, as the example
shows.

:::example[An inequality that starts late]

**Claim.** For every integer $$n \ge 5$$, $$2^n > n^2$$.

**Base case.** For $$n = 5$$: $$2^5 = 32$$ and $$5^2 = 25$$, and $$32 > 25$$.

**Inductive step.** Let $$k$$ be an integer with $$k \ge 5$$, and suppose $$P(k)$$ is true.

- **Know:** $$2^k > k^2$$.
- **Want:** $$2^{k + 1} > (k + 1)^2$$.

Going from $$2^k$$ to $$2^{k + 1}$$ means doubling, so double both sides of what we know
(:ref{#order-multiply}):

```math
2^{k + 1} = 2 \cdot 2^k > 2k^2
```

So $$2^{k + 1}$$ is bigger than $$2k^2$$. If $$2k^2$$ is in turn at least $$(k + 1)^2$$, we are
done, because then $$2^{k + 1} > 2k^2 \ge (k + 1)^2$$. That leaves one question: is doubling $$k^2$$
enough to get past $$(k + 1)^2$$? Not always:

| $$k$$         | $$1$$ | $$2$$ | $$3$$  | $$4$$  | $$5$$  |
| ------------- | ----- | ----- | ------ | ------ | ------ |
| $$2k^2$$      | $$2$$ | $$8$$ | $$18$$ | $$32$$ | $$50$$ |
| $$(k + 1)^2$$ | $$4$$ | $$9$$ | $$16$$ | $$25$$ | $$36$$ |

It fails for small $$k$$ but works once $$k$$ is a bit bigger. To show it works for every
$$k \ge 5$$, look at the difference:

```math
2k^2 - (k + 1)^2 = 2k^2 - (k^2 + 2k + 1) = k^2 - 2k - 1 = k(k - 2) - 1
```

This is where $$k \ge 5$$ gets used. It makes $$k - 2 \ge 3$$, so $$k(k - 2) \ge 5 \cdot 3 = 15$$,
and the difference is at least $$14$$, which is positive. So $$2k^2 > (k + 1)^2$$, and putting the
pieces together,

```math
2^{k + 1} > 2k^2 > (k + 1)^2
```

which is $$P(k + 1)$$.

**Conclusion.** By induction, $$2^n > n^2$$ for every $$n \ge 5$$.

:::

Why start at $$5$$ and not somewhere earlier? Two things have to hold at the starting number:

- **The base case must be true.** $$P(3)$$ and $$P(4)$$ are false ($$8 < 9$$ and $$16 = 16$$), so
  they can't be the base case.
- **The step must work from there on.** The tables show the step would also fail from $$k = 2$$,
  since $$2 \cdot 2^2 = 8$$ is not bigger than $$3^2 = 9$$.

The first starting number where both hold is $$5$$. A good habit with any "for every $$n \ge n_0$$"
claim is to check a table like the one above first. It tells you where the claim starts being true,
and so where to put the base case.

### Why both steps matter

Each half of an induction proof can be done without the other, and the result is nonsense.

- **A step without a base case.** Let $$P(n)$$ say "$$n = n + 1$$". If $$k = k + 1$$, then adding
  $$1$$ to both sides gives $$k + 1 = k + 2$$, so the inductive step works perfectly. But $$P(1)$$,
  which says $$1 = 2$$, is false, so nothing ever gets started. The dominoes are well spaced but
  nobody pushed the first one.
- **A base case without a step.** The $$n^2 + n + 11$$ example earlier was true for
  $$n = 1, \dots, 9$$ and then failed. Checking first cases, however many, is not induction.

### Writing an induction proof

Every induction proof can be laid out the same way:

1. **Say what $$P(n)$$ is**, in words or as a formula.
2. **Base case:** check $$P(1)$$ (or $$P(n_0)$$ if starting later).
3. **Inductive step:** "Suppose $$P(k)$$ is true for some $$k$$." Write out what you know and what
   you want. Find $$P(k)$$ inside $$P(k + 1)$$, use it, and reach $$P(k + 1)$$.
4. **Conclude:** "By induction, $$P(n)$$ is true for every $$n$$."

Later in the guide, induction proves that congruences can be raised to any power: if $$a \equiv b$$,
then $$a^n \equiv b^n$$. The base case is $$n = 1$$, and the step multiplies the case $$k$$ by
$$a \equiv b$$ to reach the case $$k + 1$$.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating examples as a proof.** However many cases you check, a "for every" claim needs an
  argument that covers all of them.
- **Proving the converse instead.** "If $$P$$ then $$Q$$" and "if $$Q$$ then $$P$$" are different
  statements. Make sure you start from what you are told.
- **Getting the opposite wrong.** The opposite of "every number is even" is "some number is odd",
  not "every number is odd".
- **Doing only half of "exactly when".** Both directions need a proof.
- **Cases that miss something.** Splitting into "positive" and "negative" forgets $$0$$.
- **Using what you want to prove.** A step that quietly assumes the conclusion makes the argument go
  round in a circle. (Assuming $$P(k)$$ in an inductive step is **not** this mistake, because the
  step only proves "if $$P(k)$$ then $$P(k + 1)$$".)
- **Forgetting the base case.** The inductive step alone proves nothing.

## In short

- A proof uses only definitions, basic rules and earlier results, and settles every case at once.
- Direct proof: unpack what you are given and what you want, then bridge the gap.
- One counterexample shows a "for every" statement is false; examples never prove one.
- A statement and its contrapositive stand or fall together; its converse is a different claim.
- "Exactly when" needs a proof in each direction.
- Proof by contradiction supposes the statement is false and reaches something impossible.
- Proof by cases must cover every possibility.
- Induction proves a base case, and that each case leads to the next, which together reach every
  case.
