How many ways are there to choose $$3$$ toppings from $$5$$? Why are the numbers in front of
$$(a + b)^3 = a^3 + 3a^2 b + 3ab^2 + b^3$$ exactly $$1, 3, 3, 1$$? Both questions have the same
answer, and this page builds it from two ideas: factorials and binomial coefficients. Working with
them is mostly a matter of cancelling fractions, which is why they fit here. They come back in much
more detail in a later guide on counting.

## Factorials

Many counting questions end up multiplying a run of whole numbers all the way down to $$1$$, such as
$$5 \cdot 4 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 \cdot 1 = 120$$. This is the number of ways to line up $$5$$ people, as
the next section explains. Products like this turn up so often that they get their own short name
and symbol.

:::definition[Factorial]{#factorial}

For a positive integer $$n$$, **$$n$$ factorial**, written $$n!$$, is the product of the first $$n$$
positive integers:

```math
n! = 1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdots n
```

We also define $$0! = 1$$.

:::

Rather than multiplying everything out each time, notice that each factorial is the one before it
times one more number: $$n! = n \cdot (n - 1)!$$. So the factorials can be built up a step at a
time:

| $$n$$ | $$n!$$                   |
| ----- | ------------------------ |
| $$1$$ | $$1$$                    |
| $$2$$ | $$2 \cdot 1 = 2$$        |
| $$3$$ | $$3 \cdot 2 = 6$$        |
| $$4$$ | $$4 \cdot 6 = 24$$       |
| $$5$$ | $$5 \cdot 24 = 120$$     |
| $$6$$ | $$6 \cdot 120 = 720$$    |
| $$7$$ | $$7 \cdot 720 = 5040$$   |
| $$8$$ | $$8 \cdot 5040 = 40320$$ |

Factorials grow very quickly. The same rule explains the choice $$0! = 1$$: taking $$n = 1$$ in
$$n! = n \cdot (n - 1)!$$ gives $$1! = 1 \cdot 0!$$, which only works if $$0! = 1$$.

## Choosing

Factorials count **arrangements**. To put $$n$$ different things in a row, there are $$n$$ choices
for the first place, then $$n - 1$$ left for the second, and so on down to $$1$$ for the last. So
there are $$n \cdot (n - 1) \cdots 1 = n!$$ ways. For example, the letters $$a$$, $$b$$, $$c$$ can
be arranged in $$3! = 6$$ ways: $$abc$$, $$acb$$, $$bac$$, $$bca$$, $$cab$$, $$cba$$.

Now suppose we want to **choose** $$n$$ things out of $$m$$, where only the selection matters, not
the order. Picking them one at a time in order gives $$m$$ choices for the first, $$m - 1$$ for the
second, and so on, $$n$$ times:

```math
m(m - 1) \cdots (m - n + 1) = \frac{m!}{(m - n)!}
```

ordered picks. (In the fraction, $$m!$$ is every number from $$m$$ down to $$1$$, and dividing by
$$(m - n)!$$ cancels the ones from $$m - n$$ down, leaving $$m$$ down to $$m - n + 1$$.) But every
selection has been counted once for each order its $$n$$ things could be picked in, which is $$n!$$
times. Dividing by $$n!$$ counts each selection once.

:::definition[Binomial coefficient]{#binomial}

For natural numbers $$m$$ and $$n$$ with $$n$$ between $$0$$ and $$m$$, the **binomial coefficient**
"$$m$$ choose $$n$$" is

```math
\binom{m}{n} = \frac{m!}{n!\,(m - n)!}
```

:::

By the argument above, it counts the number of ways to choose $$n$$ things out of $$m$$, when the
order doesn't matter. For example, choosing $$2$$ toppings from $$4$$ (say $$a$$, $$b$$, $$c$$,
$$d$$) gives $$4 \cdot 3 = 12$$ ordered picks, and each pair is counted twice ($$ab$$ and $$ba$$),
so there are $$6$$ ways: $$ab$$, $$ac$$, $$ad$$, $$bc$$, $$bd$$, $$cd$$. The formula agrees:

```math
\binom{4}{2} = \frac{4!}{2!\,2!} = \frac{24}{2 \cdot 2} = 6
```

Writing the factorials out lets most of the product cancel before multiplying. For example,

```math
\binom{6}{3} = \frac{6!}{3!\,3!} = \frac{6 \cdot 5 \cdot 4 \cdot \cancel{3!}}{3! \cdot \cancel{3!}}
= \frac{6 \cdot 5 \cdot 4}{6} = 20
```

and in the same way $$\binom{5}{2} = \frac{5 \cdot 4}{2} = 10$$. At the ends, $$\binom{m}{0} = 1$$
and $$\binom{m}{m} = 1$$, since there is just one way to choose nothing, and one way to choose
everything.

## Two rules for binomial coefficients

Both rules come from thinking about what is being counted. Choosing $$2$$ toppings to put on a pizza
from $$5$$ is the same as choosing the $$3$$ to leave off, so there must be as many ways to choose
$$2$$ as to choose $$3$$. That is the first rule.

For the second, pick out one particular topping, say mushrooms, and sort the choices of $$2$$ from
$$5$$ into two piles. Choices with mushrooms need $$1$$ more topping from the other $$4$$, which can
be done in $$4$$ ways. Choices without mushrooms take both toppings from the other $$4$$, which can
be done in $$6$$ ways. So there are $$4 + 6 = 10$$ ways in all. Pascal's rule says the same thing
for any numbers, and the proof below checks it with the formula.

:::proposition[Symmetry of binomial coefficients]{#binomial-symmetry}

For natural numbers $$m$$ and $$n$$ with $$n$$ between $$0$$ and $$m$$,

```math
\binom{m}{n} = \binom{m}{m - n}
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binomial_coefficient#Factorial_formula"}

Choosing $$n$$ things to take is the same as choosing the $$m - n$$ things to leave behind, so the
two counts should match. To check it with the formula (:ref{#binomial}), put $$m - n$$ in place of
$$n$$. This is allowed, because $$m - n$$ is also between $$0$$ and $$m$$. The second factor in the
denominator becomes $$\big(m - (m - n)\big)! = n!$$, so

```math
\binom{m}{m - n} = \frac{m!}{(m - n)!\,n!}
```

This is the same fraction as $$\binom{m}{n}$$ with the two factors of the denominator swapped, and
the order of a product doesn't matter.

:::

For example, $$\binom{5}{2} = \binom{5}{3} = 10$$.

:::proposition[Pascal's rule]{#pascal}

For natural numbers $$m$$ and $$n$$ with $$1 \le n \le m$$,

```math
\binom{m}{n - 1} + \binom{m}{n} = \binom{m + 1}{n}
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pascal%27s_rule#Algebraic_proof"}

This is an exercise in adding fractions. The plan is to write both terms as fractions using the
formula in :ref{#binomial}, give them a common denominator, add, and recognise the answer as
$$\binom{m + 1}{n}$$.

**Write out both terms.** For the first term, the bottom of the binomial coefficient is $$n - 1$$,
and $$m - (n - 1) = m - n + 1$$. So

```math
\binom{m}{n - 1} + \binom{m}{n}
= \frac{m!}{(n - 1)!\,(m - n + 1)!} + \frac{m!}{n!\,(m - n)!}
```

Both coefficients make sense because $$1 \le n \le m$$, so $$n - 1$$ and $$n$$ are both between
$$0$$ and $$m$$.

**Find a common denominator.** The denominators are different, but they are close. The rule
$$k! = k \cdot (k - 1)!$$, for any positive integer $$k$$, says

```math
n! = n \cdot (n - 1)! \qquad \text{and} \qquad (m - n + 1)! = (m - n + 1) \cdot (m - n)!
```

(both $$n$$ and $$m - n + 1$$ are at least $$1$$). So multiplying the top and bottom of the first
fraction by $$n$$, and of the second by $$m - n + 1$$, gives both the denominator
$$n!\,(m - n + 1)!$$, without changing their values (:ref{#fraction-cancel}).

**Add.** Adding the numerators over the common denominator (:ref{#fraction-addition}):

```math
\begin{aligned}
&= \frac{m! \cdot n}{n!\,(m - n + 1)!} + \frac{m! \cdot (m - n + 1)}{n!\,(m - n + 1)!} \\
&= \frac{m! \cdot (n + m - n + 1)}{n!\,(m - n + 1)!}
= \frac{m! \cdot (m + 1)}{n!\,(m - n + 1)!}
\end{aligned}
```

In the second line we added the numerators, took out the common factor $$m!$$, and noticed that
$$n$$ and $$-n$$ cancel.

**Recognise the answer.** Finally, $$m! \cdot (m + 1) = (m + 1)!$$ and $$m - n + 1 = (m + 1) - n$$,
so this is

```math
\frac{(m + 1)!}{n!\,\big((m + 1) - n\big)!} = \binom{m + 1}{n}
```

For example, with $$m = 4$$ and $$n = 2$$ this says $$\binom{4}{1} + \binom{4}{2} = 4 + 6 = 10$$,
which is $$\binom{5}{2}$$.

:::

## Pascal's triangle

Pascal's rule means the binomial coefficients can be built without any factorials at all. Write them
in rows, $$\binom{m}{0}, \binom{m}{1}, \dots, \binom{m}{m}$$ for $$m = 0, 1, 2, \dots$$. Each row
starts and ends with $$1$$, and every other entry is the sum of the two entries just above it. This
is **Pascal's triangle**:

```math
\begin{array}{c}
1 \\
1 \quad 1 \\
1 \quad 2 \quad 1 \\
1 \quad 3 \quad 3 \quad 1 \\
1 \quad 4 \quad 6 \quad 4 \quad 1 \\
1 \quad 5 \quad 10 \quad 10 \quad 5 \quad 1
\end{array}
```

The row for $$m = 4$$ is $$1, 4, 6, 4, 1$$, and its middle entry is $$\binom{4}{2} = 6$$, the
toppings count from before.

## The binomial theorem

Compare the rows of the triangle with the formulas on the [Algebraic identities](./identities.mdx)
page: $$(a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$$ has the numbers $$1, 2, 1$$ in front, and $$(a + b)^3$$ has
$$1, 3, 3, 1$$. These are rows $$2$$ and $$3$$. That is no coincidence, and it works for every
power. The reason is counting again. Multiplying out $$(a + b)^3$$ means picking $$a$$ or $$b$$ from
each of the three brackets, and a term like $$a^2 b$$ turns up once for each way of choosing which
bracket gives the $$b$$. Counting choices is exactly what binomial coefficients do.

:::theorem[The binomial theorem]{#binomial-theorem}

For any numbers $$a$$ and $$b$$ and any positive integer $$m$$,

```math
(a + b)^m = \binom{m}{0} a^m + \binom{m}{1} a^{m-1} b + \binom{m}{2} a^{m-2} b^2 + \cdots
+ \binom{m}{m} b^m
```

The term with $$b^n$$ is $$\binom{m}{n} a^{m-n} b^n$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binomial_theorem#Combinatorial_proof"}

The idea is to multiply out the brackets without simplifying, and then count how many times each
kind of term turns up. Think of $$(a + b)^m$$ as $$m$$ brackets multiplied together:

```math
(a + b)(a + b) \cdots (a + b)
```

**What the terms look like.** Multiplying out with distributivity, every term is made by picking
either $$a$$ or $$b$$ from each bracket and multiplying the picks together. For example, with
$$m = 3$$ there are $$2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 = 8$$ terms:

```math
aaa + aab + aba + abb + baa + bab + bba + bbb
```

Since the order of a product doesn't matter, a term with $$n$$ factors of $$b$$ has $$m - n$$
factors of $$a$$, so it equals $$a^{m-n} b^n$$. (At the ends, $$n = 0$$ and $$n = m$$, the terms are
just $$a^m$$ and $$b^m$$.)

**How many there are.** A term with exactly $$n$$ factors of $$b$$ is decided by which $$n$$ of the
$$m$$ brackets supply the $$b$$s. So these terms match up with the ways of choosing $$n$$ brackets
out of $$m$$, and there are $$\binom{m}{n}$$ of them, by the counting meaning of binomial
coefficients (:ref{#binomial}). With $$m = 3$$ and $$n = 1$$, the terms with exactly one $$b$$ are
$$aab$$, $$aba$$ and $$baa$$: there are $$3 = \binom{3}{1}$$ of them, one for each choice of the
bracket that supplies the $$b$$. Together they make the $$3a^2 b$$ in $$(a + b)^3$$.

**Collect.** Adding up these $$\binom{m}{n}$$ equal terms gives $$\binom{m}{n} a^{m-n} b^n$$, for
each $$n$$ from $$0$$ to $$m$$, which is the formula.

:::

So expanding any power of a sum just needs a row of Pascal's triangle. Row $$4$$ is
$$1, 4, 6, 4, 1$$, so

```math
(a + b)^4 = a^4 + 4a^3 b + 6a^2 b^2 + 4ab^3 + b^4
```

and with $$a = x$$ and $$b = -1$$, using row $$5$$,

```math
(x - 1)^5 = x^5 - 5x^4 + 10x^3 - 10x^2 + 5x - 1
```

where the signs alternate because odd powers of $$-1$$ are negative. Taking $$a = b = 1$$ shows that
each row of the triangle adds up to $$(1 + 1)^m = 2^m$$.

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on factorials and binomial coefficients, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-factorials-not-additive}

Is $$(2 + 3)!$$ equal to $$2! + 3!$$? Is $$(2 \cdot 3)!$$ equal to $$2! \cdot 3!$$?

:::solution

Neither is. $$(2 + 3)! = 5! = 120$$, but $$2! + 3! = 2 + 6 = 8$$. And $$(2 \cdot 3)! = 6! = 720$$,
but $$2! \cdot 3! = 2 \cdot 6 = 12$$.

A factorial is a product of a whole run of numbers (:ref{#factorial}), so it doesn't split over sums
or products the way powers of a product do.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-factorials-shelf}

How many ways are there to arrange $$6$$ different books in a row on a shelf? How many ways are
there to choose $$3$$ of them and put those $$3$$ in a row?

:::solution

**All six.** There are $$6$$ choices for the first place, $$5$$ for the second, and so on, so there
are $$6! = 720$$ arrangements.

**Three in a row.** Now only three places are filled: $$6$$ choices for the first, $$5$$ for the
second and $$4$$ for the third, giving $$6 \cdot 5 \cdot 4 = 120$$. This is
$$\frac{6!}{3!} = \frac{720}{6}$$, the ordered picks from the Choosing section.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-factorials-simplify}

Simplify $$\dfrac{(n + 1)!}{(n - 1)!}$$ for a positive integer $$n$$, and check your answer with
$$n = 4$$.

:::solution

Using $$k! = k \cdot (k - 1)!$$ twice,

```math
(n + 1)! = (n + 1) \cdot n! = (n + 1) \cdot n \cdot (n - 1)!
```

so cancelling the common factor $$(n - 1)!$$ (:ref{#fraction-cancel}) leaves

```math
\frac{(n + 1)!}{(n - 1)!} = n(n + 1)
```

Check with $$n = 4$$: $$\frac{5!}{3!} = \frac{120}{6} = 20$$, and $$4 \cdot 5 = 20$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-factorials-committee}

A club of $$9$$ people chooses a committee of $$3$$.

1. How many committees are possible?
2. How many of them include one particular member, Sam, and how many leave Sam out? Check that the
   two counts add up to your first answer, and say which rule this is.

:::solution

1. The order doesn't matter, so the count is (:ref{#binomial})

```math
\binom{9}{3} = \frac{9!}{3!\,6!} = \frac{9 \cdot 8 \cdot 7}{6} = 84
```

2. A committee with Sam needs $$2$$ more people from the other $$8$$: $$\binom{8}{2} = 28$$ ways. A
   committee without Sam takes all $$3$$ from the other $$8$$:
   $$\binom{8}{3} = \frac{8 \cdot 7 \cdot 6}{6} = 56$$ ways. And $$28 + 56 = 84$$.

This is :ref{#pascal} with $$m = 8$$ and $$n = 3$$: $$\binom{8}{2} + \binom{8}{3} = \binom{9}{3}$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-factorials-expand}

Use :ref{#binomial-theorem} to expand $$(2x + 1)^4$$, and check your answer by putting $$x = 1$$.

:::solution

Take $$a = 2x$$ and $$b = 1$$, with row $$4$$ of Pascal's triangle, $$1, 4, 6, 4, 1$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
(2x + 1)^4 &= (2x)^4 + 4(2x)^3 + 6(2x)^2 + 4(2x) + 1 \\
&= 16x^4 + 32x^3 + 24x^2 + 8x + 1
\end{aligned}
```

Check with $$x = 1$$: the left side is $$3^4 = 81$$, and the right side is
$$16 + 32 + 24 + 8 + 1 = 81$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-factorials-coefficient}

Find the number in front of $$x^3$$ when $$(x + 2)^6$$ is multiplied out, without multiplying out
the whole thing.

:::solution

By :ref{#binomial-theorem} with $$a = x$$, $$b = 2$$ and $$m = 6$$, the term with $$b^n$$ is
$$\binom{6}{n} x^{6 - n} 2^n$$. For $$x^3$$, $$6 - n = 3$$, so $$n = 3$$, and the term is

```math
\binom{6}{3} x^3 \cdot 2^3 = 20 \cdot 8 \cdot x^3 = 160x^3
```

So the number is $$160$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-factorials-alternating}

Show that for every positive integer $$m$$,

```math
\binom{m}{0} - \binom{m}{1} + \binom{m}{2} - \cdots \pm \binom{m}{m} = 0
```

where the signs alternate. Check it on row $$4$$ of Pascal's triangle.

:::solution

Take $$a = 1$$ and $$b = -1$$ in :ref{#binomial-theorem}. Every power of $$a$$ is $$1$$, and
$$(-1)^n$$ is $$1$$ for even $$n$$ and $$-1$$ for odd $$n$$, so the right side is exactly the
alternating sum. The left side is $$(1 + (-1))^m = 0^m = 0$$, since $$m$$ is positive.

Check on row $$4$$: $$1 - 4 + 6 - 4 + 1 = 0$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-factorials-absorb}

Prove that for integers $$m$$ and $$n$$ with $$1 \le n \le m$$,

```math
n \binom{m}{n} = m \binom{m - 1}{n - 1}
```

:::solution

Write both sides with :ref{#binomial}, and use $$k! = k \cdot (k - 1)!$$.

**Left side.** Since $$n! = n \cdot (n - 1)!$$, the factor $$n$$ cancels:

```math
n \binom{m}{n} = \frac{n \cdot m!}{n \cdot (n - 1)!\,(m - n)!} = \frac{m!}{(n - 1)!\,(m - n)!}
```

**Right side.** The bottom of $$\binom{m - 1}{n - 1}$$ uses $$(m - 1) - (n - 1) = m - n$$, and
$$m \cdot (m - 1)! = m!$$, so

```math
m \binom{m - 1}{n - 1} = \frac{m \cdot (m - 1)!}{(n - 1)!\,(m - n)!}
= \frac{m!}{(n - 1)!\,(m - n)!}
```

Both sides are the same fraction. For example, with $$m = 5$$ and $$n = 2$$:
$$2 \cdot 10 = 20 = 5 \cdot 4$$.

:::
