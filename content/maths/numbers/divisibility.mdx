Some questions about whole numbers can be answered without working anything out. Is
$$37 \cdot 41 + 12$$ even or odd? Can a square ever be one less than a multiple of $$4$$? Questions
like these are about how numbers split into equal parts, and this page builds the language for them:
even and odd, divisibility, remainders and common divisors. The next page,
[Congruence](./congruence.mdx), turns that language into a way of calculating.

## Even and odd

Counting up from $$1$$, the integers alternate between two kinds:

- The **even** integers $$2, 4, 6, 8, 10, \dots$$ are the ones that can be split into pairs with
  nothing left over.
- The **odd** integers $$1, 3, 5, 7, 9, \dots$$ always have one left over.

Both go up in steps of $$2$$, so they take turns along the number line. Here the even integers are
marked:

::number-line{from=-1 to=10 points="0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10"}

"Split into pairs" means the number is $$2$$ times something, and "one left over" means one more
than that.

:::definition[Even and odd]{#even-odd}

An integer is **even** if it can be written as $$2n$$ for some integer $$n$$, and **odd** if it can
be written as $$2n + 1$$ for some integer $$n$$.

:::

For example, $$14 = 2 \cdot 7$$ and $$30 = 2 \cdot 15$$ are even, while $$1 = 2 \cdot 0 + 1$$,
$$15 = 2 \cdot 7 + 1$$ and $$31 = 2 \cdot 15 + 1$$ are odd. Odd integers can equally be written as
one _less_ than an even integer, $$2n - 1$$, so $$15 = 2 \cdot 8 - 1$$. Which form to use is just a
matter of convenience.

The same definitions work for zero and the negative integers: $$0 = 2 \cdot 0$$ is even,
$$-6 = 2 \cdot (-3)$$ is even, and $$-5 = 2 \cdot (-3) + 1$$ is odd. Every integer is either even or
odd, and never both. The number line makes this look obvious, and it is proved
[below](#division-with-remainder) as part of a more general fact.

## Sums and products of even and odd integers

Whether a sum or product is even or odd depends only on whether its parts are. Think of each number
as a pile of objects split into pairs. An even pile has no object left over, and an odd pile has
one. Putting two piles together keeps all the pairs, so the only question is what happens to the
leftovers. One leftover stays on its own, so even plus odd is odd. Two leftovers form a new pair, so
odd plus odd is even: $$7 + 5$$ has pairs from each pile, and the spare $$1$$ from $$7$$ pairs up
with the spare $$1$$ from $$5$$.

For products, $$3 \cdot 5$$ means three piles of $$5$$. If any factor is even, the product is a
number of copies of an even pile, or an even number of copies of a pile, and either way it splits
into pairs. If both factors are odd, there is an odd number of leftovers, one from each odd pile, so
one is still left over at the end.

:::proposition[Adding even and odd integers]{#parity-sums}

| $$a$$ | $$b$$ | $$a + b$$ | Example        |
| ----- | ----- | --------- | -------------- |
| even  | even  | even      | $$8 + 6 = 14$$ |
| even  | odd   | odd       | $$8 + 5 = 13$$ |
| odd   | even  | odd       | $$7 + 6 = 13$$ |
| odd   | odd   | even      | $$7 + 5 = 12$$ |

:::

:::proof{source="https://www.mathsisfun.com/numbers/even-odd.html"}

Write the numbers in the forms from :ref{#even-odd}, add, and pull out a factor of $$2$$ with
distributivity. In each case $$n$$ and $$k$$ are integers, so $$n + k$$ and $$n + k + 1$$ are too.

If both are even, $$a = 2n$$ and $$b = 2k$$, then $$a + b = 2(n + k)$$, which is even.

If $$a = 2n$$ is even and $$b = 2k + 1$$ is odd, then

```math
a + b = 2n + 2k + 1 = 2(n + k) + 1
```

which is $$2$$ times an integer, plus $$1$$, so it is odd. The row with $$a$$ odd and $$b$$ even is
the same sum in the other order, since $$a + b = b + a$$.

If both are odd, $$a = 2n + 1$$ and $$b = 2k + 1$$, then the two leftover $$1$$s pair up with each
other:

```math
a + b = 2n + 1 + 2k + 1 = 2(n + k + 1)
```

which is even.

:::

:::proposition[Multiplying even and odd integers]{#parity-products}

An even integer times any integer is even, and an odd integer times an odd integer is odd.

:::

:::proof{source="https://math.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Mathematical_Logic_and_Proof/Book%3A_Mathematical_Reasoning__Writing_and_Proof_(Sundstrom)/01%3A_Introduction_to_Writing_Proofs_in_Mathematics/1.02%3A_Constructing_Direct_Proofs"}

If $$a = 2n$$ is even and $$b$$ is any integer, then regrouping the product gives
$$ab = (2n)b = 2(nb)$$, which is even. Since $$ab = ba$$, it doesn't matter which side the even
factor is on.

If $$a = 2n + 1$$ and $$b = 2k + 1$$ are both odd, multiply out the brackets and pull out a $$2$$
from every term except the last:

```math
ab = (2n + 1)(2k + 1) = 4nk + 2n + 2k + 1 = 2(2nk + n + k) + 1
```

which is odd.

:::

So a product is odd only when **every** factor is odd. For example, $$3 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 = 105$$ is
odd, but $$3 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 \cdot 2 = 210$$ is even.

## Squares

A square has the same kind as the number being squared. This is what the rule for products predicts,
since a square is a number times itself: an even number times anything is even, and an odd number
times an odd number is odd. So $$6^2$$ must be even and $$9^2$$ must be odd, without working either
out.

:::proposition[Squares of even and odd integers]{#squares-parity}

The square of an even integer is even, and the square of an odd integer is odd.

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Parity_of_Integer_equals_Parity_of_its_Square"}

We use the forms from :ref{#even-odd} and check that the square has the same form.

If $$a$$ is even, write $$a = 2n$$. Then $$a^2 = (2n)^2 = 4n^2 = 2(2n^2)$$ (:ref{#power-rules}),
which is $$2$$ times an integer, so it is even.

If $$a$$ is odd, write $$a = 2n + 1$$. Expanding with the square of a sum (:ref{#square-formulas}),

```math
a^2 = (2n + 1)^2 = 4n^2 + 4n + 1 = 2(2n^2 + 2n) + 1
```

The first two terms both contain a factor of $$2$$, so we can pull it out, leaving $$1$$ over. That
is the form of an odd integer.

:::

For example, $$6^2 = 36$$ is even and $$9^2 = 81$$ is odd. This also works backwards.

:::corollary[Numbers with even or odd squares]{#squares-parity-converse}

If $$a^2$$ is even, then $$a$$ is even. If $$a^2$$ is odd, then $$a$$ is odd.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_proof#Proof_by_contraposition"}

This uses :ref{#even-or-odd}, which also says that no integer is both. That fact is proved further
down the page, using only division with remainder, so it is safe to use here. Because $$a$$ has to
be one of the two, it is enough to rule out the wrong one.

Suppose $$a^2$$ is even. If $$a$$ were odd, then by :ref{#squares-parity} its square would be odd.
But $$a^2$$ is even, and no integer is both even and odd, so this can't happen. So $$a$$ must be
even. The same reasoning with "even" and "odd" swapped proves the second statement.

:::

This is an example of proving the contrapositive (:ref{#basic-proofs/contrapositive}): "if $$a$$ is
odd then $$a^2$$ is odd" says the same as "if $$a^2$$ is not odd then $$a$$ is not odd". So, for
example, if $$a^2 = 144$$ then without working out $$a$$ we already know it is even.

The same splitting into even and odd settles powers of $$-1$$. If $$n = 2k$$ is even, then
$$(-1)^n = ((-1)^2)^k = 1^k = 1$$. If $$n = 2k + 1$$ is odd, then
$$(-1)^n = (-1)^{2k} \cdot (-1) = -1$$. So $$(-1)^n$$ is $$1$$ when $$n$$ is even and $$-1$$ when
$$n$$ is odd.

## Divisibility

Being even means being a multiple of $$2$$. The same idea works with any number in place of $$2$$.
Saying that $$4$$ divides $$28$$ means $$28$$ objects can be put into rows of $$4$$ with none left
over. Here there are $$7$$ rows. On the number line, it means you can get from $$0$$ to $$28$$ in
equal steps of $$4$$. The definition just says this with a multiplication: $$28$$ is $$4$$ times a
whole number.

<RowsOfDots n={28} d={4} />

:::definition[Divisibility]{#divides}

An integer $$d$$ **divides** an integer $$n$$, or $$n$$ is **divisible** by $$d$$, if

```math
n = dk \qquad \text{for some integer } k
```

This is written $$d \mid n$$, and $$d$$ is called a **divisor** of $$n$$.

:::

For example:

- $$4 \mid 28$$, because $$28 = 4 \cdot 7$$.
- $$6 \mid -42$$, because $$-42 = 6 \cdot (-7)$$.
- $$5$$ does **not** divide $$12$$, because no integer $$k$$ gives $$5k = 12$$. This is written
  $$5 \nmid 12$$.

So "even" is the same as "divisible by $$2$$". A few things are always true:

- Every integer is divisible by $$1$$, because $$n = 1 \cdot n$$.
- Every integer is divisible by itself, because $$n = n \cdot 1$$.
- $$0$$ is divisible by every integer, because $$0 = d \cdot 0$$.

A common question is how many times a number can be divided by $$2$$ (or $$3$$, or anything else).
Splitting $$48$$ into factors of $$2$$ until an odd number is left gives

```math
\begin{aligned}
48 &= 2 \cdot 24 = 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 12 = 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 6 \\
&= 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 = 2^4 \cdot 3
\end{aligned}
```

so $$2^4 = 16$$ divides $$48$$, but $$2^5 = 32$$ does not. In the same way $$54 = 2 \cdot 3^3$$, so
$$3^3 = 27$$ is the largest power of $$3$$ that divides $$54$$.

## Combining multiples

If two numbers are both multiples of $$d$$, then so is anything built from them by adding,
subtracting or multiplying. Think of rows of $$7$$: $$21$$ is three rows and $$35$$ is five rows.
Put them together and you have eight rows, which is $$56$$. Take three rows away from five rows and
two rows are left, so the difference is a multiple of $$7$$ too. And four copies of three rows is
twelve rows. Nothing ever breaks a row, so the answer is always a whole number of rows.

:::lemma[Combining multiples]{#multiples}

Let $$d$$, $$m$$ and $$n$$ be integers with $$d \mid m$$ and $$d \mid n$$. Then $$d \mid m + n$$,
$$d \mid m - n$$, and $$d \mid mc$$ for any integer $$c$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://math.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Combinatorics_and_Discrete_Mathematics/Elementary_Number_Theory_(Raji)/01%3A_Introduction/1.03%3A_Divisibility_and_the_Division_Algorithm"}

The statement is about multiples of $$d$$, so the natural first step is to unpack the definition of
divisibility and write each number we know about as $$d$$ times something. Since $$d \mid m$$ and
$$d \mid n$$, there are integers $$k$$ and $$l$$ with

```math
m = dk \qquad \text{and} \qquad n = dl
```

To show that a number is divisible by $$d$$, we need to write it as $$d$$ times an integer. So in
each case we substitute and then pull out the common factor $$d$$ using distributivity:

```math
\begin{aligned}
m + n &= dk + dl = d(k + l) \\
m - n &= dk - dl = d(k - l) \\
mc &= (dk)c = d(kc)
\end{aligned}
```

The last line uses associativity to regroup the product. Because sums, differences and products of
integers are integers, $$k + l$$, $$k - l$$ and $$kc$$ are all integers. So each of $$m + n$$,
$$m - n$$ and $$mc$$ has been written as $$d$$ times an integer, which is exactly what $$d$$
dividing it means.

:::

For example, $$7 \mid 21$$ and $$7 \mid 35$$, so $$7$$ also divides $$21 + 35 = 56$$,
$$21 - 35 = -14$$ and $$21 \cdot 4 = 84$$. This lemma is used again and again on the next page.

## Division with remainder

Most numbers aren't divisible by a given $$d$$, but they always land somewhere between two multiples
of it. Picture the multiples of $$5$$ on the number line. They cut it into stretches $$5$$ steps
long, and $$17$$ lands in the stretch that starts at $$15$$, two steps in:

::number-line{from=0 to=20 unit=30 points="0, 5, 10, 15, 20" jumps="15>17: + 2"}

So $$17 = 5 \cdot 3 + 2$$: three whole fives, and $$2$$ left over.

:::theorem[Division with remainder]{#division-remainder}

Let $$n$$ and $$d$$ be integers with $$d > 0$$. Then there are integers $$q$$ and $$r$$ with

```math
n = dq + r \qquad \text{and} \qquad 0 \le r < d
```

and there is only one such pair. The number $$q$$ is called the **quotient** and $$r$$ the
**remainder** when $$n$$ is divided by $$d$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_division#Proof"}

The theorem makes two claims: that a quotient and remainder **exist**, and that there is **only
one** possible pair. We prove them separately.

**Part 1: there is such a pair.** The idea is the picture above, for any $$n$$ and $$d$$.

The multiples of $$d$$,

```math
\dots, \; -2d, \; -d, \; 0, \; d, \; 2d, \; 3d, \; \dots
```

are spaced $$d$$ apart and go on forever in both directions. Between them, they cut the whole number
line into stretches of length $$d$$, each starting at a multiple and stopping just before the next
one. Every integer $$n$$ lands in one of these stretches. This is clear from the picture, and we
take it as a basic fact about the integers rather than proving it, just as we take induction
(:ref{#basic-proofs/induction}). (Part 2 shows that $$n$$ can't land in two stretches.)

Call the multiple at the start of $$n$$'s stretch $$dq$$. In other words, $$dq$$ is the largest
multiple of $$d$$ that is not bigger than $$n$$, so

```math
dq \le n < dq + d
```

Now let $$r = n - dq$$, which is how many steps $$n$$ is past the start of its stretch. Subtracting
$$dq$$ from each part of the line above (:ref{#order-rules}) gives

```math
0 \le r < d
```

and $$r = n - dq$$ rearranges to $$n = dq + r$$. So $$q$$ and $$r$$ are the pair we wanted.

For example, with $$n = 17$$ and $$d = 5$$, the stretch containing $$17$$ runs from $$15$$ up to
(but not including) $$20$$. So $$dq = 15$$, $$q = 3$$, and $$r = 17 - 15 = 2$$.

**Part 2: there is only one pair.** First, an example of why. With $$n = 17$$ and $$d = 5$$, every
choice of quotient gives some remainder, but only one of them lands between $$0$$ and $$4$$:

| Quotient $$q$$           | $$2$$       | $$3$$ | $$4$$        |
| ------------------------ | ----------- | ----- | ------------ |
| Remainder $$17 - 5q$$    | $$7$$       | $$2$$ | $$-3$$       |
| Between $$0$$ and $$4$$? | no, too big | yes   | no, negative |

Taking one fewer $$5$$ leaves too much over, and taking one more $$5$$ overshoots. The proof shows
the same thing happens in general, using the stretches from Part 1.

Suppose $$n = dq + r$$ for some integers $$q$$ and $$r$$ with $$0 \le r < d$$. This could be any
such pair, not necessarily the one from Part 1. The two facts about $$r$$ say where $$n$$ sits:

- $$r \ge 0$$ means $$n = dq + r$$ is **at least** $$dq$$.
- $$r < d$$ means $$n = dq + r$$ is **less than** $$dq + d$$.

Together, $$dq \le n < dq + d$$: $$n$$ lies in the stretch that starts at the multiple $$dq$$.

So any valid pair puts $$n$$ in a stretch. The last thing to check is that $$n$$ can't be in two
different stretches, because the stretches don't overlap. Suppose two pairs put $$n$$ in the
stretches starting at $$dq$$ and $$dq'$$, so

```math
dq \le n < dq + d \qquad \text{and} \qquad dq' \le n < dq' + d
```

If $$q < q'$$, then $$q' \ge q + 1$$, because there is no integer strictly between $$q$$ and
$$q + 1$$. From this, $$dq' \ge d(q + 1) = dq + d$$:

- if $$q' = q + 1$$, then $$dq' = d(q + 1)$$, so the two sides are equal;
- if $$q' > q + 1$$, then multiplying by the positive number $$d$$ keeps the inequality
  (:ref{#order-multiply}), so $$dq' > d(q + 1)$$.

In words, the second stretch starts at or after the point where the first one ends. But $$n$$ is
before the end of the first stretch and at or after the start of the second:

```math
n < dq + d \le dq' \le n
```

which says $$n < n$$, which is impossible. Swapping the roles of the two pairs rules out $$q' < q$$
in the same way. So $$q = q'$$: every valid pair has the same quotient.

Once $$q$$ is fixed, so is $$r$$, because it has to be $$n - dq$$. So there is only one pair.

:::

For a negative number the remainder is still between $$0$$ and $$d - 1$$:
$$-17 = 5 \cdot (-4) + 3$$, so the remainder is $$3$$, not $$-2$$. And $$d \mid n$$ says exactly
that the remainder is $$0$$.

Dividing by $$2$$ settles the claim from the start of the page.

:::corollary[Every integer is even or odd]{#even-or-odd}

Every integer is either even or odd, and not both.

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Equivalence_of_Definitions_of_Odd_Integer"}

Take :ref{#division-remainder} with $$d = 2$$. The remainder is between $$0$$ and $$1$$, so every
integer $$n$$ can be written as

```math
n = 2q + 0 \qquad \text{or} \qquad n = 2q + 1
```

The first form says $$n$$ is even and the second says $$n$$ is odd (:ref{#even-odd}), so $$n$$ is at
least one of them.

It can't be both. If $$n = 2k$$ and also $$n = 2m + 1$$, then $$n$$ would have two different
remainders, $$0$$ and $$1$$, when divided by $$2$$. The theorem says there is only one.

:::

In the same way, dividing by $$3$$ sorts every integer into exactly one of three kinds,

```math
3k, \qquad 3k + 1 \qquad \text{or} \qquad 3k + 2
```

and splitting into these cases is a common way to prove things about multiples of $$3$$.

## Common divisors

Two numbers often have divisors in common. For example, $$36$$ and $$60$$ are both multiples of
$$12$$: one is $$3$$ twelves and the other is $$5$$ twelves. A common divisor is a step size that
reaches both numbers exactly from $$0$$. The greatest one is the biggest step size that does this.
It is the largest factor that can be taken out of both at once, which is what is needed to simplify
a fraction like $$\tfrac{36}{60}$$ all the way down to $$\tfrac{3}{5}$$ in one go.

:::definition[Common divisors]{#gcd}

A **common divisor** of two integers is an integer that divides both of them. For positive integers
$$m$$ and $$n$$, the largest of their common divisors is their **greatest common divisor**.

:::

For example, the positive divisors of $$36$$ are $$1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36$$, and those of
$$60$$ are $$1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20, 30, 60$$. The common ones are $$1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12$$,
so the greatest common divisor of $$36$$ and $$60$$ is $$12$$.

There always is a greatest one. A positive divisor $$d$$ of a positive integer $$m$$ is never bigger
than $$m$$, because $$m = dk$$ with $$k \ge 1$$, so $$m \ge d$$. That leaves only finitely many
candidates, and $$1$$ is always one of them. When the greatest common divisor is $$1$$, the two
numbers share no factor worth cancelling; that is what lowest form means for fractions, on the
[Rational numbers](./rational-numbers.mdx) page.
