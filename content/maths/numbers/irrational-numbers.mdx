---
title: Irrational numbers
description: Why no fraction squares to 2.
---

Draw a square with sides $$1$$ cm long and measure its diagonal. You'll get a little over $$1.4$$
cm. The [Pythagoras theorem](../distance-and-angles/pythagoras.mdx), proved in a later chapter, says
the diagonal squared is exactly $$1^2 + 1^2 = 2$$.

Fractions are packed densely along the number line, with another between any two, so it's natural to
expect one of them to be this length. But no fraction squares to exactly $$2$$, and in this guide
we'll prove it. So there are lengths that no fraction can describe.

## Hunting for a square root of 2

The question is whether some rational number has square $$2$$. Such a number would be a **square
root** of $$2$$, written $$\sqrt{2}$$. Since $$1^2 = 1$$ is too small and $$2^2 = 4$$ is too big, a
positive square root would lie between $$1$$ and $$2$$. You can hunt for it one decimal place at a
time:

| Try       | Square       |           |
| --------- | ------------ | --------- |
| $$1.4$$   | $$1.96$$     | too small |
| $$1.5$$   | $$2.25$$     | too big   |
| $$1.41$$  | $$1.9881$$   | too small |
| $$1.42$$  | $$2.0164$$   | too big   |
| $$1.414$$ | $$1.999396$$ | too small |
| $$1.415$$ | $$2.002225$$ | too big   |

Each step pins the answer down to one more decimal place, and the squares get closer and closer to
$$2$$. But none of them ever hits $$2$$ exactly, and the next theorem shows that no amount of
searching ever will.

## The proof

The idea is that a fraction squaring to $$2$$ could be simplified forever. Suppose some fraction did
square to $$2$$. The argument below shows that its top and bottom would both have to be even. Then
you could cancel a $$2$$ from each, giving a fraction with smaller top and bottom that still squares
to $$2$$. Its top and bottom would again both be even, so you could cancel again, and so on without
end. But positive whole numbers can't be halved forever, so no such fraction can exist. The proof
below shortens this by starting with a fraction that has already been simplified as far as it can
go.

:::theorem[No rational square root of 2]{#sqrt-two}

There is no rational number whose square is $$2$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_root_of_2#Proof_by_infinite_descent"}

The argument is by contradiction (:ref{#basic-proofs/contradiction}): suppose such a number exists,
and show that this leads to something impossible. The plan is:

1. write the number as a fraction $$\frac{m}{n}$$ in lowest form, so $$m$$ and $$n$$ are not both
   even;
2. show that $$m$$ is even;
3. show that $$n$$ is even too, which contradicts step 1.

**Step 1: a fraction in lowest form.** Suppose some rational number $$x$$ has $$x^2 = 2$$. It isn't
$$0$$, because $$0^2 = 0$$. If it is negative, then $$-x$$ is positive, and
$$(-x)^2 = (-x)(-x) = x \cdot x = 2$$ by :ref{#two-minus}. So either way there is a **positive**
rational number whose square is $$2$$.

By :ref{#lowest-form-exists}, you can write this positive number in lowest form as $$\frac{m}{n}$$,
with $$m$$ and $$n$$ positive integers. So $$m$$ and $$n$$ are **not both even**
(:ref{#not-both-even}). This is the fact the proof will contradict.

**Step 2: $$m$$ is even.** Squaring with :ref{#fraction-powers},

```math
\frac{m^2}{n^2} = 2 = \frac{2}{1}
```

and cross-multiplying (:ref{#cross-multiplying}) gives $$m^2 \cdot 1 = 2 \cdot n^2$$, that is,

```math
m^2 = 2n^2
```

The right-hand side is $$2$$ times an integer, so $$m^2$$ is even. By
:ref{#squares-parity-converse}, an integer with an even square is itself even, so $$m$$ is even.

**Step 3: $$n$$ is even.** Since $$m$$ is even, you can write $$m = 2k$$ for some integer $$k$$.
Substituting this into $$m^2 = 2n^2$$,

```math
(2k)^2 = 4k^2 = 2n^2
```

Writing the left side as $$2 \cdot 2k^2$$ and cancelling the factor $$2$$ from both sides
(:ref{#cancel-product}) gives $$n^2 = 2k^2$$. So $$n^2$$ is even, and by
:ref{#squares-parity-converse} again, $$n$$ is even.

**The contradiction.** Steps 2 and 3 show that $$m$$ and $$n$$ are both even, but step 1 says they
are not both even. So the supposition was false, and no rational number has square $$2$$.

:::

## Irrational numbers

The theorem shows that fractions are not enough to measure every length. The diagonal of a square
with sides of length $$1$$ is a perfectly real length, but no fraction describes it. Numbers like
this need a name.

:::definition[Irrational numbers]{#irrational}

A number that is not rational is called **irrational**.

:::

So if there is a number whose square is $$2$$, it must be irrational. The diagonal of the unit
square says there should be one, and its decimal starts $$1.41421356\ldots$$ without ever stopping
or repeating. Making sense of numbers like this, and of their endless decimals, needs the **real
numbers**, which fill in all the gaps between the fractions. They are the subject of the
[Real numbers](../real-numbers/introduction.mdx) chapter.

## Other square roots

What made the proof work was one fact about $$2$$: if $$2$$ divides a square, it divides the number
being squared. The number $$3$$ has the same property, so exactly the same argument works for it.
For $$3$$, the proof uses :ref{#squares-three} in place of :ref{#squares-parity-converse}. The
argument must break down somewhere for $$4$$, since $$\sqrt{4} = 2$$ is rational, and it breaks at
exactly this fact: $$4$$ divides $$2^2 = 4$$ but does not divide $$2$$.

:::proposition[No rational square root of 3]{#sqrt-three}

There is no rational number whose square is $$3$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://www.cuemath.com/algebra/prove-that-root-3-is-irrational/#Prove-That-Root-3-is-Irrational-by-Contradiction-Method"}

The plan is the same as for :ref{#sqrt-two}, with "is a multiple of $$3$$" in place of "is even".

**A fraction in lowest form.** Suppose some rational number squares to $$3$$. As in the proof of
:ref{#sqrt-two}, you can take it to be positive and write it in lowest form as $$\frac{m}{n}$$ with
$$m$$ and $$n$$ positive integers (:ref{#lowest-form-exists}). In lowest form the only positive
common divisor of $$m$$ and $$n$$ is $$1$$, so $$3$$ does **not** divide both of them.

**$$m$$ is a multiple of $$3$$.** Squaring and cross-multiplying as in the proof of :ref{#sqrt-two}
gives

```math
m^2 = 3n^2
```

so $$3 \mid m^2$$, and by :ref{#squares-three}, $$3 \mid m$$.

**$$n$$ is a multiple of $$3$$.** Write $$m = 3k$$ for some integer $$k$$. Substituting,
$$9k^2 = 3n^2$$, that is, $$3 \cdot 3k^2 = 3n^2$$. Cancelling the factor $$3$$
(:ref{#cancel-product}) gives $$n^2 = 3k^2$$. So $$3 \mid n^2$$, and by :ref{#squares-three} again,
$$3 \mid n$$.

**The contradiction.** Now $$3$$ divides both $$m$$ and $$n$$, which contradicts the fraction being
in lowest form. So no rational number has square $$3$$.

:::

The same method shows that $$\sqrt{5}$$, $$\sqrt{6}$$ and $$\sqrt{7}$$ are irrational
(:ref{#ex-sqrt-six} does $$\sqrt{6}$$). In fact the square root of a positive integer is rational
exactly when the integer is a perfect square like $$4$$ or $$9$$. Proving that in general needs
facts about prime numbers that these guides don't cover.

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on irrational numbers, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-irrational-numbers-hunt-three}

Hunt for a square root of $$3$$ as the table did for $$2$$. Find two decimals with two decimal
places, $$0.01$$ apart, such that the square of the first is too small and the square of the second
is too big.

:::solution

Since $$1^2 = 1$$ and $$2^2 = 4$$, a positive square root of $$3$$ lies between $$1$$ and $$2$$. One
decimal place at a time:

| Try      | Square     |           |
| -------- | ---------- | --------- |
| $$1.7$$  | $$2.89$$   | too small |
| $$1.8$$  | $$3.24$$   | too big   |
| $$1.73$$ | $$2.9929$$ | too small |
| $$1.74$$ | $$3.0276$$ | too big   |

So the answer is $$1.73$$ and $$1.74$$. By :ref{#sqrt-three}, no fraction squares to exactly $$3$$,
so this search, like the one for $$2$$, never ends.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-irrational-numbers-four}

The number $$4$$ has the rational square root $$2$$, so the proof of :ref{#sqrt-two} must fail if
you copy it with $$4$$ in place of $$2$$. Try it: suppose $$\frac{m}{n}$$ is in lowest form with
$$m^2 = 4n^2$$, and find the step that breaks.

:::solution

Copying the proof, the first step would be "$$4$$ divides $$m^2$$, so $$4$$ divides $$m$$". That
step is false. For example $$m = 2$$: $$4$$ divides $$m^2 = 4$$, but not $$m = 2$$.

In fact $$m = 2$$ and $$n = 1$$ give a fraction in lowest form with $$m^2 = 4 = 4n^2$$, so there is
nothing to contradict. The proofs for $$2$$ and $$3$$ work because those numbers do have the
property: if they divide a square, they divide the number being squared
(:ref{#squares-parity-converse} and :ref{#squares-three}).

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-irrational-numbers-sqrt-five}

Take as given that if $$5$$ divides $$m^2$$, then $$5$$ divides $$m$$, for any integer $$m$$. (This
follows from facts about prime numbers that these guides don't cover.) Prove that no rational number
has square $$5$$.

:::solution

Argue by contradiction (:ref{#basic-proofs/contradiction}), following :ref{#sqrt-three}.

**A fraction in lowest form.** Suppose some rational number squares to $$5$$. Its opposite squares
to $$5$$ too, by :ref{#two-minus}, and $$0^2 = 0$$, so there is a positive one. Write it in lowest
form as $$\frac{m}{n}$$ with $$m$$ and $$n$$ positive integers (:ref{#lowest-form-exists}). Then
$$5$$ does not divide both $$m$$ and $$n$$.

**$$5$$ divides $$m$$.** Squaring (:ref{#fraction-powers}) and cross-multiplying
(:ref{#cross-multiplying}) gives $$m^2 = 5n^2$$. So $$5 \mid m^2$$, and by the given fact,
$$5 \mid m$$.

**$$5$$ divides $$n$$.** Write $$m = 5k$$. Then $$25k^2 = 5n^2$$, that is
$$5 \cdot 5k^2 = 5 \cdot n^2$$, and cancelling the $$5$$ (:ref{#cancel-product}) gives
$$n^2 = 5k^2$$. So $$5 \mid n^2$$, and $$5 \mid n$$.

**The contradiction.** Now $$5$$ divides both $$m$$ and $$n$$, against the lowest form. So no
rational number has square $$5$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-irrational-numbers-reciprocal}

Let $$a$$ be a number with $$a^2 = 2$$. Show that $$\frac{1}{a}$$ is irrational.

:::solution

Argue by contradiction. First, $$a \neq 0$$, since $$0^2 = 0$$, so $$\frac{1}{a}$$ makes sense.
Suppose $$\frac{1}{a} = r$$ for some rational number $$r$$. Then $$ar = 1$$, so $$r \neq 0$$.

Every non-zero rational number has a rational inverse (:ref{#inverse-exists}), and $$ar = 1$$ says
that $$a$$ is an inverse of $$r$$. Inverses are unique (:ref{#inverse-unique}), so $$a = r^{-1}$$,
which is rational. But :ref{#sqrt-two} says no rational number squares to $$2$$. So $$\frac{1}{a}$$
is irrational.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-irrational-numbers-closed}

True or false? Give a reason or a counterexample (:ref{#basic-proofs/counterexample}).

1. The product of two irrational numbers is always irrational.
2. The sum of two irrational numbers is always irrational.

:::solution

Let $$a$$ be a number with $$a^2 = 2$$, which is irrational if it exists (:ref{#sqrt-two}).

1. False. The product $$a \cdot a = 2$$ is rational.
2. False. The number $$-a$$ is irrational too: if $$-a$$ were rational, its opposite $$a$$ would be
   rational. But $$a + (-a) = 0$$, which is rational.

So adding or multiplying two irrational numbers can give a rational number. Two rational numbers
always give a rational number.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-irrational-numbers-cube-root}

Prove that no rational number has cube $$2$$.

:::solution

Argue by contradiction, as in :ref{#sqrt-two}, with cubes in place of squares.

**A fraction in lowest form.** Suppose $$x^3 = 2$$ for a rational number $$x$$. Then $$x$$ is
positive: $$0^3 = 0$$, and a negative number cubed is negative, since $$x \cdot x$$ is positive
(:ref{#two-minus}) and a positive times a negative is negative (:ref{#minus-in-product}). Write
$$x = \frac{m}{n}$$ in lowest form (:ref{#lowest-form-exists}), so $$m$$ and $$n$$ are not both even
(:ref{#not-both-even}). Cubing and cross-multiplying gives $$m^3 = 2n^3$$.

**$$m$$ is even.** An odd number times an odd number is odd (:ref{#parity-products}), so if $$m$$
were odd, $$m^2$$ and then $$m^3 = m^2 \cdot m$$ would be odd. But $$m^3 = 2n^3$$ is even. Every
integer is even or odd (:ref{#even-or-odd}), so $$m$$ is even.

**$$n$$ is even.** Write $$m = 2k$$. Then $$8k^3 = 2n^3$$, and cancelling $$2$$
(:ref{#cancel-product}) gives $$n^3 = 4k^3 = 2 \cdot 2k^3$$, which is even. By the same argument,
$$n$$ is even.

**The contradiction.** Both $$m$$ and $$n$$ are even, against the lowest form. So no rational number
has cube $$2$$.

:::
