Imagine you are a mathematician in sixteenth-century Italy, holding a new formula for solving cubic
equations. You try it on $$x^3 = 15x + 4$$, which you know has the solution $$x = 4$$, because
$$4^3 = 64$$ and $$15 \cdot 4 + 4 = 64$$ too. Halfway through, the formula asks you for
$$\sqrt{-121}$$, the square root of a negative number. No real number squares to $$-121$$, and yet
the right answer is waiting at the end of the calculation.

Gerolamo Cardano, who published the formula in 1545, met square roots like this one and could not
get past them. In 1572 Rafael Bombelli did, on this equation: he calculated with square roots of
negative numbers as if they obeyed the usual rules, and they cancelled out to leave $$4$$. Numbers
built this way are called **complex numbers**. In this guide we'll build them as points of the
plane, find the rule for multiplying them that the square of $$-1$$ forces on us, and prove that
they obey the rules of arithmetic you already use.

## No real number squares to −1

Square any real number and you land at $$0$$ or to the right of it on the number line: $$2^2 = 4$$,
$$(-2)^2 = 4$$, and $$(-0.1)^2 = 0.01$$. That is :ref{#real-numbers/squares-positive}: a square is
never negative. So the graph of $$y = x^2$$ never dips below the $$x$$-axis.

<SquaresNeverNegative caption="The graph of y = x² stays on or above the x-axis, because a square is never negative. So it never meets the dashed line at height −1, and no real number x has x² = −1." />

So the equation $$x^2 + 1 = 0$$, which says $$x^2 = -1$$, has no real solution. The number line has
run out of room. The same thing has happened before: $$2x = 3$$ has no solution among the integers,
and fractions were brought in to solve it. Now we need a larger system that contains a number whose
square is $$-1$$, and we'll find room for it by stepping off the number line into the plane.

The chapter on quadratics promised exactly this, in
[The discriminant](../quadratics/discriminant.mdx). The guide
[Every quadratic has solutions](./every-quadratic.mdx) keeps that promise.

## What multiplying must be

Suppose for a moment that there were a number $$i$$ with $$i^2 = -1$$, and that it obeyed the usual
rules of arithmetic. Then we could multiply expressions like $$1 + 2i$$ and $$3 - i$$ by multiplying
out the brackets:

```math
\begin{aligned}
(1 + 2i)(3 - i) &= 3 - i + 6i - 2i^2 &&\qquad \text{multiply out the brackets} \\
&= 3 + 5i - 2i^2 &&\qquad \text{collect the terms in } i \\
&= 3 + 5i + 2 &&\qquad i^2 = -1 \\
&= 5 + 5i &&\qquad \text{collect the real numbers}
\end{aligned}
```

Each of the two numbers we started with is made of two real numbers, $$1$$ and $$2$$, then $$3$$ and
$$-1$$, and so is the answer, $$5$$ and $$5$$. The same steps work for any $$a + bi$$ and $$c + di$$
with $$a$$, $$b$$, $$c$$ and $$d$$ real:

```math
\begin{aligned}
(a + bi)(c + di) &= ac + adi + bci + bdi^2 &&\qquad \text{multiply out the brackets} \\
&= (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i &&\qquad i^2 = -1, \text{ and collect terms}
\end{aligned}
```

So if such numbers exist at all, there is no choice about how to multiply them. But wishing for a
number $$i$$ doesn't make one exist. Instead we build it: we take pairs of real numbers, define
adding and multiplying by the formulas we found above, and then prove that $$i^2 = -1$$ and the
usual rules hold.

## Complex numbers as points

A pair $$(a, b)$$ of real numbers is a point of the coordinate plane
(:ref{#coordinates/coordinate-plane}). So the complex numbers are the points of the plane, with one
new thing you can do to them: multiply.

:::definition[Complex number]{#complex-number}

A **complex number** is a pair $$(a, b)$$ of real numbers, that is, a point of the plane. The set of
complex numbers is written $$\mathbb{C}$$. Two complex numbers $$(a, b)$$ and $$(c, d)$$ are equal
exactly when $$a = c$$ and $$b = d$$. They are added and multiplied by the rules

```math
(a, b) + (c, d) = (a + c, b + d) \qquad\qquad (a, b)(c, d) = (ac - bd, ad + bc)
```

:::

The sum is the sum of the two points from :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/point-sum}, so adding
complex numbers is taking one step and then another. The product is the new part. With the pairs
from the calculation above,

```math
(1, 2)(3, -1) = \big(1 \cdot 3 - 2 \cdot (-1),\ 1 \cdot (-1) + 2 \cdot 3\big) = (5, 5)
```

which matches $$(1 + 2i)(3 - i) = 5 + 5i$$. The picture below shows the three points, with an arc
for the angle of each. The product looks unrelated to the two numbers at first, but measure it. Its
length is the two lengths multiplied, and its angle is the two angles added.

<MultiplyPreview caption="The points 1 + 2i (blue), 3 − i (orange) and their product 5 + 5i (purple). The lengths are √5 ≈ 2.24, √10 ≈ 3.16 and √50 ≈ 7.07, and √5 × √10 = √50. The angles from the positive real axis are about 63°, about −18°, and 45°, and 63° − 18° = 45°." />

That is the idea that carries this whole chapter: adding complex numbers slides points, and
multiplying turns by the angle and stretches by the length. The guide
[Multiplying is turning](./polar-form.mdx) proves it. Here we stay with the pairs.

## Real numbers among the complex numbers

The real numbers sit inside the complex numbers as the points $$(a, 0)$$ of the horizontal axis.
Adding or multiplying two of them gives the same answer as adding or multiplying the real numbers,
so nothing is lost by writing $$a$$ for $$(a, 0)$$. For example, $$(2, 0)(5, 0) = (10 - 0, 0 + 0)$$,
which is $$(10, 0)$$.

:::proposition[Real numbers inside the complex numbers]{#complex-reals}

For all real numbers $$a$$, $$c$$ and $$d$$:

1. $$(a, 0) + (c, 0) = (a + c, 0)$$
2. $$(a, 0)(c, 0) = (ac, 0)$$
3. $$(a, 0)(c, d) = (ac, ad)$$, which is the multiple $$a(c, d)$$ of the point $$(c, d)$$

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Real_Numbers_form_Subfield_of_Complex_Numbers"}

Each part is the definition of :ref{#complex-number} with $$b = 0$$, together with $$0 \cdot x = 0$$
for real numbers (:ref{#numbers/zero-times}).

1. $$(a, 0) + (c, 0) = (a + c, 0 + 0) = (a + c, 0)$$.
2. $$(a, 0)(c, 0) = (ac - 0 \cdot 0, a \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot c) = (ac, 0)$$.
3. $$(a, 0)(c, d) = (ac - 0 \cdot d, ad + 0 \cdot c) = (ac, ad)$$, and $$(ac, ad)$$ is $$a(c, d)$$
   by :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/scalar-multiple}.

:::

From now on we write $$a$$ for the complex number $$(a, 0)$$. In particular $$0$$ is $$(0, 0)$$ and
$$1$$ is $$(1, 0)$$. Part 3 says that multiplying a complex number by a real number scales the
point: $$2(3, -1) = (6, -2)$$ is twice as far from $$0$$, in the same direction.

## The number i

The point one step up from $$0$$ is the number we were wishing for.

:::definition[The number i]{#i}

The complex number $$i$$ is the point $$(0, 1)$$.

:::

:::proposition[i squared]{#i-squared}

1. $$i^2 = -1$$.
2. Every complex number $$(a, b)$$ equals $$a + bi$$. If $$a + bi = c + di$$, with $$a$$, $$b$$,
   $$c$$ and $$d$$ real, then $$a = c$$ and $$b = d$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imaginary_unit#Definition"}

**Part 1.** By the rule for multiplying,

```math
i^2 = (0, 1)(0, 1) = (0 \cdot 0 - 1 \cdot 1,\ 0 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 0) = (-1, 0)
```

and $$(-1, 0)$$ is the real number $$-1$$.

**Part 2.** By part 3 of :ref{#complex-reals},
$$bi = (b, 0)(0, 1) = (b \cdot 0, b \cdot 1) = (0, b)$$. So

```math
a + bi = (a, 0) + (0, b) = (a + 0, 0 + b) = (a, b)
```

If $$a + bi = c + di$$, this says $$(a, b) = (c, d)$$, so $$a = c$$ and $$b = d$$ by the meaning of
equality in :ref{#complex-number}.

:::

Multiplying by $$i$$ has a picture. Multiplying $$1$$ by $$i$$ gives $$i$$, a quarter turn
anticlockwise about $$0$$. Multiplying $$i$$ by $$i$$ gives $$-1$$, a second quarter turn. Two
quarter turns make a half turn, and a half turn takes $$1$$ to $$-1$$, which is why $$i^2 = -1$$.

<TimesITurns caption="Multiplying by i turns a quarter turn anticlockwise about 0: it takes 1 to i, and then i to −1. Two quarter turns make a half turn, so i · i = −1." />

One slip catches nearly everyone once. Since a square is never negative, it can feel as though
$$i^2$$ ought to be $$1$$. But that rule is about real numbers, and $$i$$ is not one. Its square is
whatever the rule for multiplying says, and the rule says $$-1$$.

Part 2 lets us write every complex number as $$a + bi$$, and that is how we'll write them from now
on. So $$3 - i$$ is the point $$(3, -1)$$, the number $$5i$$ is $$(0, 5)$$, and $$-2$$ is
$$(-2, 0)$$. Two complex numbers written this way are equal exactly when their real numbers match
and their numbers in front of $$i$$ match.

## The rules of arithmetic

When we multiplied out $$(1 + 2i)(3 - i)$$ we assumed the usual rules: that brackets can be
multiplied out, and that order and grouping don't matter. Now that complex numbers are defined, each
of those rules has to be checked against the definition. They all hold, so every calculation with
complex numbers can go as it does with real numbers.

:::theorem[Complex numbers obey the rules of arithmetic]{#complex-arithmetic}

For all complex numbers $$z$$, $$w$$ and $$u$$:

1. **Order doesn't matter:** $$z + w = w + z$$ and $$zw = wz$$.
2. **Grouping doesn't matter:** $$(z + w) + u = z + (w + u)$$ and $$(zw)u = z(wu)$$.
3. **Zero and one:** $$z + 0 = z$$ and $$1 \cdot z = z$$, and $$0 \ne 1$$.
4. **Opposites:** the number $$-z = (-a, -b)$$, for $$z = (a, b)$$, has $$z + (-z) = 0$$.
5. **Distributivity:** $$z(w + u) = zw + zu$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Complex_Numbers_form_Field"}

Write $$z = (a, b)$$, $$w = (c, d)$$ and $$u = (e, f)$$. Every step inside a pair is a step with
real numbers, allowed by :ref{#real-numbers/real-rules}.

**Adding.** Adding complex numbers is adding points, so the parts about $$+$$ in rules 1 to 4 are
the four parts of :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/sum-rules}, with $$O = (0, 0) = 0$$.

**Order of multiplying.** $$zw = (ac - bd, ad + bc)$$ and $$wz = (ca - db, cb + da)$$. These are
equal, because $$ac = ca$$, $$bd = db$$, $$ad = da$$ and $$bc = cb$$.

**One, and zero is not one.** By part 3 of :ref{#complex-reals}, $$1 \cdot z = (1, 0)(a, b)$$ is
$$(1 \cdot a, 1 \cdot b) = (a, b)$$. And $$0 = (0, 0)$$ differs from $$1 = (1, 0)$$, since their
first parts $$0$$ and $$1$$ differ.

**Grouping of multiplying.** Work out $$(zw)u$$ first, using $$zw = (ac - bd, ad + bc)$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
(zw)u &= (ac - bd, ad + bc)(e, f) &&\qquad \text{the product } zw \\
&= \big((ac - bd)e - (ad + bc)f,\ (ac - bd)f + (ad + bc)e\big) &&\qquad \text{rule for multiplying} \\
&= (ace - bde - adf - bcf,\ acf - bdf + ade + bce) &&\qquad \text{multiply out each part}
\end{aligned}
```

Now $$z(wu)$$, using $$wu = (ce - df, cf + de)$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
z(wu) &= (a, b)(ce - df, cf + de) &&\qquad \text{the product } wu \\
&= \big(a(ce - df) - b(cf + de),\ a(cf + de) + b(ce - df)\big) &&\qquad \text{rule for multiplying} \\
&= (ace - adf - bcf - bde,\ acf + ade + bce - bdf) &&\qquad \text{multiply out each part}
\end{aligned}
```

The first parts are made of the same four terms, $$ace$$, $$-bde$$, $$-adf$$ and $$-bcf$$, in a
different order, and so are the second parts. So $$(zw)u = z(wu)$$.

**Distributivity.** Here $$w + u = (c + e, d + f)$$, so

```math
\begin{aligned}
z(w + u) &= (a, b)(c + e, d + f) &&\qquad \text{the sum } w + u \\
&= \big(a(c + e) - b(d + f),\ a(d + f) + b(c + e)\big) &&\qquad \text{rule for multiplying} \\
&= (ac + ae - bd - bf,\ ad + af + bc + be) &&\qquad \text{multiply out each part}
\end{aligned}
```

On the other side, $$zw = (ac - bd, ad + bc)$$ and $$zu = (ae - bf, af + be)$$, so

```math
zw + zu = (ac - bd + ae - bf,\ ad + bc + af + be)
```

These have the same terms as before in each part, so $$z(w + u) = zw + zu$$.

:::

So the steps we took on trust at the start are allowed: to calculate with complex numbers, use the
usual rules and replace $$i^2$$ by $$-1$$ wherever it appears. Every result that the Numbers chapter
proved from these rules alone holds for complex numbers too, with the same proof. For example:

- $$0 \cdot z = 0$$ (:ref{#numbers/zero-times}), and $$(-1)z = -z$$ (:ref{#numbers/minus-one})
- $$(z + w)^2 = z^2 + 2zw + w^2$$, and the other formulas in :ref{#numbers/square-formulas}
- the rules for powers, :ref{#numbers/power-rules}, where $$z^n$$ means $$n$$ copies of $$z$$
  multiplied together, and $$z^0 = 1$$

The rules for real numbers in :ref{#real-numbers/real-rules} include one more: every number except
$$0$$ has an inverse. For complex numbers, that comes in [The complex plane](./complex-plane.mdx).
Results about positive and negative numbers don't carry over, because a complex number like $$i$$ is
never called positive or negative. Indeed $$i^2 = -1$$ shows that "a square is never negative" fails
for complex numbers.

## The square roots of −1

The number $$i$$ squares to $$-1$$, and so does $$-i$$, since $$(-i)^2 = (-i)(-i) = i \cdot i = -1$$
by :ref{#numbers/two-minus}. These two are the only ones. To see why, square a general $$a + bi$$
and compare its two parts with those of $$-1 = -1 + 0i$$.

:::proposition[The square roots of −1]{#square-roots-minus-one}

For a complex number $$z$$, $$z^2 = -1$$ exactly when $$z = i$$ or $$z = -i$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imaginary_unit"}

Both $$i$$ and $$-i$$ square to $$-1$$, as shown above. Now suppose $$z^2 = -1$$, and write
$$z = a + bi$$ with $$a$$ and $$b$$ real. By the rule for multiplying,

```math
z^2 = (a + bi)(a + bi) = (a^2 - b^2) + 2abi
```

This equals $$-1 + 0i$$, so by part 2 of :ref{#i-squared}, $$a^2 - b^2 = -1$$ and $$2ab = 0$$.

From $$2ab = 0$$, either $$a = 0$$ or $$b = 0$$, by :ref{#numbers/zero-product} for real numbers. If
$$b = 0$$, the first equation says $$a^2 = -1$$, which no real number does, by
:ref{#real-numbers/squares-positive}. So $$a = 0$$, and the first equation says $$-b^2 = -1$$, that
is, $$b^2 = 1$$. By :ref{#real-numbers/square-root-count}, $$b = 1$$ or $$b = -1$$. So $$z = i$$ or
$$z = -i$$.

:::

The proof compares parts, so it only needs facts about real numbers. There is a second proof, by
factoring $$z^2 + 1 = (z - i)(z + i)$$. It needs the fact that a product of complex numbers is $$0$$
exactly when one of the factors is, and that fact is :ref{#complex-zero-product}, proved in the next
guide once we can divide.

The same comparison of parts finds the square roots of every negative real number. For instance,
$$(2i)^2 = 4i^2 = -4$$ and $$(-2i)^2 = -4$$, so $$-4$$ has the two square roots $$2i$$ and $$-2i$$.

## Square roots of negative numbers: a warning

For a real number $$a \ge 0$$, the symbol $$\sqrt{a}$$ means the one square root that is not
negative (:ref{#real-numbers/square-root}). A negative number has two square roots, like $$2i$$ and
$$-2i$$ for $$-4$$, and neither of them is positive, so there is no rule that picks one. This
chapter doesn't give $$\sqrt{-4}$$ a meaning.

People who write $$\sqrt{-4} = 2i$$ anyway soon meet trouble with the rule
$$\sqrt{a}\,\sqrt{b} = \sqrt{ab}$$. Taking $$\sqrt{-4} = 2i$$ and $$\sqrt{-9} = 3i$$, the left side
is $$2i \cdot 3i = 6i^2 = -6$$. But the right side is $$\sqrt{(-4)(-9)} = \sqrt{36} = 6$$. The rule
is :ref{#real-numbers/root-product}, and it is proved only for $$a \ge 0$$ and $$b \ge 0$$. Outside
that range it fails, so don't use it there.

The same slip gives a false argument that $$i^2 = 1$$:

```math
i^2 = \sqrt{-1}\,\sqrt{-1} = \sqrt{(-1)(-1)} = \sqrt{1} = 1
```

The second step is the product rule used on negative numbers. Bombelli's $$\sqrt{-121}$$ is a square
root of $$-121$$, one of $$11i$$ and $$-11i$$.

## Powers of i

Each multiplication by $$i$$ is a quarter turn, and four quarter turns bring you back where you
started. So the powers of $$i$$ should go round four points and repeat. Working them out one at a
time, each from the one before:

```math
\begin{aligned}
i^1 &= i \\
i^2 &= -1 &&\qquad \text{part 1 of the proposition i squared} \\
i^3 &= i^2 \cdot i = (-1)i = -i &&\qquad \text{multiplying by } -1 \\
i^4 &= i^3 \cdot i = (-i)i = -i^2 = 1 &&\qquad \text{moving the minus sign} \\
i^5 &= i^4 \cdot i = 1 \cdot i = i &&\qquad \text{back to the start}
\end{aligned}
```

The fourth line uses :ref{#numbers/minus-in-product}. From $$i^5$$ on, the pattern $$i$$, $$-1$$,
$$-i$$, $$1$$ repeats.

<PowersOfI caption="The powers of i go round the four points 1, i, −1 and −i, one quarter turn each time. Each point is labelled with the powers of i that land on it." />

So to find a power of $$i$$, you only need to know where you are in the cycle of four, which is the
remainder when the exponent is divided by $$4$$.

:::proposition[Powers of i]{#powers-of-i}

Let $$n \ge 0$$ be a whole number, and write $$n = 4q + r$$ with $$0 \le r < 4$$, as in
:ref{#numbers/division-remainder}. Then $$i^n = i^r$$. So $$i^n$$ is $$1$$, $$i$$, $$-1$$ or $$-i$$
when $$r$$ is $$0$$, $$1$$, $$2$$ or $$3$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Powers_of_Imaginary_Unit"}

If $$q = 0$$, then $$n = r$$ and there is nothing to prove. If $$r = 0$$ and $$q \ge 1$$, then
$$i^n = (i^4)^q = 1^q = 1 = i^0$$, by the rule $$(a^m)^n = a^{mn}$$ in :ref{#numbers/power-rules}
and $$i^4 = 1$$. Otherwise $$q \ge 1$$ and $$r \ge 1$$, and

```math
\begin{aligned}
i^n &= i^{4q} \cdot i^r &&\qquad a^{m + n} = a^m a^n \\
&= (i^4)^q \cdot i^r &&\qquad a^{mn} = (a^m)^n \\
&= 1^q \cdot i^r &&\qquad i^4 = 1 \\
&= i^r &&\qquad 1^q = 1
\end{aligned}
```

The values of $$i^0$$, $$i^1$$, $$i^2$$ and $$i^3$$ are $$1$$, $$i$$, $$-1$$ and $$-i$$, as worked
out above.

:::

For example, $$27 = 4 \cdot 6 + 3$$, so $$i^{27} = i^3 = -i$$. As a check with the picture, $$27$$
quarter turns are $$6$$ full turns and $$3$$ quarter turns more, and three quarter turns from $$1$$
land on $$-i$$. In the same way $$i^{100} = 1$$, since $$100 = 4 \cdot 25$$ leaves remainder $$0$$.

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on meeting i, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-meeting-i-calculate}

Write each answer in the form $$a + bi$$.

1. $$(2 + 5i) + (4 - 3i)$$
2. $$(2 + i)(3 + 4i)$$
3. $$(1 - 3i)^2$$
4. $$i(4 - 7i)$$

:::solution

1. Add the real numbers and the numbers in front of $$i$$ separately:
   $$(2 + 4) + (5 - 3)i = 6 + 2i$$.
2. Multiply out: $$6 + 8i + 3i + 4i^2 = 6 + 11i - 4 = 2 + 11i$$.
3. Multiply out: $$(1 - 3i)(1 - 3i) = 1 - 3i - 3i + 9i^2 = 1 - 6i - 9 = -8 - 6i$$.
4. Multiply out: $$4i - 7i^2 = 4i + 7 = 7 + 4i$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-meeting-i-pairs}

1. Write $$(-3, 2)$$, $$(0, 5)$$ and $$(7, 0)$$ in the form $$a + bi$$.
2. Use the rule $$(a, b)(c, d) = (ac - bd, ad + bc)$$ to work out $$(0, 2)(0, 3)$$, and check your
   answer by working out $$2i \cdot 3i$$ instead.

:::solution

1. By part 2 of :ref{#i-squared}, $$(-3, 2) = -3 + 2i$$, $$(0, 5) = 5i$$ and $$(7, 0) = 7$$.
2. $$(0, 2)(0, 3) = (0 \cdot 0 - 2 \cdot 3, 0 \cdot 3 + 2 \cdot 0) = (-6, 0)$$, which is the real
   number $$-6$$. As a check, $$2i \cdot 3i = 6i^2 = -6$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-meeting-i-powers}

Work out $$i^{10}$$, $$i^{35}$$ and $$i^{100}$$, and show that $$i + i^2 + i^3 + i^4 = 0$$.

:::solution

By :ref{#powers-of-i}:

- $$10 = 4 \cdot 2 + 2$$, so $$i^{10} = i^2 = -1$$
- $$35 = 4 \cdot 8 + 3$$, so $$i^{35} = i^3 = -i$$
- $$100 = 4 \cdot 25 + 0$$, so $$i^{100} = i^0 = 1$$

For the sum, $$i + i^2 + i^3 + i^4 = i + (-1) + (-i) + 1 = (-1 + 1) + (i - i) = 0$$. In the picture,
the four points $$i$$, $$-1$$, $$-i$$ and $$1$$ are placed evenly round $$0$$, and adding them as
steps brings you back to $$0$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-meeting-i-bombelli}

This exercise finishes Bombelli's calculation. The formula for $$x^3 = 15x + 4$$ gives $$x$$ as the
sum of a cube root of $$2 + 11i$$ and a cube root of $$2 - 11i$$ (and $$11i$$ is a square root of
$$-121$$).

1. Show that $$(2 + i)^2 = 3 + 4i$$, and then that $$(2 + i)^3 = 2 + 11i$$.
2. Show in the same way that $$(2 - i)^3 = 2 - 11i$$.
3. Add $$2 + i$$ and $$2 - i$$, and check that the sum solves $$x^3 = 15x + 4$$.

:::solution

1. $$(2 + i)^2 = 4 + 2i + 2i + i^2 = 4 + 4i - 1 = 3 + 4i$$. Then
   $$(2 + i)^3 = (3 + 4i)(2 + i) = 6 + 3i + 8i + 4i^2 = 6 + 11i - 4 = 2 + 11i$$.
2. $$(2 - i)^2 = 4 - 4i + i^2 = 3 - 4i$$, and
   $$(2 - i)^3 = (3 - 4i)(2 - i) = 6 - 3i - 8i + 4i^2 = 2 - 11i$$.
3. $$(2 + i) + (2 - i) = 4$$. As a check, $$4^3 = 64$$ and $$15 \cdot 4 + 4 = 64$$. The square roots
   of $$-121$$ appear halfway through and cancel at the end, as Bombelli found.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-meeting-i-minus-nine}

Find every complex number $$z$$ with $$z^2 = -9$$, and prove there are no others.

:::solution

Both $$3i$$ and $$-3i$$ work: $$(3i)^2 = 9i^2 = -9$$, and $$(-3i)^2 = 9i^2 = -9$$ too.

For the others, follow the proof of :ref{#square-roots-minus-one}. Write $$z = a + bi$$, so
$$z^2 = (a^2 - b^2) + 2abi$$. If this is $$-9 + 0i$$, then $$a^2 - b^2 = -9$$ and $$2ab = 0$$. So
$$a = 0$$ or $$b = 0$$. If $$b = 0$$, then $$a^2 = -9$$, which no real number does. So $$a = 0$$ and
$$b^2 = 9$$, which gives $$b = 3$$ or $$b = -3$$ by :ref{#real-numbers/square-root-count}. So
$$z = 3i$$ or $$z = -3i$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-meeting-i-quarter-turn}

Show that multiplying a complex number $$a + bi$$ by $$i$$ gives $$-b + ai$$. Then explain why this
says that multiplying by $$i$$ is the quarter turn of :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/quarter-turn},
and check it on $$3 + i$$.

:::solution

Multiplying out, $$i(a + bi) = ai + bi^2 = ai - b = -b + ai$$.

As points, this sends $$(a, b)$$ to $$(-b, a)$$. That is the map $$G_{90}(x, y) = (-y, x)$$ of
:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/quarter-turn}, the quarter turn anticlockwise about $$0$$. For
$$3 + i$$, the product is $$i(3 + i) = 3i + i^2 = -1 + 3i$$, and $$G_{90}(3, 1) = (-1, 3)$$, the
same point.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-meeting-i-false-proof}

A friend writes $$\sqrt{-1}$$ for $$i$$ and argues that $$1 = -1$$, like this:

```math
1 = \sqrt{1} = \sqrt{(-1)(-1)} = \sqrt{-1} \cdot \sqrt{-1} = i \cdot i = -1
```

Find the step that goes wrong, and say why.

:::solution

The step $$\sqrt{(-1)(-1)} = \sqrt{-1} \cdot \sqrt{-1}$$ uses the rule
$$\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{a}\,\sqrt{b}$$. That rule, :ref{#real-numbers/root-product}, is proved only for
$$a \ge 0$$ and $$b \ge 0$$, and here $$a = b = -1$$. The first two steps are fine, since
$$\sqrt{1} = 1$$ and $$(-1)(-1) = 1$$, and the last is $$i^2 = -1$$ from :ref{#i-squared}. It is the
false argument for $$i^2 = 1$$ in
[Square roots of negative numbers: a warning](#square-roots-of-negative-numbers-a-warning), run
backwards.
