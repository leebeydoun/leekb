These exercises use the whole chapter, and most need ideas from more than one guide. They start
gently and get harder towards the end. Try each one before you open its solution, and name the rule
or result behind each step, as the guides do.

One habit carries most of them: choose the form that suits the job. Adding and conjugating take the
fewest steps with $$a + bi$$, while multiplying, dividing, powers and roots take the fewest in polar
form $$re^{i\theta}$$. In several of the exercises below, switching between the two is the whole
trick.

## Arithmetic and the plane

::::exercise{#ex-complex-numbers-ch-quadratic}

Solve $$z^2 + 4z + 8 = 0$$. Write both solutions in polar form, and check that their product is
$$8$$.

:::solution

The discriminant is $$16 - 32 = -16 < 0$$, so by :ref{#negative-discriminant} the solutions are

```math
z = \frac{-4 \pm i\sqrt{16}}{2} = -2 \pm 2i
```

Each has length $$\sqrt{4 + 4} = 2\sqrt2$$. The point $$-2 + 2i$$ lies on the line $$y = -x$$ in the
second quarter of the plane, at angle $$\frac{3\pi}{4}$$, and its conjugate $$-2 - 2i$$ is at angle
$$-\frac{3\pi}{4}$$ (:ref{#polar-divide}). So the solutions are $$2\sqrt2\,e^{\pm 3\pi i/4}$$
(:ref{#polar-form}). Their product has length $$(2\sqrt2)^2 = 8$$ and angle
$$\frac{3\pi}{4} - \frac{3\pi}{4} = 0$$ (:ref{#polar-multiply}), so it is $$8$$, which is
$$\frac{c}{a}$$ as :ref{#every-quadratic-vieta} says.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-complex-numbers-ch-high-power}

Write $$\frac{1 + i}{1 - i}$$ in the form $$a + bi$$, and use it to find
$$\left(\frac{1 + i}{1 - i}\right)^{2025}$$.

:::solution

Multiply the top and bottom by the conjugate of the bottom (:ref{#complex-inverse}):

```math
\frac{1 + i}{1 - i} = \frac{(1 + i)(1 + i)}{(1 - i)(1 + i)} = \frac{1 + 2i + i^2}{1 - i^2} = \frac{2i}{2} = i
```

So the power is $$i^{2025}$$. Since $$2025 = 4 \cdot 506 + 1$$, :ref{#powers-of-i} gives
$$i^{2025} = i^1 = i$$. In pictures: $$1 + i$$ and $$1 - i$$ have the same length and angles
$$\pm\frac{\pi}{4}$$, so their quotient has length $$1$$ and angle $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-complex-numbers-ch-unit-circle}

Prove that a complex number $$z \ne 0$$ lies on the unit circle exactly when
$$\frac{1}{z} = \bar z$$.

:::solution

By :ref{#complex-inverse}, $$\frac1z = \frac{\bar z}{\lvert z \rvert^2}$$. If
$$\lvert z \rvert = 1$$ this is $$\bar z$$. Conversely, if $$\frac1z = \bar z$$, multiplying by
$$z$$ gives $$1 = z\bar z = \lvert z \rvert^2$$ (:ref{#times-conjugate}), so
$$\lvert z \rvert = 1$$, since lengths are never negative. In polar form: on the unit circle,
$$\frac{1}{e^{i\theta}} = e^{-i\theta}$$, the mirror image in the real axis.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-complex-numbers-ch-parallelogram}

Prove that
$$\lvert z + w \rvert^2 + \lvert z - w \rvert^2 = 2\lvert z \rvert^2 + 2\lvert w \rvert^2$$ for all
complex numbers $$z$$ and $$w$$, and say what it means for a parallelogram.

:::solution

Use $$\lvert u \rvert^2 = u\bar u$$ (:ref{#times-conjugate}) and :ref{#conjugate-rules}:

```math
\begin{aligned}
\lvert z + w \rvert^2 &= (z + w)(\bar z + \bar w) = z\bar z + z\bar w + w\bar z + w\bar w \\
\lvert z - w \rvert^2 &= (z - w)(\bar z - \bar w) = z\bar z - z\bar w - w\bar z + w\bar w
\end{aligned}
```

Adding, the middle terms cancel and leave
$$2z\bar z + 2w\bar w = 2\lvert z \rvert^2 + 2\lvert w \rvert^2$$. In the parallelogram with corners
$$0$$, $$z$$, $$z + w$$ and $$w$$, the diagonals have lengths $$\lvert z + w \rvert$$ and
$$\lvert z - w \rvert$$: the squares of the two diagonals add up to the squares of the four sides.

:::

## Multiplying, powers and roots

::::exercise{#ex-complex-numbers-ch-equilateral}

Find the third corner $$c$$ of an equilateral triangle with corners $$a = 1 + i$$ and
$$b = 4 + 2i$$, taking $$c$$ on the left as you walk from $$a$$ to $$b$$. Check that all three sides
have the same length.

:::solution

Turning the step $$b - a$$ by $$\frac{\pi}{3}$$ about $$a$$ gives the third corner, since the
triangle's angle at $$a$$ is $$\frac{\pi}{3}$$ and a turn keeps length (:ref{#multiply-turn}). So

```math
\begin{aligned}
c &= a + e^{i\pi/3}(b - a) = (1 + i) + \left(\tfrac12 + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}i\right)(3 + i) \\
&= (1 + i) + \left(\tfrac32 - \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right) + \left(\tfrac12 + \tfrac{3\sqrt3}{2}\right)i \\
&= \left(\tfrac52 - \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right) + \left(\tfrac32 + \tfrac{3\sqrt3}{2}\right)i
\end{aligned}
```

about $$1.63 + 4.10i$$. Check: $$\lvert b - a \rvert^2 = 9 + 1 = 10$$. Next,
$$c - a = e^{i\pi/3}(b - a)$$ has length $$1 \cdot \sqrt{10}$$ (:ref{#abs-product-complex}), so
$$\lvert c - a \rvert^2 = 10$$. Last, $$c - b$$ has real part $$-\tfrac32 - \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$$ and
imaginary part $$-\tfrac12 + \tfrac{3\sqrt3}{2}$$, so

```math
\lvert c - b \rvert^2 = \left(\tfrac32 + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)^2 + \left(-\tfrac12 + \tfrac{3\sqrt3}{2}\right)^2 = \left(3 + \tfrac{3\sqrt3}{2}\right) + \left(7 - \tfrac{3\sqrt3}{2}\right) = 10
```

:::

::::exercise{#ex-complex-numbers-ch-cube-conjugate}

Find every complex number $$z$$ with $$z^3 = \bar z$$.

:::solution

Taking lengths, $$\lvert z \rvert^3 = \lvert \bar z \rvert = \lvert z \rvert$$
(:ref{#abs-product-complex}), so $$\lvert z \rvert$$ is $$0$$ or $$1$$.

- If $$\lvert z \rvert = 0$$, then $$z = 0$$, which works.
- If $$\lvert z \rvert = 1$$, multiply both sides by $$z$$: $$z^4 = z\bar z = 1$$
  (:ref{#times-conjugate}). So $$z$$ is a fourth root of unity: $$1$$, $$i$$, $$-1$$ or $$-i$$
  (:ref{#roots-of-unity-polygon}). Each works; for example $$i^3 = -i = \bar i$$.

So there are five solutions: $$0$$, $$1$$, $$i$$, $$-1$$ and $$-i$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-complex-numbers-ch-hexagon}

A regular hexagon has its centre at $$2 + i$$ and one corner at $$4 + i$$. Find its other corners.

:::solution

Slide the centre to $$0$$: the corner becomes $$2$$, and the corners of a regular hexagon centred at
$$0$$ with a corner at $$2$$ are $$2\omega_k$$, where $$\omega_k = e^{2\pi ik/6}$$ are the sixth
roots of unity (:ref{#roots-of-unity-polygon}). These are $$\pm 1$$ and
$$\pm\frac12 \pm \frac{\sqrt3}{2}i$$, so the corners about $$0$$ are $$\pm 2$$ and
$$\pm 1 \pm \sqrt3\,i$$. Slide back by adding $$2 + i$$:

```math
4 + i, \quad 3 + (1 + \sqrt3)i, \quad 1 + (1 + \sqrt3)i, \quad i, \quad 1 + (1 - \sqrt3)i, \quad 3 + (1 - \sqrt3)i
```

Check: each is at distance $$2$$ from $$2 + i$$, for example
$$\lvert 1 + \sqrt3\,i \rvert = \sqrt{1 + 3} = 2$$.

:::

## Polynomials

::::exercise{#ex-complex-numbers-ch-cubic}

Given that $$1 + 2i$$ is a root of $$z^3 - 3z^2 + 7z - 5$$, find the other two roots.

:::solution

The coefficients are real, so $$1 - 2i$$ is also a root (:ref{#conjugate-pairs}). By the factor
theorem (:ref{#complex-root-bound}) twice, the polynomial has the factor

```math
(z - (1 + 2i))(z - (1 - 2i)) = (z - 1)^2 - (2i)^2 = z^2 - 2z + 5
```

Dividing, $$z^3 - 3z^2 + 7z - 5 = (z^2 - 2z + 5)(z - 1)$$; check by expanding:
$$z^3 - 2z^2 + 5z - z^2 + 2z - 5$$. So the roots are $$1 + 2i$$, $$1 - 2i$$ and $$1$$, and there are
no others (:ref{#complex-root-bound}).

:::

::::exercise{#ex-complex-numbers-ch-fourth}

Find all four solutions of $$z^4 + 4 = 0$$, and use them to write $$z^4 + 4$$ as a product of two
quadratics with real coefficients.

:::solution

We want the fourth roots of $$-4 = 4e^{i\pi}$$. By :ref{#nth-roots-complex} they are

```math
\sqrt[4]{4}\,e^{i(\pi + 2\pi k)/4} = \sqrt2\,e^{i\pi(2k + 1)/4} \qquad k = 0, 1, 2, 3
```

that is, $$1 + i$$, $$-1 + i$$, $$-1 - i$$ and $$1 - i$$. Check one: $$(1 + i)^2 = 2i$$, so
$$(1 + i)^4 = (2i)^2 = -4$$. Pair each root with its conjugate:

```math
\begin{aligned}
(z - (1 + i))(z - (1 - i)) &= z^2 - 2z + 2 \\
(z - (-1 + i))(z - (-1 - i)) &= z^2 + 2z + 2
\end{aligned}
```

So $$z^4 + 4 = (z^2 - 2z + 2)(z^2 + 2z + 2)$$. Check by expanding: the product is
$$(z^2 + 2)^2 - (2z)^2 = z^4 + 4z^2 + 4 - 4z^2 = z^4 + 4$$.

:::
