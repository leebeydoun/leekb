  A complex number by its length and its angle, the shorthand e to the i theta, and why multiplying
  two complex numbers multiplies their lengths and adds their angles.
---

Imagine a drawing on a sheet of tracing paper, pinned to the table through one point. Turn the sheet
a quarter turn anticlockwise about the pin. Every point of the drawing moves, but each one turns by
the same quarter turn and stays the same distance from the pin. Now turn the sheet a second quarter.
The drawing is upside down, and every point has ended up on the opposite side of the pin from where
it started.

Put the pin at $$0$$ in the complex plane, and the first quarter turn is what multiplying by $$i$$
does to every point. The second quarter turn multiplies by $$i$$ again, and sending every point to
the opposite side of $$0$$ is multiplying by $$-1$$. So two quarter turns make a half turn, and that
is the picture behind $$i^2 = -1$$. In this guide we'll describe each complex number by its length
and its angle, and see that multiplying two of them multiplies the lengths and adds the angles.

## Multiplying by i is a quarter turn

Start with numbers. Take $$z = 3 + 2i$$, the point $$(3, 2)$$, and multiply it by $$i$$, replacing
$$i^2$$ by $$-1$$ as :ref{#i-squared} allows:

```math
i(3 + 2i) = 3i + 2i^2 = -2 + 3i
```

So the point $$(3, 2)$$ has gone to $$(-2, 3)$$. That is where the quarter turn
$$G_{90}(x, y) = (-y, x)$$ of :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/quarter-turn} sends it. Multiplying by
$$i$$ once more gives $$i(-2 + 3i) = -2i + 3i^2 = -3 - 2i$$, which is $$-z$$, the point on the other
side of $$0$$ (:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/negative-half-turn}).

Nothing about $$3 + 2i$$ was special. For every complex number $$x + yi$$,

```math
i(x + yi) = xi + yi^2 = -y + xi
```

so multiplying by $$i$$ sends each point $$(x, y)$$ to $$(-y, x)$$, all at once. The whole plane
turns a quarter turn about $$0$$, like the sheet of tracing paper.

<QuarterTurns caption="A flag with its tip at z = 3 + 2i (blue). Multiplying every point of it by i turns it a quarter turn about 0, to the orange flag with its tip at iz = −2 + 3i. Multiplying by i again gives the green flag, with its tip at −z = −3 − 2i." />

Multiplying by a real number does something different. By :ref{#complex-reals},
$$2(3 + 2i) = 6 + 4i$$ is the point $$(3, 2)$$ scaled by $$2$$
(:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/scalar-multiple}): a zoom about $$0$$ that doubles every distance
from $$0$$. So $$i$$ turns and $$2$$ zooms. A number such as $$1 + i$$ does some of each, and to see
how much, we need to describe it by how far it is from $$0$$ and in which direction.

## Length and angle

To reach a point of the plane from $$0$$, you can face in a direction and then walk a distance. The
Trigonometry chapter calls the distance and the angle of the direction the point's polar coordinates
(:ref{#trigonometry/polar-coordinates}). For a complex number, the distance is its absolute value,
its length as a point (:ref{#complex-abs}).

Take $$z = -2 + 2i$$. Its length is $$|z| = \sqrt{(-2)^2 + 2^2} = \sqrt8 = 2\sqrt2$$. The point
$$(-2, 2)$$ sits on the diagonal line through the second quarter of the plane, at the angle
$$\frac{3\pi}{4}$$ from the positive real axis. To check, $$\cos\frac{3\pi}{4} = -\frac{\sqrt2}{2}$$
and $$\sin\frac{3\pi}{4} = \frac{\sqrt2}{2}$$, by :ref{#trigonometry/sin-cos-45} and
:ref{#trigonometry/supplement-identities}. So

```math
2\sqrt2\cos\tfrac{3\pi}{4} = 2\sqrt2 \cdot \left(-\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\right) = -2 \qquad\text{and}\qquad 2\sqrt2\sin\tfrac{3\pi}{4} = 2\sqrt2 \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2} = 2
```

which are the two coordinates of $$z$$. In one line,
$$-2 + 2i = 2\sqrt2\left(\cos\frac{3\pi}{4} + i\sin\frac{3\pi}{4}\right)$$.

:::definition[Polar form]{#polar-form}

Let $$z = x + yi$$ be a complex number, and let $$r \ge 0$$ and $$\theta$$ be real numbers. Writing

```math
z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)
```

is a **polar form** of $$z$$ when $$r$$ and $$\theta$$ are polar coordinates of the point
$$(x, y)$$, that is, when $$x = r\cos\theta$$ and $$y = r\sin\theta$$. The angle $$\theta$$ is then
called an **argument** of $$z$$.

:::

The two descriptions match because $$r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta) = r\cos\theta + (r\sin\theta)i$$,
by :ref{#complex-arithmetic}. So the real part is $$r\cos\theta$$ and the imaginary part is
$$r\sin\theta$$, as the definition asks.

Every complex number has a polar form, because every point has polar coordinates
(:ref{#trigonometry/polar-exist}). For $$z \ne 0$$, the length is always $$r = |z|$$, and exactly
one argument lies in $$0 \le \theta < 2\pi$$. The others are $$\theta + 2\pi n$$ for whole numbers
$$n$$, since a whole turn more or less lands on the same point. The number $$0$$ is the odd one out:
its length is $$0$$ and every angle works.

:::example[Polar forms you can read off the picture]

Each of these numbers sits on an axis, so its angle is a quarter turn, a half turn or nothing at
all, with the values of :ref{#trigonometry/quarter-values}:

- $$3i$$ is $$3$$ up from $$0$$, so $$3i = 3\left(\cos\frac{\pi}{2} + i\sin\frac{\pi}{2}\right)$$
- $$-5$$ is $$5$$ to the left of $$0$$, so $$-5 = 5(\cos\pi + i\sin\pi)$$
- $$4$$ is $$4$$ to the right of $$0$$, so $$4 = 4(\cos 0 + i\sin 0)$$

The length is never negative. The minus sign of $$-5$$ goes into the angle $$\pi$$, and
$$-5(\cos 0 + i\sin 0)$$ is not a polar form, even though it equals $$-5$$.

:::

For a point that isn't on an axis or a diagonal, the angle comes from the tangent, as in
:ref{#trigonometry/rotations-polar-angle}.

## A shorthand: e to the i theta

The bracket $$\cos\theta + i\sin\theta$$ is going to appear on almost every line from here on. It is
the number of length $$1$$ at angle $$\theta$$, the point $$(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$$ of the unit
circle. A bracket used that often deserves a short name.

:::definition[The shorthand e to the i theta]{#e-i-theta}

For every real number $$\theta$$,

```math
e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta
```

Read $$e^{i\theta}$$ aloud as "e to the i theta". With it, a polar form is $$z = re^{i\theta}$$.

:::

Take this as a name and nothing more. Nobody is raising the number $$e$$ to a power here: it is a
label for the bracket. The name is chosen because $$e^{i\theta}$$ obeys the rule for adding
exponents, $$e^{i\theta}e^{i\varphi} = e^{i(\theta + \varphi)}$$, which we prove below, in the same
way that $$a^x a^y = a^{x + y}$$ does in :ref{#functions/exponential-rules}. Calculus explains a
deeper link with the number $$e$$, but this chapter doesn't use it.

A few values, read from the unit circle:

- $$e^{i0} = \cos 0 + i\sin 0 = 1$$
- $$e^{i\pi/2} = \cos\frac{\pi}{2} + i\sin\frac{\pi}{2} = i$$
- $$e^{i3\pi/4} = -\frac{\sqrt2}{2} + \frac{\sqrt2}{2}i$$, so $$-2 + 2i = 2\sqrt2\,e^{i3\pi/4}$$

Every $$e^{i\theta}$$ has length $$1$$, since
$$|e^{i\theta}| = \sqrt{\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta} = 1$$ by
:ref{#trigonometry/pythagorean-identity}.

## Lengths multiply and angles add

Take $$z = 1 + i$$ and $$w = -1 + i$$. Both have length $$\sqrt{1 + 1} = \sqrt2$$, and their angles
are $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$ and $$\frac{3\pi}{4}$$, so $$z = \sqrt2\,e^{i\pi/4}$$ and
$$w = \sqrt2\,e^{i3\pi/4}$$. Multiplying them the long way,

```math
(1 + i)(-1 + i) = -1 + i - i + i^2 = -2
```

and $$-2 = 2e^{i\pi}$$. The length $$2$$ is $$\sqrt2 \cdot \sqrt2$$, the two lengths multiplied, and
the angle $$\pi$$ is $$\frac{\pi}{4} + \frac{3\pi}{4}$$, the two angles added.

:::theorem[Multiplying in polar form]{#polar-multiply}

For all real numbers $$\theta$$ and $$\varphi$$,

```math
e^{i\theta}e^{i\varphi} = e^{i(\theta + \varphi)}
```

So for all $$r, s \ge 0$$,

```math
(re^{i\theta})(se^{i\varphi}) = rs\,e^{i(\theta + \varphi)}
```

In words, the length of a product is the product of the lengths, and the angles add.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_number#Polar_form"}

Multiply out the two brackets, one step at a time:

```math
\begin{aligned}
e^{i\theta}e^{i\varphi} &= (\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)(\cos\varphi + i\sin\varphi) &&\qquad \text{the shorthand} \\
&= \cos\theta\cos\varphi + i\cos\theta\sin\varphi + i\sin\theta\cos\varphi + i^2\sin\theta\sin\varphi &&\qquad \text{multiply out} \\
&= (\cos\theta\cos\varphi - \sin\theta\sin\varphi) + i(\sin\theta\cos\varphi + \cos\theta\sin\varphi) &&\qquad i^2 = -1\text{, and collect} \\
&= \cos(\theta + \varphi) + i\sin(\theta + \varphi) &&\qquad \text{addition formulas} \\
&= e^{i(\theta + \varphi)} &&\qquad \text{the shorthand}
\end{aligned}
```

Multiplying out uses the rules of :ref{#complex-arithmetic}, the third line uses :ref{#i-squared},
and the fourth uses :ref{#trigonometry/addition-formulas}. For the second sentence, the same rules
let us reorder a product of four numbers, so
$$(re^{i\theta})(se^{i\varphi}) = rs\,(e^{i\theta}e^{i\varphi}) = rs\,e^{i(\theta + \varphi)}$$.

:::

Look at where the trigonometry went in that proof: the fourth line, and nowhere else. So this
theorem is the addition formulas for sine and cosine, packed into one line. Read the other way, it
gives them back: if you remember only that angles add, multiplying out $$e^{i\theta}e^{i\varphi}$$
and comparing the real parts and the imaginary parts rebuilds both formulas.
[Powers and identities](./powers-and-identities.mdx) builds a whole method on that.

The length half of the theorem is not new: :ref{#abs-product-complex} already says
$$|zw| = |z||w|$$. The angle half is the new part, and it has a slip worth naming. Angles add when
you multiply; they don't multiply. For $$z = 1 + i$$ and $$w = -1 + i$$, the product of the angles
is $$\frac{\pi}{4} \cdot \frac{3\pi}{4} = \frac{3\pi^2}{16}$$, which is not an angle of $$-2$$.

stay the sum of the other two.

## Multiplying by a number of length 1 turns

When $$|w| = 1$$, multiplying by $$w$$ leaves every length alone and adds the same angle to every
direction. That is a turn about $$0$$, by the angle of $$w$$. The Trigonometry chapter has a formula
for turning a point, and multiplying out shows it is the same formula.

:::corollary[Multiplying by a number of length 1 turns]{#multiply-turn}

Let $$\varphi$$ be a real number. For every complex number $$z = x + yi$$, the product
$$e^{i\varphi}z$$ is the point $$G_\varphi(x, y)$$, where $$G_\varphi$$ is the turn by $$\varphi$$
about $$0$$. So multiplying by $$e^{i\varphi}$$ turns the whole plane by $$\varphi$$ about $$0$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_(mathematics)#Two_dimensions"}

Multiply out, and collect the real and imaginary parts:

```math
\begin{aligned}
e^{i\varphi}(x + yi) &= (\cos\varphi + i\sin\varphi)(x + yi) &&\qquad \text{the shorthand} \\
&= x\cos\varphi + iy\cos\varphi + ix\sin\varphi + i^2 y\sin\varphi &&\qquad \text{multiply out} \\
&= (x\cos\varphi - y\sin\varphi) + (x\sin\varphi + y\cos\varphi)i &&\qquad i^2 = -1\text{, and collect}
\end{aligned}
```

The point with these two parts is $$(x\cos\varphi - y\sin\varphi,\ x\sin\varphi + y\cos\varphi)$$.
Line for line, that is the formula for $$G_\varphi(x, y)$$ in :ref{#trigonometry/rotation-formula}.

:::

:::corollary[Multiplying by i]{#multiply-by-i}

Multiplying by $$i$$ is the quarter turn about $$0$$: for every complex number $$x + yi$$,
$$i(x + yi) = -y + xi$$, the point $$G_{90}(x, y) = (-y, x)$$ of
:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/quarter-turn}.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imaginary_unit#Quarter-turn_rotation"}

By :ref{#trigonometry/quarter-values}, $$\cos\frac{\pi}{2} = 0$$ and $$\sin\frac{\pi}{2} = 1$$, so
$$e^{i\pi/2} = 0 + 1 \cdot i = i$$. Putting $$\varphi = \frac{\pi}{2}$$ in :ref{#multiply-turn}
gives

```math
i(x + yi) = (x \cdot 0 - y \cdot 1) + (x \cdot 1 + y \cdot 0)i = -y + xi
```

:::

A number $$w$$ of any length $$s > 0$$ is $$w = se^{i\varphi}$$, so $$wz = s(e^{i\varphi}z)$$. In
words, multiplying by $$w$$ turns by its angle $$\varphi$$, then zooms about $$0$$ by its length
$$s$$, which is what multiplying by the real number $$s$$ does (:ref{#complex-reals}).

:::example[Turning and stretching a triangle]

Multiply each corner of the triangle with corners $$2$$, $$3 + i$$ and $$1 + 2i$$ by
$$w = 1 + i = \sqrt2\,e^{i\pi/4}$$:

```math
2(1 + i) = 2 + 2i \qquad (3 + i)(1 + i) = 3 + 3i + i + i^2 = 2 + 4i \qquad (1 + 2i)(1 + i) = 1 + i + 2i + 2i^2 = -1 + 3i
```

The new triangle is the old one turned by $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$ about $$0$$ and zoomed by $$\sqrt2$$. As
a check on the zoom, the side from $$2$$ to $$3 + i$$ has length $$|1 + i| = \sqrt2$$, and its new
side from $$2 + 2i$$ to $$2 + 4i$$ has length $$|2i| = 2$$, which is $$\sqrt2$$ times as long.

:::

## Dividing and conjugates

Dividing by $$w$$ undoes multiplying by $$w$$, so it should undo the turn and the zoom: turn back by
the angle, and zoom by $$1$$ over the length. The conjugate is a flip in the real axis
(:ref{#conjugate}), which keeps the length and turns the angle $$\theta$$ into $$-\theta$$. Below,
$$e^{-i\theta}$$ is short for $$e^{i(-\theta)}$$, the number of length $$1$$ at angle $$-\theta$$.

:::proposition[Dividing and conjugates in polar form]{#polar-divide}

For all real numbers $$\theta$$ and $$\varphi$$, and all $$r \ge 0$$ and $$s > 0$$,

```math
\frac{1}{se^{i\varphi}} = \frac{1}{s}e^{-i\varphi} \qquad \overline{re^{i\theta}} = re^{-i\theta} \qquad \frac{re^{i\theta}}{se^{i\varphi}} = \frac{r}{s}e^{i(\theta - \varphi)}
```

So dividing divides the lengths and subtracts the angles.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_number#Polar_form"}

**The inverse.** By :ref{#polar-multiply},

```math
(se^{i\varphi})\left(\tfrac{1}{s}e^{-i\varphi}\right) = \left(s \cdot \tfrac{1}{s}\right)e^{i(\varphi - \varphi)} = e^{i0} = \cos 0 + i\sin 0 = 1
```

The number $$se^{i\varphi}$$ is not $$0$$, since its length is $$s > 0$$. By :ref{#complex-inverse}
it has exactly one inverse, so that inverse is $$\frac{1}{s}e^{-i\varphi}$$.

**The conjugate.** The number $$re^{i\theta}$$ has real part $$r\cos\theta$$ and imaginary part
$$r\sin\theta$$, so by :ref{#conjugate} and then :ref{#trigonometry/negative-angle},

```math
\overline{re^{i\theta}} = r\cos\theta - (r\sin\theta)i = r\big(\cos(-\theta) + i\sin(-\theta)\big) = re^{-i\theta}
```

**The quotient.** Dividing by a number is multiplying by its inverse (:ref{#complex-inverse}), so by
the first part and :ref{#polar-multiply},

```math
\frac{re^{i\theta}}{se^{i\varphi}} = re^{i\theta} \cdot \tfrac{1}{s}e^{-i\varphi} = \frac{r}{s}e^{i(\theta - \varphi)}
```

:::

:::example[Dividing −2 by 1 + i]

In polar form $$-2 = 2e^{i\pi}$$ and $$1 + i = \sqrt2\,e^{i\pi/4}$$, so

```math
\frac{-2}{1 + i} = \frac{2}{\sqrt2}e^{i(\pi - \pi/4)} = \sqrt2\,e^{i3\pi/4} = -1 + i
```

using the value of $$e^{i3\pi/4}$$ found earlier. As a check, multiply back:
$$(-1 + i)(1 + i) = -1 - i + i + i^2 = -2$$.

:::

## Turning about another point

Multiplying by $$e^{i\varphi}$$ turns the plane about $$0$$. To turn about a point $$c$$ instead,
slide $$c$$ to $$0$$, turn, and slide back. That is the recipe of
:ref{#trigonometry/rotations-about-point}, and in complex numbers it fits in one line: the turn by
$$\varphi$$ about $$c$$ sends $$z$$ to

```math
c + e^{i\varphi}(z - c)
```

:::example[Turning 3 + i by π/3 about 1 + i]

The step from the centre $$c = 1 + i$$ to $$z = 3 + i$$ is $$z - c = 2$$. By
:ref{#trigonometry/sin-cos-30-60}, $$e^{i\pi/3} = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{\sqrt3}{2}i$$, so the turned
step is

```math
2e^{i\pi/3} = 1 + \sqrt3\,i
```

Sliding back adds $$c$$, and the turned point is $$(1 + i) + (1 + \sqrt3\,i) = 2 + (1 + \sqrt3)i$$,
about $$2 + 2.73i$$. As a check, its distance from $$z$$ is
$$|(2 - 3) + \sqrt3\,i| = \sqrt{1 + 3} = 2$$, the same as the distance from $$c$$ to $$z$$. So the
centre, $$z$$ and the turned point make an equilateral triangle, as a turn by $$\frac{\pi}{3}$$
should.

:::

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on multiplying in polar form, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-polar-form-read-off}

Write each number in polar form $$re^{i\theta}$$, with $$r$$ its length:

1. $$-7i$$
2. $$-4$$
3. $$1 - i$$

:::solution

1. The point $$(0, -7)$$ is $$7$$ down from $$0$$, at angle $$-\frac{\pi}{2}$$, since
   $$\cos\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = 0$$ and $$\sin\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = -1$$. So
   $$-7i = 7e^{-i\pi/2}$$, and $$7e^{i3\pi/2}$$ is the same number, a whole turn later.
2. The point $$(-4, 0)$$ is $$4$$ to the left of $$0$$, at angle $$\pi$$, so $$-4 = 4e^{i\pi}$$.
3. The length is $$\sqrt{1 + 1} = \sqrt2$$. The point $$(1, -1)$$ is on the diagonal below the
   positive real axis, at angle $$-\frac{\pi}{4}$$, since
   $$\cos\left(-\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = \frac{\sqrt2}{2}$$ and
   $$\sin\left(-\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = -\frac{\sqrt2}{2}$$ (:ref{#trigonometry/negative-angle}). So
   $$1 - i = \sqrt2\,e^{-i\pi/4}$$, and $$\sqrt2\,e^{i7\pi/4}$$ is the same number, a whole turn
   later.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-polar-form-back}

Write each number in the form $$a + bi$$:

1. $$6e^{i5\pi/6}$$
2. $$2e^{-i\pi/2}$$
3. $$\sqrt2\,e^{i5\pi/4}$$

:::solution

1. By :ref{#trigonometry/supplement-identities} and :ref{#trigonometry/sin-cos-30-60},
   $$\cos\frac{5\pi}{6} = -\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$ and $$\sin\frac{5\pi}{6} = \frac{1}{2}$$. So
   $$6e^{i5\pi/6} = 6\left(-\frac{\sqrt3}{2} + \frac{1}{2}i\right) = -3\sqrt3 + 3i$$.
2. $$\cos\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = 0$$ and $$\sin\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = -1$$, so
   $$2e^{-i\pi/2} = -2i$$, two down from $$0$$.
3. The angle $$\frac{5\pi}{4}$$ is a half turn past $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$, so the point is opposite
   $$\frac{\sqrt2}{2} + \frac{\sqrt2}{2}i$$, at $$-\frac{\sqrt2}{2} - \frac{\sqrt2}{2}i$$. Times
   $$\sqrt2$$, that is $$-1 - i$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-polar-form-five-twelfths}

Let $$z = \sqrt3 + i$$ and $$w = 1 + i$$.

1. Find the length and an angle of $$z$$, of $$w$$ and of $$zw$$, without multiplying out.
2. Multiply out $$zw$$, and use the two answers to find $$\cos\frac{5\pi}{12}$$ and
   $$\sin\frac{5\pi}{12}$$.

:::solution

1. $$|z| = \sqrt{3 + 1} = 2$$ with angle $$\frac{\pi}{6}$$, since
   $$2e^{i\pi/6} = 2\left(\frac{\sqrt3}{2} + \frac{1}{2}i\right) = \sqrt3 + i$$. And
   $$w = \sqrt2\,e^{i\pi/4}$$. By :ref{#polar-multiply}, $$zw$$ has length $$2\sqrt2$$ and angle
   $$\frac{\pi}{6} + \frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{5\pi}{12}$$.
2. Multiplying out,

   ```math
   (\sqrt3 + i)(1 + i) = \sqrt3 + \sqrt3\,i + i + i^2 = (\sqrt3 - 1) + (\sqrt3 + 1)i
   ```

   By the first part this is $$2\sqrt2\left(\cos\frac{5\pi}{12} + i\sin\frac{5\pi}{12}\right)$$.
   Equal complex numbers have equal real parts and equal imaginary parts (:ref{#complex-number}), so

   ```math
   \cos\frac{5\pi}{12} = \frac{\sqrt3 - 1}{2\sqrt2} = \frac{\sqrt6 - \sqrt2}{4} \approx 0.259 \qquad \sin\frac{5\pi}{12} = \frac{\sqrt3 + 1}{2\sqrt2} = \frac{\sqrt6 + \sqrt2}{4} \approx 0.966
   ```

   The second form multiplies top and bottom by $$\sqrt2$$. A calculator in radian mode agrees.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-polar-form-minus-i}

Show that multiplying by $$-i$$ is the turn by $$-\frac{\pi}{2}$$, a quarter turn clockwise. Then
find $$-i(3 + 2i)$$ and check that it is where three quarter turns anticlockwise take $$3 + 2i$$.

:::solution

Since $$\cos\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = 0$$ and $$\sin\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = -1$$, we have
$$e^{-i\pi/2} = -i$$. So by :ref{#multiply-turn}, multiplying by $$-i$$ is the turn by
$$-\frac{\pi}{2}$$. For the number,

```math
-i(3 + 2i) = -3i - 2i^2 = 2 - 3i
```

Three quarter turns anticlockwise multiply by $$i$$ three times. From the start of the guide,
$$i(3 + 2i) = -2 + 3i$$ and $$i(-2 + 3i) = -3 - 2i$$, and once more,
$$i(-3 - 2i) = -3i - 2i^2 = 2 - 3i$$. The two agree, as they should: three quarter turns one way end
where one quarter turn the other way does.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-polar-form-divide}

Find $$\dfrac{-3 + 3i}{1 + i}$$ in two ways: in polar form, and by multiplying the top and bottom by
the conjugate of $$1 + i$$.

:::solution

**Polar form.** $$-3 + 3i = 3\sqrt2\,e^{i3\pi/4}$$, with length $$\sqrt{9 + 9} = 3\sqrt2$$, and
$$1 + i = \sqrt2\,e^{i\pi/4}$$. By :ref{#polar-divide},

```math
\frac{-3 + 3i}{1 + i} = \frac{3\sqrt2}{\sqrt2}e^{i(3\pi/4 - \pi/4)} = 3e^{i\pi/2} = 3i
```

**The conjugate.** Multiply top and bottom by $$1 - i$$, as in :ref{#complex-inverse}:

```math
\frac{(-3 + 3i)(1 - i)}{(1 + i)(1 - i)} = \frac{-3 + 3i + 3i - 3i^2}{1 - i^2} = \frac{6i}{2} = 3i
```

As a check, $$3i(1 + i) = 3i + 3i^2 = -3 + 3i$$.

::::exercise{#ex-polar-form-keeps-distance}

Let $$w$$ be a complex number. Prove that multiplying by $$w$$ multiplies every distance by $$|w|$$:
for all complex numbers $$z_1$$ and $$z_2$$, $$|wz_1 - wz_2| = |w|\,|z_1 - z_2|$$. Deduce that
multiplying by a number of length $$1$$ keeps every distance.

:::solution

The distance between two points is the length of their difference
(:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/distance-norm}), so it is enough to prove the equation:

```math
\begin{aligned}
|wz_1 - wz_2| &= |w(z_1 - z_2)| &&\qquad \text{distributive rule} \\
&= |w|\,|z_1 - z_2| &&\qquad \text{length of a product}
\end{aligned}
```

The first step uses :ref{#complex-arithmetic} and the second :ref{#abs-product-complex}. When
$$|w| = 1$$, the right side is $$|z_1 - z_2|$$, so the distance is unchanged. That fits
:ref{#multiply-turn}: a turn keeps every distance.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-polar-form-turn-about}

Turn the point $$5 + 2i$$ by $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$ about the point $$2 + i$$. Check that the turned
point is as far from $$2 + i$$ as $$5 + 2i$$ is.

:::solution

The step from the centre is $$(5 + 2i) - (2 + i) = 3 + i$$. Turning it by $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$ is
multiplying by $$i$$ (:ref{#multiply-by-i}): $$i(3 + i) = 3i + i^2 = -1 + 3i$$. Sliding back adds
the centre:

```math
(2 + i) + (-1 + 3i) = 1 + 4i
```

The two distances from $$2 + i$$ are $$|3 + i| = \sqrt{9 + 1} = \sqrt{10}$$ and
$$|(1 + 4i) - (2 + i)| = |-1 + 3i| = \sqrt{1 + 9} = \sqrt{10}$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-polar-form-slip}

A friend writes $$\left(2e^{i\pi/3}\right)\left(3e^{i\pi/4}\right) = 6e^{i\pi^2/12}$$. Say what went
wrong, and give the right answer in polar form.

:::solution

The lengths are right: $$2 \cdot 3 = 6$$. The slip is in the angles, which were multiplied:
$$\frac{\pi}{3} \cdot \frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{\pi^2}{12}$$. By :ref{#polar-multiply}, angles add when
you multiply, so the angle is $$\frac{\pi}{3} + \frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{7\pi}{12}$$, and the product
is $$6e^{i7\pi/12}$$.
