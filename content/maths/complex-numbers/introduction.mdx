Picture the number line as a road with no turnings. Every real number has its place on it, and yet
the equation $$x^2 + 1 = 0$$ has nowhere to land, because no point on the road squares to a negative
number. The way out is to step off the road into the plane around it. A **complex number** is a
point of that plane, written $$a + bi$$, where $$i$$ is the point one step up from $$0$$ and
$$i^2 = -1$$. In this chapter we'll build these numbers from scratch, see what adding and
multiplying them do to the picture, and use them to solve every quadratic equation.

One idea carries the chapter: a complex number is a point, adding points slides, and multiplying
turns by the angle and stretches by the length. Multiplying by $$i$$ is a quarter turn, which is why
$$i^2 = -1$$: two quarter turns face you the other way. The same idea turns the trigonometric
identities into calculations you can redo whenever you need them.

## What this chapter builds on

Adding complex numbers is adding points, from
[Adding and scaling points](../adding-and-scaling-points/introduction.mdx). Multiplying them uses
polar coordinates and the addition formulas from [Trigonometry](../trigonometry/introduction.mdx),
and the factor theorem from [Polynomials](../functions/polynomials.mdx) counts their roots.

Nothing about complex numbers is taken as given: they are defined as pairs of real numbers, and the
rules of arithmetic are proved for them (:ref{#complex-arithmetic}). One famous result, the
fundamental theorem of algebra (:ref{#fundamental-theorem-algebra}), is stated at the end for its
interest and used in no proof.

Along the way the chapter keeps two promises from earlier chapters:

- **Every quadratic has solutions**: [The discriminant](../quadratics/discriminant.mdx) said that a
  larger number system would give every quadratic solutions, by the same formula. Here it does
  (:ref{#complex-quadratic-formula}).
- **Identities you can rebuild**: [Trigonometry](../trigonometry/addition-formulas.mdx) proved the
  double-angle formulas from the addition formulas. Here they, and the triple-angle formulas, come
  out of one calculation (:ref{#de-moivre}, :ref{#triple-angle}).

:::accordion[Summary of definitions and rules]

**Definitions**

| Term                        | Meaning                                                                       |
| --------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| :ref{#complex-number}       | A pair $$(a, b)$$ of real numbers, with $$(a, b)(c, d) = (ac - bd, ad + bc)$$ |
| :ref{#i}                    | $$i = (0, 1)$$                                                                |
| :ref{#real-imaginary-parts} | $$\operatorname{Re}(a + bi) = a$$ and $$\operatorname{Im}(a + bi) = b$$       |
| :ref{#conjugate}            | $$\overline{a + bi} = a - bi$$, the flip in the real axis                     |
| :ref{#complex-abs}          | $$\lvert a + bi \rvert = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$$, the distance from $$0$$          |
| :ref{#polar-form}           | $$z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)$$ with $$r = \lvert z \rvert$$              |
| :ref{#e-i-theta}            | $$e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$$, a shorthand                       |
| :ref{#roots-of-unity}       | Numbers $$\omega$$ with $$\omega^n = 1$$                                      |

**Stated, not used in any proof**

| Result                             | Statement                                                         |
| ---------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| :ref{#fundamental-theorem-algebra} | Every complex polynomial of degree $$n \ge 1$$ has a complex root |

**Arithmetic and the plane**

| Result                        | Statement                                                                     |
| ----------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| :ref{#complex-reals}          | The pairs $$(a, 0)$$ add and multiply like the real numbers $$a$$             |
| :ref{#i-squared}              | $$i^2 = -1$$, and every complex number is $$a + bi$$ in exactly one way       |
| :ref{#complex-arithmetic}     | Complex numbers obey the rules of arithmetic                                  |
| :ref{#square-roots-minus-one} | $$z^2 = -1$$ exactly when $$z = \pm i$$                                       |
| :ref{#powers-of-i}            | $$i^n$$ depends on the remainder of $$n$$ on division by $$4$$                |
| :ref{#conjugate-rules}        | $$\overline{z + w} = \bar z + \bar w$$ and $$\overline{zw} = \bar z\,\bar w$$ |
| :ref{#times-conjugate}        | $$z\bar z = \lvert z \rvert^2$$                                               |
| :ref{#complex-inverse}        | $$\frac1z = \frac{\bar z}{\lvert z \rvert^2}$$ for $$z \ne 0$$                |
| :ref{#complex-zero-product}   | $$zw = 0$$ only when $$z = 0$$ or $$w = 0$$                                   |
| :ref{#abs-product-complex}    | $$\lvert zw \rvert = \lvert z \rvert\lvert w \rvert$$                         |
| :ref{#complex-triangle}       | $$\lvert z + w \rvert \le \lvert z \rvert + \lvert w \rvert$$                 |

**Multiplying, powers and roots**

| Result                        | Statement                                                                                                        |
| ----------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| :ref{#polar-multiply}         | $$re^{i\theta} \cdot se^{i\varphi} = rs\,e^{i(\theta + \varphi)}$$                                               |
| :ref{#multiply-turn}          | Multiplying by $$e^{i\varphi}$$ is the turn by $$\varphi$$ about $$0$$                                           |
| :ref{#multiply-by-i}          | Multiplying by $$i$$ is the quarter turn                                                                         |
| :ref{#polar-divide}           | Dividing divides lengths and subtracts angles; $$\overline{re^{i\theta}} = re^{-i\theta}$$                       |
| :ref{#de-moivre}              | $$(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)^n = \cos n\theta + i\sin n\theta$$                                                  |
| :ref{#identities-double}      | $$\cos 2\theta = \cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta$$ and $$\sin 2\theta = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta$$                      |
| :ref{#triple-angle}           | $$\cos 3\theta = 4\cos^3\theta - 3\cos\theta$$ and $$\sin 3\theta = 3\sin\theta - 4\sin^3\theta$$                |
| :ref{#cos-sin-from-e}         | $$\cos\theta = \frac{e^{i\theta} + e^{-i\theta}}{2}$$ and $$\sin\theta = \frac{e^{i\theta} - e^{-i\theta}}{2i}$$ |
| :ref{#complex-square-roots}   | Every $$z \ne 0$$ has exactly two square roots, $$\pm\sqrt{r}\,e^{i\theta/2}$$                                   |
| :ref{#complex-root-bound}     | The factor theorem holds, and a complex polynomial of degree $$n$$ has at most $$n$$ roots                       |
| :ref{#roots-of-unity-polygon} | The $$n$$-th roots of unity are $$e^{2\pi ik/n}$$, the corners of a regular $$n$$-gon                            |
| :ref{#roots-sum-zero}         | For $$n \ge 2$$ they add up to $$0$$                                                                             |
| :ref{#nth-roots-complex}      | $$w^n = z$$ has exactly $$n$$ solutions $$\sqrt[n]{r}\,e^{i(\theta + 2\pi k)/n}$$                                |

**Quadratics**

| Result                           | Statement                                                                             |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| :ref{#complex-quadratic-formula} | $$az^2 + bz + c = 0$$ has solutions $$\frac{-b \pm w}{2a}$$, with $$w^2 = b^2 - 4ac$$ |
| :ref{#every-quadratic-vieta}     | The solutions add to $$-\frac{b}{a}$$ and multiply to $$\frac{c}{a}$$                 |
| :ref{#negative-discriminant}     | A real quadratic with negative discriminant has two conjugate complex solutions       |
| :ref{#conjugate-pairs}           | With real coefficients, if $$z$$ is a root then so is $$\bar z$$                      |
