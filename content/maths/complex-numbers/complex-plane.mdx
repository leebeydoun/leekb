Imagine a treasure map with a grid drawn over it and an old oak tree marked near one corner. The
first clue says: from the oak, walk 4 paces east and 1 pace north. The second clue says: from where
the first clue left you, walk 1 pace west and 3 paces north. You end up 3 paces east and 4 paces
north of the oak, and the two clues together work like one longer set of directions.

<TreasureMap caption="The first clue (blue) walks 4 paces east and 1 north from the oak. The second clue (orange) walks 1 west and 3 north from there. Together they reach the treasure, 3 east and 4 north of the oak, which is 5 paces away in a straight line (dashed)." />

Each place on the map is a pair of numbers, paces east and paces north, so each place is a complex
number. The first clue is $$4 + i$$, the second is $$-1 + 3i$$, and following both is adding them:
$$(4 + i) + (-1 + 3i) = 3 + 4i$$. The plane drawn this way, with each point read as a complex
number, is called the **complex plane**. In this guide we'll read each operation on complex numbers
as a move on this map, and then divide one complex number by another.

## Complex numbers as points

By :ref{#complex-number}, a complex number is a point of the plane, and by :ref{#i-squared} the
point $$(a, b)$$ is the number $$a + bi$$. So $$3 + 2i$$ is the point three across and two up, and
$$-2 + i$$ is two to the left and one up. The horizontal axis holds the numbers $$a + 0i$$, which
are the real numbers (:ref{#complex-reals}), so it is called the **real axis**. The vertical axis
holds the numbers $$bi$$, called **imaginary numbers**, so it is called the **imaginary axis**. The
name "imaginary" is a leftover from the days when these numbers were distrusted. On the map, $$2i$$
is a place two steps north of $$0$$, as definite as $$2$$, two steps east.

<PointsInPlane caption="Five complex numbers as points. The real number 2 sits on the real axis, the imaginary number −2i sits on the imaginary axis, and 3 + 2i, −2 + i and −3 − 2i are off both axes." />

## Adding is following directions

On the treasure map, adding two complex numbers is taking one step and then the other. That is
adding points, so the sum is the far corner of the parallelogram with sides $$z$$ and $$w$$
(:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/parallelogram-law}). Taking the steps in the other order, $$w$$
first and then $$z$$, goes round the other two sides of the parallelogram to the same corner, which
is $$z + w = w + z$$.

<SumParallelogram caption="Adding z = 4 + i and w = −1 + 3i. Taking z and then w (solid blue, dashed orange) or w and then z (solid orange, dashed blue) reaches the same corner, z + w = 3 + 4i, at the end of the purple diagonal." />

Subtracting runs a step backwards. With $$z = a + bi$$ and $$w = c + di$$, the difference is
$$z - w = (a - c) + (b - d)i$$, the difference of points from
:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/point-difference}. It is the step that takes you from $$w$$ to
$$z$$, because $$w + (z - w) = z$$. For example, with $$z = 4 + i$$ and $$w = 1 + 3i$$,

```math
z - w = (4 - 1) + (1 - 3)i = 3 - 2i
```

so from $$w$$ you walk 3 east and 2 south to reach $$z$$.

<DifferenceStep caption="The difference z − w for z = 4 + i and w = 1 + 3i is the step from w to z (purple arrow): 3 across and 2 down. The same step taken from 0 (dashed) ends at z − w = 3 − 2i." />

## Real and imaginary parts

Each complex number has two numbers in it, how far across and how far up, and each gets a name.

:::definition[Real and imaginary parts]{#real-imaginary-parts}

Let $$z = a + bi$$ with $$a$$ and $$b$$ real. The **real part** of $$z$$ is
$$\operatorname{Re} z = a$$, and the **imaginary part** of $$z$$ is $$\operatorname{Im} z = b$$.

:::

For example, $$\operatorname{Re}(3 - 2i) = 3$$ and $$\operatorname{Im}(3 - 2i) = -2$$. Notice that
the imaginary part is the real number $$-2$$, not $$-2i$$: it is the number in front of $$i$$, how
far up the point is. By part 2 of :ref{#i-squared}, two complex numbers are equal exactly when their
real parts are equal and their imaginary parts are equal.

Adding works one part at a time, so
$$\operatorname{Re}(z + w) = \operatorname{Re} z + \operatorname{Re} w$$, and the same for imaginary
parts. Multiplying does not. By the rule for multiplying,

```math
\operatorname{Re}(zw) = (\operatorname{Re} z)(\operatorname{Re} w) - (\operatorname{Im} z)(\operatorname{Im} w)
```

For example, $$(1 + 2i)(3 - i) = 5 + 5i$$, and $$1 \cdot 3 - 2 \cdot (-1) = 5$$ is its real part.

## The conjugate: a flip in the real axis

Hold a mirror along the real axis. The point $$3 + 2i$$, two above the axis, appears at $$3 - 2i$$,
two below it, and a point on the axis stays where it is. Flipping a complex number in the real axis
keeps its real part and changes the sign of its imaginary part.

:::definition[Conjugate]{#conjugate}

The **conjugate** of the complex number $$z = a + bi$$, with $$a$$ and $$b$$ real, is

```math
\bar z = a - bi
```

read "$$z$$ bar". As a map of the plane, $$z \mapsto \bar z$$ is the reflection
$$(x, y) \mapsto (x, -y)$$ in the real axis (:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/axis-reflections}).

:::

<ConjugateFlip caption="The conjugate is the mirror image in the real axis (purple). z = 3 + 2i flips to 3 − 2i, and w = −2 + 3i flips to −2 − 3i. The real number 1 lies on the mirror, so it is its own conjugate." />

The conjugate changes the sign of the imaginary part only. So the conjugate of $$-3 + 2i$$ is
$$-3 - 2i$$, and not $$3 - 2i$$, which would flip the real part as well. Conjugating also fits with
adding and multiplying. With $$z = 3 + 2i$$ and $$w = -1 + 4i$$,

```math
zw = -3 + 12i - 2i + 8i^2 = -11 + 10i \qquad \bar z\,\bar w = (3 - 2i)(-1 - 4i) = -3 - 12i + 2i + 8i^2 = -11 - 10i
```

so the conjugate of the product is the product of the conjugates.

:::proposition[Conjugates of sums and products]{#conjugate-rules}

For all complex numbers $$z$$ and $$w$$:

1. $$\overline{z + w} = \bar z + \bar w$$
2. $$\overline{zw} = \bar z\,\bar w$$
3. $$\overline{\bar z} = z$$
4. $$z + \bar z = 2\operatorname{Re} z$$
5. $$z - \bar z = 2i\operatorname{Im} z$$
6. $$\bar z = z$$ exactly when $$z$$ is real

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_conjugate#Properties"}

Write $$z = a + bi$$ and $$w = c + di$$ with $$a$$, $$b$$, $$c$$ and $$d$$ real.

1. $$z + w = (a + c) + (b + d)i$$, so its conjugate is
   $$(a + c) - (b + d)i = (a - bi) + (c - di) = \bar z + \bar w$$.
2. By the rule for multiplying, $$zw = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i$$, so
   $$\overline{zw} = (ac - bd) - (ad + bc)i$$. The same rule, used on $$a + (-b)i$$ and
   $$c + (-d)i$$, gives

   ```math
   \bar z\,\bar w = \big(ac - (-b)(-d)\big) + \big(a(-d) + (-b)c\big)i = (ac - bd) - (ad + bc)i
   ```

   using :ref{#numbers/two-minus} and :ref{#numbers/minus-in-product}. The two agree.

3. $$\bar z = a + (-b)i$$, so its conjugate is $$a - (-b)i = a + bi = z$$.
4. $$z + \bar z = (a + bi) + (a - bi) = 2a$$, and $$a = \operatorname{Re} z$$.
5. $$z - \bar z = (a + bi) - (a - bi) = 2bi$$, and $$b = \operatorname{Im} z$$.
6. $$\bar z = z$$ says $$a - bi = a + bi$$, which by part 2 of :ref{#i-squared} holds exactly when
   $$-b = b$$, that is, $$b = 0$$. And $$b = 0$$ says that $$z = a$$ is real.

:::

Parts 4 and 5 give the two parts back from $$z$$ and $$\bar z$$:
$$\operatorname{Re} z = \frac{z + \bar z}{2}$$ and $$\operatorname{Im} z = \frac{z - \bar z}{2i}$$,
once we can divide by $$2i$$, which comes later in this guide.

## The absolute value: a distance from 0

The treasure at $$3 + 4i$$ lies 5 paces from the oak in a straight line. The walk 3 east and 4 north
makes the two shorter sides of a right triangle, and the straight line is the long side, so by
Pythagoras its length is $$\sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{25} = 5$$. That distance from $$0$$ is the size
of the complex number.

:::definition[Absolute value]{#complex-abs}

The **absolute value** of the complex number $$z = a + bi$$, with $$a$$ and $$b$$ real, is

```math
|z| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}
```

It is the length of the point $$(a, b)$$ (:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/norm}), which is its
distance from $$0$$. It is also called the **modulus** of $$z$$.

:::

<AbsoluteCircle caption="The absolute value of 3 + 4i is 5, the long side of the right triangle with sides 3 and 4. Every point on the circle of radius 5 about 0 has absolute value 5, including 5, −4 + 3i, −5i and 4 − 3i." />

The absolute value of $$a + bi$$ is $$\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$$, and not $$a + b$$. For $$3 + 4i$$ the slip
gives $$3 + 4 = 7$$, which is the length of the walk along the grid lines, not the straight line.
For a real number $$a = a + 0i$$, the definition gives $$\sqrt{a^2}$$, which is the old absolute
value $$|a|$$ of :ref{#real-numbers/absolute-value}, by part 4 of :ref{#real-numbers/abs-basics}. So
$$|-7| = 7$$ means the same thing for both kinds of number.

Multiplying a number by its conjugate gives the square of its absolute value. With numbers,
$$(3 + 4i)(3 - 4i) = 9 - 12i + 12i - 16i^2 = 9 + 16 = 25$$, which is $$5^2$$. The terms in $$i$$
cancel, so the answer is real.

:::proposition[A number times its conjugate]{#times-conjugate}

For every complex number $$z$$, $$z\bar z = |z|^2$$. This is a real number, it is never negative,
and it is $$0$$ exactly when $$z = 0$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Product_of_Complex_Number_with_Conjugate"}

Write $$z = a + bi$$ with $$a$$ and $$b$$ real. By the rule for multiplying, used on $$a + bi$$ and
$$a + (-b)i$$,

```math
\begin{aligned}
z\bar z &= \big(a \cdot a - b(-b)\big) + \big(a(-b) + ba\big)i &&\qquad \text{rule for multiplying} \\
&= (a^2 + b^2) + 0i &&\qquad \text{two minus signs, and } -ab + ab = 0 \\
&= |z|^2 &&\qquad \text{the square of } \sqrt{a^2 + b^2} \text{ is } a^2 + b^2
\end{aligned}
```

The last step is the definition of the square root (:ref{#real-numbers/square-root}). The number
$$a^2 + b^2$$ is real and never negative by :ref{#real-numbers/squares-positive}, and by part 2 of
the same corollary it is $$0$$ exactly when $$a = 0$$ and $$b = 0$$, that is, when $$z = 0$$.

:::

## Dividing

To divide by a real number like $$5$$, you multiply by $$\frac{1}{5}$$, the number that multiplies
$$5$$ to give $$1$$. For a complex number $$z$$ we want the same: a number that multiplies $$z$$ to
give $$1$$. The conjugate nearly does it, since $$z\bar z = |z|^2$$ is a real number. Dividing that
real number by $$|z|^2$$ leaves $$1$$.

Here it is with numbers, for $$z = 1 + 2i$$. Its conjugate is $$1 - 2i$$, and
$$|z|^2 = 1^2 + 2^2 = 5$$, so the inverse should be
$$\frac{1}{5}(1 - 2i) = \frac{1}{5} - \frac{2}{5}i$$. As a check, $$(1 + 2i)(1 - 2i) = 1 + 4 = 5$$,
so

```math
(1 + 2i) \cdot \tfrac{1}{5}(1 - 2i) = \tfrac{1}{5} \cdot 5 = 1
```

:::theorem[Dividing complex numbers]{#complex-inverse}

Every complex number $$z \ne 0$$ has exactly one inverse: a complex number $$\frac{1}{z}$$, also
written $$z^{-1}$$, with $$z \cdot \frac{1}{z} = 1$$. It is

```math
\frac{1}{z} = \frac{\bar z}{|z|^2} \qquad\text{that is,}\qquad \frac{1}{a + bi} = \frac{a}{a^2 + b^2} - \frac{b}{a^2 + b^2}\,i
```

For complex numbers $$w$$ and $$z \ne 0$$, the quotient is $$\frac{w}{z} = w \cdot \frac{1}{z}$$,
which is $$\frac{w\bar z}{|z|^2}$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Inverse_for_Complex_Multiplication"}

**There is one.** Since $$z \ne 0$$, the real number $$|z|^2$$ is not $$0$$, by
:ref{#times-conjugate}. So it has a real inverse $$\frac{1}{|z|^2}$$, by rule 5 of
:ref{#real-numbers/real-rules}. Let $$u = \frac{1}{|z|^2}\,\bar z$$. Then

```math
\begin{aligned}
zu &= z\left(\tfrac{1}{|z|^2}\,\bar z\right) &&\qquad \text{the number } u \\
&= \tfrac{1}{|z|^2}\,(z\bar z) &&\qquad \text{order and grouping don't matter} \\
&= \tfrac{1}{|z|^2}\,|z|^2 &&\qquad \text{a number times its conjugate} \\
&= 1 &&\qquad \text{a real number times its inverse}
\end{aligned}
```

The second step uses rules 1 and 2 of :ref{#complex-arithmetic}, and the last is a product of real
numbers, by part 2 of :ref{#complex-reals}. Writing $$z = a + bi$$, part 3 of :ref{#complex-reals}
gives $$u = \frac{a}{a^2 + b^2} - \frac{b}{a^2 + b^2}\,i$$.

**There is only one.** Suppose $$zv = 1$$ as well. Then, using :ref{#complex-arithmetic} at each
step,

```math
\begin{aligned}
v &= 1 \cdot v &&\qquad \text{one times anything} \\
&= (uz)v &&\qquad uz = zu = 1 \\
&= u(zv) &&\qquad \text{grouping doesn't matter} \\
&= u \cdot 1 = u &&\qquad zv = 1
\end{aligned}
```

**The quotient.** By definition
$$\frac{w}{z} = w \cdot \frac{1}{z} = w \cdot \frac{1}{|z|^2}\,\bar z$$, and by order and grouping
this is $$\frac{1}{|z|^2}\,w\bar z$$, which is written $$\frac{w\bar z}{|z|^2}$$.

:::

In practice you divide by multiplying the top and the bottom by the conjugate of the bottom. The
bottom then becomes the real number $$|z|^2$$. For example,

```math
\begin{aligned}
\frac{4 + 3i}{1 + 2i} &= \frac{(4 + 3i)(1 - 2i)}{(1 + 2i)(1 - 2i)} &&\qquad \text{multiply top and bottom by } 1 - 2i \\
&= \frac{4 - 8i + 3i - 6i^2}{1 + 4} &&\qquad \text{multiply out} \\
&= \frac{10 - 5i}{5} &&\qquad i^2 = -1 \\
&= 2 - i &&\qquad \text{divide each part by } 5
\end{aligned}
```

The first line is the quotient in :ref{#complex-inverse}, since $$\frac{w\bar z}{|z|^2}$$ has
$$w\bar z$$ on top and $$z\bar z = |z|^2$$ on the bottom. As a check, multiply back:
$$(2 - i)(1 + 2i) = 2 + 4i - i - 2i^2 = 4 + 3i$$. One more worth knowing is $$\frac{1}{i} = -i$$,
since $$i(-i) = -i^2 = 1$$. So dividing by $$i$$ is multiplying by $$-i$$.

With inverses, complex numbers obey all six rules of :ref{#real-numbers/real-rules}. So every result
the Real numbers chapter proved from those six rules alone holds for complex numbers too. The one
we'll need most is about products equal to zero.

:::proposition[Products equal to zero]{#complex-zero-product}

For complex numbers $$z$$ and $$w$$, if $$zw = 0$$, then $$z = 0$$ or $$w = 0$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Field_has_no_Proper_Zero_Divisors"}

If $$z = 0$$ there is nothing to prove. Otherwise $$z$$ has an inverse, by :ref{#complex-inverse},
and

```math
\begin{aligned}
w &= 1 \cdot w &&\qquad \text{one times anything} \\
&= \left(\tfrac{1}{z}\,z\right)w &&\qquad \tfrac{1}{z}\,z = 1 \\
&= \tfrac{1}{z}\,(zw) &&\qquad \text{grouping doesn't matter} \\
&= \tfrac{1}{z} \cdot 0 = 0 &&\qquad zw = 0, \text{ and zero times anything is zero}
\end{aligned}
```

The last step is $$(c, d)(0, 0) = (0, 0)$$, straight from the rule for multiplying.

:::

This gives the second proof of :ref{#square-roots-minus-one} promised in
[Meeting i](./meeting-i.mdx). By :ref{#numbers/square-formulas},
$$(z - i)(z + i) = z^2 - i^2 = z^2 + 1$$. So $$z^2 = -1$$ exactly when $$(z - i)(z + i) = 0$$, and
by :ref{#complex-zero-product} that happens exactly when $$z = i$$ or $$z = -i$$.

## The absolute value of a product

In [Meeting i](./meeting-i.mdx), the product $$(1 + 2i)(3 - i) = 5 + 5i$$ had length $$\sqrt{50}$$,
which is $$\sqrt5 \cdot \sqrt{10}$$, the two lengths multiplied. That happens for every product, so
multiplying by $$w$$ stretches every distance from $$0$$ by $$|w|$$. The guide
[Multiplying is turning](./polar-form.mdx) shows what happens to the angles.

:::proposition[Absolute value of a product]{#abs-product-complex}

For all complex numbers $$z$$ and $$w$$,

```math
|zw| = |z|\,|w| \qquad\text{and, if } w \ne 0, \qquad \left|\frac{z}{w}\right| = \frac{|z|}{|w|}
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Complex_Modulus_of_Product_of_Complex_Numbers"}

**The product.** Square the left side, and use :ref{#times-conjugate} and :ref{#conjugate-rules}:

```math
\begin{aligned}
|zw|^2 &= (zw)\,\overline{zw} &&\qquad \text{a number times its conjugate} \\
&= zw\,\bar z\,\bar w &&\qquad \text{the conjugate of a product} \\
&= (z\bar z)(w\bar w) &&\qquad \text{order and grouping don't matter} \\
&= |z|^2\,|w|^2 &&\qquad \text{a number times its conjugate, twice} \\
&= \big(|z|\,|w|\big)^2 &&\qquad (xy)^2 = x^2y^2
\end{aligned}
```

The last step is :ref{#numbers/power-rules} for the real numbers $$|z|$$ and $$|w|$$. Both $$|zw|$$
and $$|z|\,|w|$$ are real and never negative, and they have the same square, so they are equal by
:ref{#real-numbers/equal-squares}.

**The quotient.** Since $$w \cdot \frac{z}{w} = z$$, the product part gives
$$|w|\left|\frac{z}{w}\right| = |z|$$. The number $$|w|$$ is not $$0$$, because $$w \ne 0$$ (by
:ref{#times-conjugate}), so dividing by it gives $$\left|\frac{z}{w}\right| = \frac{|z|}{|w|}$$.

:::

For example, $$|(3 + 4i)(5 - 12i)| = 5 \cdot 13 = 65$$ without multiplying out. As a check,
$$(3 + 4i)(5 - 12i) = 15 - 36i + 20i - 48i^2 = 63 - 16i$$, and
$$\sqrt{63^2 + 16^2} = \sqrt{3969 + 256} = \sqrt{4225} = 65$$.

## The triangle inequality

The two clues on the treasure map walk $$|4 + i| = \sqrt{17} \approx 4.12$$ paces and then
$$|-1 + 3i| = \sqrt{10} \approx 3.16$$ paces, about $$7.3$$ in all. The straight line to the
treasure is only $$|3 + 4i| = 5$$ paces. A detour is never shorter than the straight line, and the
absolute value of a sum says so.

:::theorem[The triangle inequality]{#complex-triangle}

For all complex numbers $$z$$ and $$w$$:

1. $$|z + w| \le |z| + |w|$$
2. $$|z - w|$$ is the distance between the points $$z$$ and $$w$$

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Triangle_Inequality_for_Complex_Numbers"}

By :ref{#complex-abs}, the absolute value of a complex number is the length of the point, and adding
or subtracting complex numbers is adding or subtracting points. So part 1 is
:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/norm-triangle}, and part 2 is
:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/distance-norm}.

:::

Part 2 turns a distance into an absolute value. For example, the points at distance $$2$$ from
$$1 + i$$ are the numbers $$z$$ with $$|z - (1 + i)| = 2$$, and they make up the circle of radius
$$2$$ about $$1 + i$$ (:ref{#coordinates/circle-equation}).

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on the complex plane, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-complex-plane-parts}

For each number, give its real part, its imaginary part, its conjugate and its absolute value, and
say where it is in the plane.

1. $$5 - 12i$$
2. $$-8i$$
3. $$-6$$

:::solution

1. $$\operatorname{Re} = 5$$, $$\operatorname{Im} = -12$$, conjugate $$5 + 12i$$, and
   $$|5 - 12i| = \sqrt{25 + 144} = \sqrt{169} = 13$$. It is 5 to the right and 12 down.
2. $$\operatorname{Re} = 0$$, $$\operatorname{Im} = -8$$, conjugate $$8i$$, and
   $$|-8i| = \sqrt{0 + 64} = 8$$. It is on the imaginary axis, 8 below $$0$$.
3. $$\operatorname{Re} = -6$$, $$\operatorname{Im} = 0$$, conjugate $$-6$$ (it is real, by part 6 of
   :ref{#conjugate-rules}), and $$|-6| = \sqrt{36} = 6$$. It is on the real axis, 6 to the left of
   $$0$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-complex-plane-steps}

Let $$z = 1 + 4i$$ and $$w = 3 - 2i$$. Work out $$z + w$$ and $$z - w$$, and find the distance
between the points $$z$$ and $$w$$.

:::solution

$$z + w = (1 + 3) + (4 - 2)i = 4 + 2i$$, and $$z - w = (1 - 3) + (4 - (-2))i = -2 + 6i$$.

By part 2 of :ref{#complex-triangle}, the distance is
$$|z - w| = \sqrt{(-2)^2 + 6^2} = \sqrt{40} = 2\sqrt{10} \approx 6.32$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-complex-plane-divide}

Write each quotient in the form $$a + bi$$, and check each answer by multiplying back.

1. $$\frac{1}{3 + 4i}$$
2. $$\frac{8 + i}{2 - i}$$
3. $$\frac{5}{i}$$

:::solution

1. Multiply top and bottom by $$3 - 4i$$: the bottom becomes $$9 + 16 = 25$$, so
   $$\frac{1}{3 + 4i} = \frac{3 - 4i}{25} = \frac{3}{25} - \frac{4}{25}i$$. As a check,
   $$(3 + 4i)(3 - 4i) = 25$$, and $$\frac{25}{25} = 1$$.
2. Multiply top and bottom by $$2 + i$$: the top is
   $$(8 + i)(2 + i) = 16 + 8i + 2i + i^2 = 15 + 10i$$ and the bottom is $$4 + 1 = 5$$, so the
   quotient is $$3 + 2i$$. As a check, $$(3 + 2i)(2 - i) = 6 - 3i + 4i - 2i^2 = 8 + i$$.
3. Since $$\frac{1}{i} = -i$$, $$\frac{5}{i} = 5(-i) = -5i$$. As a check, $$(-5i)i = -5i^2 = 5$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-complex-plane-solve}

Find the complex number $$z$$ with $$(2 + i)z = 7 + i$$.

:::solution

Multiply both sides by $$\frac{1}{2 + i}$$, which exists by :ref{#complex-inverse}:

```math
\begin{aligned}
z &= \frac{7 + i}{2 + i} = \frac{(7 + i)(2 - i)}{(2 + i)(2 - i)} &&\qquad \text{multiply top and bottom by } 2 - i \\
&= \frac{14 - 7i + 2i - i^2}{4 + 1} &&\qquad \text{multiply out} \\
&= \frac{15 - 5i}{5} = 3 - i &&\qquad i^2 = -1
\end{aligned}
```

As a check, $$(2 + i)(3 - i) = 6 - 2i + 3i - i^2 = 7 + i$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-complex-plane-product-size}

Find $$|(1 + i)(2 - 3i)(4 + 4i)|$$ without multiplying out, and then check your answer by
multiplying out.

:::solution

By :ref{#abs-product-complex}, used twice, the absolute value is

```math
|1 + i|\,|2 - 3i|\,|4 + 4i| = \sqrt2 \cdot \sqrt{13} \cdot \sqrt{32} = \sqrt{2 \cdot 13 \cdot 32} = \sqrt{832} = 8\sqrt{13}
```

which is about $$28.84$$. To check, $$(1 + i)(4 + 4i) = 4 + 4i + 4i + 4i^2 = 8i$$, and
$$8i(2 - 3i) = 16i - 24i^2 = 24 + 16i$$. Then $$|24 + 16i| = \sqrt{576 + 256} = \sqrt{832}$$, the
same.

:::

Describe each set of points in the plane.

2. The numbers $$z$$ with $$|z - 1| = |z + 1|$$.
3. The numbers $$z$$ with $$\bar z = -z$$.

:::solution

1. By part 2 of :ref{#complex-triangle}, $$|z - (2 - i)|$$ is the distance from $$z$$ to the point
   $$2 - i$$. So this is the circle of radius $$3$$ about $$2 - i$$.
2. $$|z + 1| = |z - (-1)|$$ is the distance from $$z$$ to $$-1$$, so these are the points as far
   from $$1$$ as from $$-1$$. With $$z = x + yi$$, squaring both sides gives
   $$(x - 1)^2 + y^2 = (x + 1)^2 + y^2$$, which simplifies to $$-2x = 2x$$, so $$x = 0$$. The set is
   the imaginary axis.
3. With $$z = a + bi$$, $$\bar z = -z$$ says $$a - bi = -a - bi$$. Comparing real parts gives
   $$a = -a$$, so $$a = 0$$, and the imaginary parts agree for every $$b$$. The set is the imaginary
   axis again: the numbers whose mirror image in the real axis is also their half turn about $$0$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-complex-plane-parts-bound}

Prove that $$|\operatorname{Re} z| \le |z|$$ and $$|\operatorname{Im} z| \le |z|$$ for every complex
number $$z$$, and say what they mean in the picture.

:::solution

Write $$z = a + bi$$. By part 4 of :ref{#real-numbers/abs-basics}, $$|a| = \sqrt{a^2}$$, and
$$|z| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$$. If $$b = 0$$, these are equal. Otherwise $$b^2 > 0$$ by
:ref{#real-numbers/squares-positive}, so $$0 \le a^2 < a^2 + b^2$$, and taking square roots keeps
the order (:ref{#real-numbers/root-order}):

```math
|a| = \sqrt{a^2} < \sqrt{a^2 + b^2} = |z|
```

Either way $$|a| \le |z|$$. The same argument with $$a$$ and $$b$$ swapped gives $$|b| \le |z|$$.

In the picture, $$|a|$$ and $$|b|$$ are the two shorter sides of the right triangle from $$0$$ to
$$z$$, and $$|z|$$ is its long side. So each says that a shorter side is no longer than the long
side.

:::
