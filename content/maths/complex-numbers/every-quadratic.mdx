Imagine the graph of $$y = x^2 + 2x + 5$$ drawn on squared paper. It is a U-shaped curve whose
lowest point is $$(-1, 4)$$, four squares above the $$x$$-axis, and from there it climbs on both
sides. Run a pencil along the $$x$$-axis and it never touches the curve. So the equation
$$x^2 + 2x + 5 = 0$$ has no real solution, and its discriminant $$2^2 - 4 \cdot 5 = -16$$ is
negative, as :ref{#quadratics/root-count} predicts.

Now look off the line, in the complex plane. The numbers $$-1 + 2i$$ and $$-1 - 2i$$ both solve the
equation. For the first, $$(-1 + 2i)^2 = 1 - 4i + 4i^2 = -3 - 4i$$, so
$$x^2 + 2x + 5 = (-3 - 4i) + (-2 + 4i) + 5 = 0$$. The two solutions sit straight above and below
$$-1$$, where the curve has its lowest point, and each is the mirror image of the other in the real
axis.

<ParabolaAndRoots caption="The graph of y = x² + 2x + 5, which never meets the x-axis, and the two solutions of x² + 2x + 5 = 0 in the complex plane, −1 + 2i and −1 − 2i, mirror images in the real axis." />

The guide [The discriminant](../quadratics/discriminant.mdx) made a promise about equations like
this one: among the complex numbers, every quadratic equation has solutions, given by the same
formula. In this guide we'll keep that promise, for real coefficients and for complex ones, and see
what it says about equations of higher degree.

## The quadratic formula, with complex numbers

The quadratic formula came from completing the square, and completing the square only adds,
multiplies and divides by numbers other than $$0$$. Complex numbers obey all the same rules
(:ref{#complex-arithmetic}). The one step that failed for a negative discriminant was taking a
square root, and :ref{#complex-square-roots} says every complex number has one. So the formula works
with no exceptions left.

Here it is on the opening equation, with $$a = 1$$, $$b = 2$$ and $$c = 5$$. The discriminant is
$$-16$$, and $$4i$$ is a square root of it, since $$(4i)^2 = 16i^2 = -16$$. Putting $$4i$$ where the
formula had $$\sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}$$ gives

```math
z = \frac{-2 \pm 4i}{2} = -1 \pm 2i
```

These are the two solutions from the picture.

:::theorem[The quadratic formula for complex numbers]{#complex-quadratic-formula}

Let $$a$$, $$b$$ and $$c$$ be complex numbers with $$a \ne 0$$, and let $$w$$ be a square root of
the discriminant $$b^2 - 4ac$$ (:ref{#complex-square-roots}). Then the solutions of
$$az^2 + bz + c = 0$$ are exactly

```math
z = \frac{-b + w}{2a} \qquad\text{and}\qquad z = \frac{-b - w}{2a}
```

These are two different solutions when $$b^2 - 4ac \ne 0$$, and one solution, $$z = -\frac{b}{2a}$$,
when $$b^2 - 4ac = 0$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Quadratic_Formula"}

**Divide by a.** Since $$a \ne 0$$, it has an inverse (:ref{#complex-inverse}). Multiplying both
sides by $$\frac{1}{a}$$, or back by $$a$$, keeps the solutions the same, so the equation has the
same solutions as

```math
z^2 + \frac{b}{a}z + \frac{c}{a} = 0
```

**Complete the square.** The proof of :ref{#quadratics/complete-square} expands a square and moves a
number to the other side, and nothing else. Those steps use only the rules of arithmetic, so they
hold for complex numbers too (:ref{#complex-arithmetic}). With $$\frac{b}{a}$$ in place of $$b$$,
the equation has the same solutions as

```math
\begin{aligned}
\left(z + \frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 &= \frac{b^2}{4a^2} - \frac{c}{a} && \text{complete the square} \\
&= \frac{b^2 - 4ac}{4a^2} && \text{over a common denominator} \\
&= \left(\frac{w}{2a}\right)^2 && \text{since } w^2 = b^2 - 4ac
\end{aligned}
```

**Solve.** Write $$u = z + \frac{b}{2a}$$ and $$v = \frac{w}{2a}$$, so the equation says
$$u^2 = v^2$$. Multiplying out, $$(u - v)(u + v) = u^2 - v^2$$, so the equation says
$$(u - v)(u + v) = 0$$. By :ref{#complex-zero-product}, that happens exactly when $$u = v$$ or
$$u = -v$$, that is, when

```math
z = -\frac{b}{2a} + \frac{w}{2a} = \frac{-b + w}{2a} \qquad\text{or}\qquad z = -\frac{b}{2a} - \frac{w}{2a} = \frac{-b - w}{2a}
```

**Count.** The two answers are equal exactly when $$w = -w$$, which means $$2w = 0$$, so $$w = 0$$.
And $$w = 0$$ exactly when $$b^2 - 4ac = w^2 = 0$$, by :ref{#complex-zero-product} applied to
$$w \cdot w$$. When $$w = 0$$, both answers are $$-\frac{b}{2a}$$.

:::

This keeps the promise made in [The discriminant](../quadratics/discriminant.mdx). Over the real
numbers, a negative discriminant meant no solution (:ref{#quadratics/root-count}). Over the complex
numbers there is always a solution, and there are two exactly when the discriminant isn't $$0$$.

It doesn't matter which square root of the discriminant you take. The other one is $$-w$$, and
swapping $$w$$ for $$-w$$ swaps the two answers.

:::example[A quadratic with complex coefficients]

Solve $$z^2 - (3 + i)z + (2 + 2i) = 0$$.

Here $$a = 1$$, $$b = -(3 + i)$$ and $$c = 2 + 2i$$. The discriminant is

```math
b^2 - 4ac = (3 + i)^2 - 4(2 + 2i) = (9 + 6i - 1) - 8 - 8i = -2i
```

For a square root $$x + iy$$ of $$-2i$$, compare parts as in
[Roots of complex numbers](./roots.mdx): $$x^2 - y^2 = 0$$ and $$2xy = -2$$, with
$$x^2 + y^2 = |-2i| = 2$$. So $$x^2 = y^2 = 1$$, and $$xy = -1$$, which gives $$w = 1 - i$$. Check:
$$(1 - i)^2 = 1 - 2i + i^2 = -2i$$. By :ref{#complex-quadratic-formula},

```math
z = \frac{(3 + i) + (1 - i)}{2} = 2 \qquad z = \frac{(3 + i) - (1 - i)}{2} = 1 + i
```

As a check, putting in $$z = 2$$ gives $$4 - (6 + 2i) + 2 + 2i = 0$$.

:::

The two solutions of a real quadratic add up to $$-\frac{b}{a}$$ and multiply to $$\frac{c}{a}$$
(:ref{#quadratics/vieta}). The same is true with complex numbers, and the proof is the one from the
real case, with $$w$$ in place of the real square root. In the example, $$2 + (1 + i) = 3 + i$$ and
$$2(1 + i) = 2 + 2i$$, which match.

:::proposition[Sum and product of the solutions]{#every-quadratic-vieta}

Let $$a$$, $$b$$, $$c$$ and $$w$$ be as in :ref{#complex-quadratic-formula}, and write
$$z_1 = \frac{-b + w}{2a}$$ and $$z_2 = \frac{-b - w}{2a}$$. Then

```math
z_1 + z_2 = -\frac{b}{a} \qquad z_1 z_2 = \frac{c}{a}
```

and $$az^2 + bz + c = a(z - z_1)(z - z_2)$$ for every complex number $$z$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vieta%27s_formulas"}

**Sum.** The $$w$$'s cancel:
$$z_1 + z_2 = \frac{-b + w - b - w}{2a} = \frac{-2b}{2a} = -\frac{b}{a}$$.

**Product.** The top is a difference of two squares, $$(-b + w)(-b - w) = b^2 - w^2$$, and
$$w^2 = b^2 - 4ac$$, so

```math
z_1 z_2 = \frac{b^2 - w^2}{4a^2} = \frac{b^2 - (b^2 - 4ac)}{4a^2} = \frac{4ac}{4a^2} = \frac{c}{a}
```

**Factors.** Multiplying out and then using the sum and the product,

```math
\begin{aligned}
a(z - z_1)(z - z_2) &= a\big(z^2 - (z_1 + z_2)z + z_1 z_2\big) && \text{multiply out} \\
&= a\left(z^2 + \frac{b}{a}z + \frac{c}{a}\right) && \text{the sum and the product} \\
&= az^2 + bz + c
\end{aligned}
```

:::

## Real quadratics with a negative discriminant

When $$a$$, $$b$$ and $$c$$ are real and the discriminant is negative, its square root needs no
work. The number $$4ac - b^2$$ is positive, so it has a real square root, and multiplying that by
$$i$$ gives a square root of the discriminant. The solutions then share the real part
$$-\frac{b}{2a}$$ and have opposite imaginary parts, so they are mirror images in the real axis, as
$$-1 \pm 2i$$ were in the opening picture.

:::corollary[Real quadratics with negative discriminant]{#negative-discriminant}

Let $$a$$, $$b$$ and $$c$$ be real numbers with $$a \ne 0$$ and $$b^2 - 4ac < 0$$. Then
$$az^2 + bz + c = 0$$ has exactly two complex solutions,

```math
z = \frac{-b + i\sqrt{4ac - b^2}}{2a} \qquad\text{and}\qquad z = \frac{-b - i\sqrt{4ac - b^2}}{2a}
```

and each is the conjugate of the other.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_equation#Discriminant"}

Since $$b^2 - 4ac < 0$$, the number $$4ac - b^2$$ is positive, so it has a real square root
$$s = \sqrt{4ac - b^2}$$ (:ref{#real-numbers/square-root}). Then

```math
(is)^2 = i^2 s^2 = -(4ac - b^2) = b^2 - 4ac
```

So $$w = is$$ is a square root of the discriminant, and :ref{#complex-quadratic-formula} gives the
two solutions in the statement. They are different, since the discriminant isn't $$0$$.

Both solutions have real part $$-\frac{b}{2a}$$, and their imaginary parts are $$\frac{s}{2a}$$ and
$$-\frac{s}{2a}$$, all real numbers because $$a$$, $$b$$ and $$s$$ are real. Two numbers with the
same real part and opposite imaginary parts are conjugates (:ref{#conjugate}).

:::

The real part $$-\frac{b}{2a}$$ is where the parabola $$y = ax^2 + bx + c$$ turns
(:ref{#quadratics/vertex}). So the solutions always sit straight above and below that point of the
real axis, as in the opening picture.

:::example[Two real quadratics]

**$$x^2 + 2x + 5 = 0$$.** Here $$4ac - b^2 = 20 - 4 = 16$$, and $$\sqrt{16} = 4$$, so
$$z = \frac{-2 \pm 4i}{2} = -1 \pm 2i$$, the solutions from the start of the guide.

**$$5x^2 + 2x + 1 = 0$$.** This is the equation in
[The quadratic formula](../quadratics/quadratic-formula.mdx) found to have no real solution. Here
$$4ac - b^2 = 20 - 4 = 16$$ again, so

```math
z = \frac{-2 \pm 4i}{10} = -\frac{1}{5} \pm \frac{2}{5}i
```

To check $$z = -\frac{1}{5} + \frac{2}{5}i$$, first
$$z^2 = \frac{1}{25} - \frac{4}{25}i + \frac{4}{25}i^2 = -\frac{3}{25} - \frac{4}{25}i$$. Then

```math
5z^2 + 2z + 1 = \left(-\tfrac{3}{5} - \tfrac{4}{5}i\right) + \left(-\tfrac{2}{5} + \tfrac{4}{5}i\right) + 1 = 0
```

The sum and product agree with :ref{#every-quadratic-vieta}: the sum is
$$-\frac{2}{5} = -\frac{b}{a}$$, and the product is
$$z\bar z = \left(\frac{1}{5}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{2}{5}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{5} = \frac{c}{a}$$
(:ref{#times-conjugate}).

:::

## Factoring sums of squares

A sum of two squares such as $$x^2 + 9$$ can't be factored with real numbers. It is never $$0$$ for
real $$x$$ (:ref{#real-numbers/squares-positive}), and a real factor $$x - c$$ would make it $$0$$
at $$x = c$$. With complex numbers it becomes a difference of two squares, because $$9 = -(3i)^2$$:

```math
x^2 + 9 = x^2 - (3i)^2 = (x - 3i)(x + 3i)
```

As a check, $$(x - 3i)(x + 3i) = x^2 + 3ix - 3ix - 9i^2 = x^2 + 9$$. In the same way, for any $$u$$
and $$v$$, $$u^2 + v^2 = (u - iv)(u + iv)$$.

Every quadratic factors like this, by the last part of :ref{#every-quadratic-vieta}. For the opening
equation, the solutions $$-1 \pm 2i$$ give

```math
x^2 + 2x + 5 = (x + 1 - 2i)(x + 1 + 2i)
```

and multiplying out the right side as a difference of two squares gives
$$(x + 1)^2 - (2i)^2 = x^2 + 2x + 1 + 4$$, which is the left side.

## Conjugate pairs

The solutions of a real quadratic with a negative discriminant come as mirror images, $$z$$ and
$$\bar z$$. The reason reaches beyond quadratics. The conjugate is the flip in the real axis, it
respects adding and multiplying (:ref{#conjugate-rules}), and it leaves real numbers where they are.
So flipping a true equation whose coefficients are real gives another true equation, now about
$$\bar z$$.

:::proposition[Conjugate pairs]{#conjugate-pairs}

Let $$f(z) = a_n z^n + \cdots + a_1 z + a_0$$ be a complex polynomial whose coefficients
$$a_0, \ldots, a_n$$ are all real numbers. If $$z$$ is a root of $$f$$, then so is $$\bar z$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_conjugate_root_theorem"}

By :ref{#conjugate-rules}, the conjugate of a product is the product of the conjugates. Using that
$$k - 1$$ times, $$\overline{z^k} = \bar z^{\,k}$$. By the same proposition, $$\bar a = a$$ for
every real number $$a$$. So

```math
\begin{aligned}
\overline{f(z)} &= \overline{a_n}\,\overline{z^n} + \cdots + \overline{a_1}\,\bar z + \overline{a_0} && \text{conjugate of sums and products} \\
&= a_n \bar z^{\,n} + \cdots + a_1 \bar z + a_0 && \text{the coefficients are real} \\
&= f(\bar z)
\end{aligned}
```

If $$f(z) = 0$$, then $$f(\bar z) = \overline{0} = 0$$.

:::

So the roots of a real polynomial that aren't real come in pairs, $$z$$ and $$\bar z$$. The
coefficients do have to be real. In the example with complex coefficients, $$1 + i$$ is a root of
$$z^2 - (3 + i)z + (2 + 2i)$$, but putting in $$1 - i$$ gives

```math
(1 - i)^2 - (3 + i)(1 - i) + 2 + 2i = -2i - (4 - 2i) + 2 + 2i = -2 + 2i
```

which isn't $$0$$.

:::example[The roots of z³ − 1]

The difference of two powers (:ref{#functions/polynomials-difference-of-powers}), which holds for
complex numbers (:ref{#complex-root-bound}), gives

```math
z^3 - 1 = (z - 1)(z^2 + z + 1)
```

By :ref{#complex-zero-product}, the roots are $$1$$ and the solutions of $$z^2 + z + 1 = 0$$. That
quadratic is the one [The discriminant](../quadratics/discriminant.mdx) found to have no real
solution. Its discriminant is $$1 - 4 = -3$$, so by :ref{#negative-discriminant},

```math
z = \frac{-1 \pm i\sqrt{3}}{2} = -\frac{1}{2} \pm \frac{\sqrt3}{2}i
```

These are a conjugate pair, as :ref{#conjugate-pairs} says they must be. They are also the cube
roots of unity $$e^{2\pi i/3}$$ and $$e^{4\pi i/3}$$ from :ref{#roots-of-unity-polygon}, so
factoring and turning give the same three numbers: one real, and two that are mirror images.

:::

## Every polynomial has a root

Going from the real numbers to the complex numbers gave every quadratic its solutions. You might
expect cubics, or equations with $$z^5$$ in them, to need yet more new numbers. They don't, and that
is the fundamental theorem of algebra.

:::remark[The fundamental theorem of algebra]{#fundamental-theorem-algebra}

Every complex polynomial $$f(z) = a_n z^n + \cdots + a_1 z + a_0$$ with $$n \ge 1$$ and
$$a_n \ne 0$$ has at least one complex root. Using this and the factor theorem in
:ref{#complex-root-bound} $$n$$ times, once for each factor, it follows that

```math
f(z) = a_n (z - c_1)(z - c_2) \cdots (z - c_n)
```

for some complex numbers $$c_1, \ldots, c_n$$, which need not be different. Every proof of the
theorem needs ideas from beyond these notes, so it is taken as given here, and no proof in these
notes uses it.

::see-also{src="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_theorem_of_algebra"}

:::

For quadratics, this guide proved the theorem: :ref{#every-quadratic-vieta} gives
$$az^2 + bz + c = a(z - z_1)(z - z_2)$$. For $$z^3 - 1$$ the factors are $$z - 1$$ and the two
factors from the conjugate pair. To solve $$x^2 + 1 = 0$$, the real numbers needed new numbers
added. The theorem says the complex numbers need none: every polynomial equation with complex
coefficients already has all its solutions among them.

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on every quadratic having solutions, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-every-quadratic-solve}

Solve each equation, and check the sum and product of the solutions.

1. $$z^2 + 4z + 13 = 0$$
2. $$z^2 - 6z + 10 = 0$$

:::solution

Both have real coefficients and a negative discriminant, so use :ref{#negative-discriminant}.

1. Here $$4ac - b^2 = 52 - 16 = 36$$, and $$\sqrt{36} = 6$$, so
   $$z = \frac{-4 \pm 6i}{2} = -2 \pm 3i$$. The sum is $$-4 = -\frac{b}{a}$$, and the product is
   $$(-2)^2 + 3^2 = 13 = \frac{c}{a}$$.
2. Here $$4ac - b^2 = 40 - 36 = 4$$, and $$\sqrt{4} = 2$$, so $$z = \frac{6 \pm 2i}{2} = 3 \pm i$$.
   The sum is $$6$$ and the product is $$3^2 + 1^2 = 10$$, matching $$-\frac{b}{a}$$ and
   $$\frac{c}{a}$$.

The product of a conjugate pair $$p \pm qi$$ is $$p^2 + q^2$$ (:ref{#times-conjugate}), which is why
the products came out as sums of squares.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-every-quadratic-factor}

Factor each expression into two factors of the form $$px + q$$ with complex numbers $$p$$ and $$q$$.

1. $$x^2 + 16$$
2. $$4x^2 + 25$$
3. $$x^2 - 2x + 2$$

:::solution

1. $$x^2 + 16 = x^2 - (4i)^2 = (x - 4i)(x + 4i)$$.
2. $$4x^2 + 25 = (2x)^2 - (5i)^2 = (2x - 5i)(2x + 5i)$$.
3. The discriminant is $$4 - 8 = -4$$, so by :ref{#negative-discriminant} the solutions of
   $$x^2 - 2x + 2 = 0$$ are $$\frac{2 \pm 2i}{2} = 1 \pm i$$. By :ref{#every-quadratic-vieta},
   $$x^2 - 2x + 2 = (x - 1 - i)(x - 1 + i)$$. Check: the right side is
   $$(x - 1)^2 - i^2 = x^2 - 2x + 1 + 1$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-every-quadratic-substitute}

Show by substituting that $$2 - 3i$$ is a solution of $$z^2 - 4z + 13 = 0$$. Then find the other
solution without using the formula.

:::solution

First $$(2 - 3i)^2 = 4 - 12i + 9i^2 = -5 - 12i$$. So

```math
z^2 - 4z + 13 = (-5 - 12i) - (8 - 12i) + 13 = -5 - 8 + 13 + (-12 + 12)i = 0
```

The coefficients are real, so by :ref{#conjugate-pairs} the conjugate $$2 + 3i$$ is a solution too.
By :ref{#complex-quadratic-formula} there are at most two, so it is the other one. As a check, the
sum is $$4 = -\frac{b}{a}$$ (:ref{#every-quadratic-vieta}).

:::

::::exercise{#ex-every-quadratic-build}

Find a quadratic equation $$z^2 + bz + c = 0$$ with real $$b$$ and $$c$$ that has $$3 + 2i$$ as a
solution. Check your answer with the formula.

:::solution

With real coefficients, the other solution has to be $$3 - 2i$$ (:ref{#conjugate-pairs}). The sum is
$$6$$ and the product is $$3^2 + 2^2 = 13$$, so by :ref{#every-quadratic-vieta} the equation is
$$z^2 - 6z + 13 = 0$$. Check: $$4ac - b^2 = 52 - 36 = 16$$, so $$z = \frac{6 \pm 4i}{2} = 3 \pm 2i$$
(:ref{#negative-discriminant}).

:::

::::exercise{#ex-every-quadratic-complex}

Solve $$z^2 + (1 - i)z - (2 + 2i) = 0$$, and check that the two solutions add up to $$-(1 - i)$$.

:::solution

The discriminant is $$(1 - i)^2 + 4(2 + 2i) = -2i + 8 + 8i = 8 + 6i$$. For a square root $$x + iy$$,
compare parts: $$x^2 - y^2 = 8$$ and $$2xy = 6$$, with
$$x^2 + y^2 = |8 + 6i| = \sqrt{64 + 36} = 10$$. So $$x^2 = 9$$ and $$y^2 = 1$$, with $$xy = 3$$, and
$$w = 3 + i$$. Check: $$(3 + i)^2 = 9 + 6i - 1 = 8 + 6i$$. By :ref{#complex-quadratic-formula},

```math
z = \frac{-(1 - i) + (3 + i)}{2} = 1 + i \qquad z = \frac{-(1 - i) - (3 + i)}{2} = -2
```

Their sum is $$-1 + i = -(1 - i)$$, as :ref{#every-quadratic-vieta} says. The solutions are not a
conjugate pair, and they don't need to be, since the coefficients aren't all real.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-every-quadratic-length}

Let $$a$$, $$b$$ and $$c$$ be real with $$a \ne 0$$ and $$b^2 - 4ac < 0$$. Show that both solutions
of $$az^2 + bz + c = 0$$ have length $$\sqrt{\frac{c}{a}}$$, so they lie on one circle centred at
$$0$$. Check this for $$z^2 + 2z + 5 = 0$$.

:::solution

By :ref{#negative-discriminant}, the solutions are $$z_1$$ and $$z_2 = \bar z_1$$. By
:ref{#every-quadratic-vieta} and :ref{#times-conjugate},

```math
\frac{c}{a} = z_1 z_2 = z_1 \bar z_1 = |z_1|^2
```

So $$\frac{c}{a} \ge 0$$, and $$|z_1| = \sqrt{\frac{c}{a}}$$. The conjugate has the same length,
since flipping in the real axis keeps the distance from $$0$$, so $$|z_2| = |z_1|$$. For
$$z^2 + 2z + 5 = 0$$, the solutions $$-1 \pm 2i$$ have length $$\sqrt{1 + 4} = \sqrt5$$, and
$$\sqrt{\frac{c}{a}} = \sqrt5$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-every-quadratic-cubic}

Find all three solutions of $$z^3 = 27$$ by factoring $$z^3 - 27$$, and check that they are $$3$$
times the cube roots of unity.

:::solution

By :ref{#functions/polynomials-difference-of-powers} with $$c = 3$$,
$$z^3 - 27 = (z - 3)(z^2 + 3z + 9)$$. So the solutions are $$3$$ and the solutions of
$$z^2 + 3z + 9 = 0$$ (:ref{#complex-zero-product}). That quadratic has $$4ac - b^2 = 36 - 9 = 27$$,
and $$\sqrt{27} = 3\sqrt3$$, so by :ref{#negative-discriminant}

```math
z = \frac{-3 \pm 3\sqrt3\, i}{2} = 3\left(-\frac{1}{2} \pm \frac{\sqrt3}{2}i\right)
```

The cube roots of unity are $$1$$ and $$-\frac{1}{2} \pm \frac{\sqrt3}{2}i$$
(:ref{#roots-of-unity-polygon}), so the three solutions are $$3$$ times them. This matches
:ref{#nth-roots-complex}: $$27$$ has length $$27$$ and angle $$0$$, and $$\sqrt[3]{27} = 3$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-every-quadratic-slip}

A friend notices that $$i$$ is a solution of $$z^2 + iz + 2 = 0$$, and concludes that $$-i$$ must be
one too, because solutions come in conjugate pairs. Test the claim, find the mistake, and find the
second solution.

:::solution

Putting in $$i$$ gives $$i^2 + i \cdot i + 2 = -1 - 1 + 2 = 0$$, so $$i$$ is a solution. But putting
in $$-i$$ gives $$(-i)^2 + i(-i) + 2 = -1 + 1 + 2 = 2$$, which isn't $$0$$. The mistake is that
:ref{#conjugate-pairs} needs every coefficient to be real, and the coefficient $$i$$ of $$z$$ isn't.

The sum of the solutions is $$-\frac{b}{a} = -i$$ (:ref{#every-quadratic-vieta}), so the second
solution is $$-i - i = -2i$$. Check: $$(-2i)^2 + i(-2i) + 2 = -4 + 2 + 2 = 0$$.

:::
