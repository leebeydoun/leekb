title: The quadratic formula
description:
  One formula that solves every quadratic equation, where it comes from, and how to use it.

If you've [completed the square](./completing-the-square.mdx) a few times, you'll notice the steps
never change. You divide by the number in front of $$x^2$$, add the missing corner, then take square
roots, and only the numbers are different. So you can do the steps once, with letters in place of
the numbers, and never do them again. The result is the quadratic formula, which goes straight from
the three numbers in an equation to its solutions.

In this guide we'll derive the formula and learn to use it safely. We'll also see when another
method needs less work.

## Doing the steps once with letters

Every quadratic $$ax^2 + bx + c = 0$$ is fixed by its three numbers $$a$$, $$b$$ and $$c$$.
Completing the square with these letters, instead of particular numbers, solves every quadratic at
once. The answer comes out as a formula in $$a$$, $$b$$ and $$c$$: put in the numbers from any
equation, and out come its solutions.

The formula also has a shape worth seeing. The solutions sit symmetrically about one point,
$$-\frac{b}{2a}$$: one is a certain distance to its right and the other the same distance to its
left. The formula gives that centre, plus or minus that distance. When the distance would be the
square root of a negative number, there are no solutions at all.

:::theorem[The quadratic formula]{#quadratic-formula}

Let $$a$$, $$b$$ and $$c$$ be real numbers with $$a \neq 0$$, and consider the quadratic equation
$$ax^2 + bx + c = 0$$ (:ref{#quadratic}).

1. If $$b^2 - 4ac \ge 0$$, its solutions are exactly the numbers given by

   ```math
   x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}
   ```

   that is, $$\frac{-b + \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$$ and $$\frac{-b - \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$$.

2. If $$b^2 - 4ac < 0$$, it has no real solution.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_formula#Derivations"}

Since $$a \neq 0$$, dividing both sides by $$a$$ is a safe step
(:ref{#linear-equations/safe-steps}), and subtracting $$\frac{c}{a}$$ from both sides is another. So
the equation is equivalent to

```math
x^2 + \frac{b}{a}x = -\frac{c}{a}
```

Now complete the square (:ref{#complete-square}). Half of $$\frac{b}{a}$$ is $$\frac{b}{2a}$$, so
adding its square $$\frac{b^2}{4a^2}$$ to both sides turns the left side into a square:

```math
\left(x + \frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 = \frac{b^2}{4a^2} - \frac{c}{a} = \frac{b^2 - 4ac}{4a^2}
```

where the right side was put over the common denominator $$4a^2$$.

The denominator $$4a^2$$ is positive, since $$a \neq 0$$ (:ref{#real-numbers/squares-positive}). So
the right side has the same sign as $$b^2 - 4ac$$.

**Part 2.** If $$b^2 - 4ac < 0$$, the right side is negative. The left side is a square, which is
never negative (:ref{#real-numbers/squares-positive}), so no $$x$$ makes the two sides equal.

**Part 1.** If $$b^2 - 4ac \ge 0$$, the right side is not negative, and solving a shifted square
(:ref{#shifted-square}) gives

```math
x + \frac{b}{2a} = \pm\sqrt{\frac{b^2 - 4ac}{4a^2}} = \pm\frac{\sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{\sqrt{4a^2}}
```

by :ref{#real-numbers/root-product}. Here $$\sqrt{4a^2}$$ is $$2a$$ if $$a > 0$$ and $$-2a$$ if
$$a < 0$$. Either way the $$\pm$$ already covers both signs, so the two values are
$$\pm\frac{\sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$$. Subtracting $$\frac{b}{2a}$$ from both sides,

```math
x = -\frac{b}{2a} \pm \frac{\sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a} = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}
```

Every step was safe, so these are exactly the solutions of the original equation.

:::

The number $$b^2 - 4ac$$ under the root decides everything about how many solutions there are, and
the [next guide](./discriminant.mdx) gives it a name and a closer look.

## Using the formula

The formula needs the equation in the standard form $$ax^2 + bx + c = 0$$, with everything on one
side and $$0$$ on the other. Then read off $$a$$, $$b$$ and $$c$$, **signs included**, and work out
$$b^2 - 4ac$$ first. Its sign says at once whether there are any solutions, and its square root is
the only awkward part of the calculation.

:::example[Answers without a square root]

Solve $$3x^2 + 5x - 2 = 0$$.

Here $$a = 3$$, $$b = 5$$ and $$c = -2$$. First,

```math
b^2 - 4ac = 5^2 - 4 \cdot 3 \cdot (-2) = 25 + 24 = 49
```

which is positive, with $$\sqrt{49} = 7$$. So

```math
x = \frac{-5 \pm 7}{2 \cdot 3} = \frac{-5 \pm 7}{6}
```

giving $$x = \frac{2}{6} = \frac{1}{3}$$ and $$x = \frac{-12}{6} = -2$$.

**Check:** $$3 \cdot \frac{1}{9} + 5 \cdot \frac{1}{3} - 2 = \frac{1}{3} + \frac{5}{3} - 2 = 0$$,
and $$3 \cdot 4 - 10 - 2 = 0$$.

:::

:::example[Answers with a square root]

Solve $$x^2 - 6x + 4 = 0$$.

Here $$a = 1$$, $$b = -6$$ and $$c = 4$$, so $$b^2 - 4ac = 36 - 16 = 20$$. Simplifying the root,
$$\sqrt{20} = \sqrt{4}\sqrt{5} = 2\sqrt{5}$$ (:ref{#real-numbers/root-product}), and

```math
x = \frac{6 \pm 2\sqrt{5}}{2} = 3 \pm \sqrt{5}
```

Both terms on top were divided by $$2$$. The solutions are $$3 + \sqrt{5}$$ and $$3 - \sqrt{5}$$,
symmetric about $$-\frac{b}{2a} = 3$$.

**Check:** $$(3 + \sqrt{5})^2 = 9 + 6\sqrt{5} + 5 = 14 + 6\sqrt{5}$$, so
$$(3 + \sqrt{5})^2 - 6(3 + \sqrt{5}) + 4 = 14 + 6\sqrt{5} - 18 - 6\sqrt{5} + 4 = 0$$. The other
solution works the same way with the signs of $$\sqrt{5}$$ swapped.

:::

:::example[First rearrange]

Solve $$3x^2 = 4x + 1$$.

The formula needs $$0$$ on one side, so subtract $$4x + 1$$ from both sides: $$3x^2 - 4x - 1 = 0$$.
Now $$a = 3$$, $$b = -4$$, $$c = -1$$, and

```math
b^2 - 4ac = 16 + 12 = 28, \qquad \sqrt{28} = 2\sqrt{7}
```

So

```math
x = \frac{4 \pm 2\sqrt{7}}{6} = \frac{2 \pm \sqrt{7}}{3}
```

:::

:::example[No real solution]

Solve $$5x^2 + 2x + 1 = 0$$.

Here $$b^2 - 4ac = 4 - 20 = -16$$, which is negative. By part 2 of :ref{#quadratic-formula}, there
is no real solution, and there is nothing more to calculate.

:::

## Checking an answer

An answer with a square root in it takes a lot of work to check by substituting. A shorter check
uses the sum and product of the solutions. For the two solutions of $$ax^2 + bx + c = 0$$, the sum
is always $$-\frac{b}{a}$$ and the product is always $$\frac{c}{a}$$. The next guide proves this
(:ref{#vieta}).

For $$3x^2 = 4x + 1$$ above, the solutions were $$\frac{2 + \sqrt{7}}{3}$$ and
$$\frac{2 - \sqrt{7}}{3}$$. Their sum is $$\frac{4}{3}$$, which is $$-\frac{b}{a} = \frac{4}{3}$$.
Their product is $$\frac{4 - 7}{9} = -\frac{1}{3}$$, using the difference of two squares
(:ref{#numbers/square-formulas}), and that is $$\frac{c}{a} = -\frac{1}{3}$$. Both agree, so the
answer is almost certainly right.

## When to use which method

The formula always works, which makes it the safe choice. For some equations, though, another method
needs less work:

- **Factoring** (:ref{#solve-by-factors}) needs less work when the solutions are whole numbers or
  neat fractions you can spot. For $$x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0$$, the numbers $$2$$ and $$3$$ have sum $$5$$
  and product $$6$$, so it is $$(x - 2)(x - 3) = 0$$, with no square roots at all.
- **With no $$x$$ term**, as in $$x^2 - 7 = 0$$, solve $$x^2 = 7$$ directly: $$x = \pm\sqrt{7}$$.
- **With no constant term**, as in $$4x^2 + 3x = 0$$, take out the common factor: $$x(4x + 3) = 0$$,
  so $$x = 0$$ or $$x = -\frac{3}{4}$$.
- **Otherwise**, or when factors don't show themselves after a short look, use the formula.

The formula also covers equations whose numbers aren't whole. For $$x^2 + \sqrt{2}\,x - 4 = 0$$,
$$b^2 - 4ac = 2 + 16 = 18$$ and $$\sqrt{18} = 3\sqrt{2}$$, so
$$x = \frac{-\sqrt{2} \pm 3\sqrt{2}}{2}$$, which is $$\sqrt{2}$$ or $$-2\sqrt{2}$$.

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on the quadratic formula, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-formula-three}

Solve with the quadratic formula, or show there is no real solution.

1. $$2x^2 - 3x - 2 = 0$$
2. $$x^2 + 4x + 1 = 0$$
3. $$3x^2 + 2x + 1 = 0$$

:::solution

Work out $$b^2 - 4ac$$ first, then use :ref{#quadratic-formula}.

1. $$a = 2$$, $$b = -3$$, $$c = -2$$, so $$b^2 - 4ac = 9 + 16 = 25$$ and $$\sqrt{25} = 5$$. So
   $$x = \frac{3 \pm 5}{4}$$, which is $$2$$ or $$-\frac{1}{2}$$.
2. $$b^2 - 4ac = 16 - 4 = 12$$, and $$\sqrt{12} = 2\sqrt{3}$$. So
   $$x = \frac{-4 \pm 2\sqrt{3}}{2} = -2 \pm \sqrt{3}$$.
3. $$b^2 - 4ac = 4 - 12 = -8$$, which is negative. By part 2 of :ref{#quadratic-formula}, there is
   no real solution.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-formula-signs}

Solve $$7 - 4x - 2x^2 = 0$$. Take care reading off $$a$$, $$b$$ and $$c$$.

:::solution

The terms are written in the opposite order to standard form. Rearranged, the equation is
$$-2x^2 - 4x + 7 = 0$$, so $$a = -2$$, $$b = -4$$ and $$c = 7$$. Then

```math
b^2 - 4ac = 16 - 4 \cdot (-2) \cdot 7 = 16 + 56 = 72, \qquad \sqrt{72} = 6\sqrt{2}
```

By :ref{#quadratic-formula},

```math
x = \frac{4 \pm 6\sqrt{2}}{-4} = -1 \mp \frac{3\sqrt{2}}{2}
```

So the solutions are $$-1 + \frac{3\sqrt{2}}{2}$$ and $$-1 - \frac{3\sqrt{2}}{2}$$, about $$1.121$$
and $$-3.121$$. Reading $$a = 7$$ from the first term, as if the equation were already in standard
form, would give wrong answers.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-formula-rearrange}

Solve $$x(x + 2) = 1$$.

:::solution

The formula needs the standard form, so expand and move the $$1$$ across: $$x^2 + 2x - 1 = 0$$. Then
$$b^2 - 4ac = 4 + 4 = 8$$ and $$\sqrt{8} = 2\sqrt{2}$$, so

```math
x = \frac{-2 \pm 2\sqrt{2}}{2} = -1 \pm \sqrt{2}
```

by :ref{#quadratic-formula}. A tempting mistake is "$$x = 1$$ or $$x + 2 = 1$$", but a product equal
to $$1$$ says nothing about either factor on its own.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-formula-centre}

Without solving, find the number the solutions of $$4x^2 - 12x + 1 = 0$$ are centred on. Then solve
the equation and check that the solutions sit evenly on either side of it.

:::solution

The formula gives $$-\frac{b}{2a}$$ plus or minus a distance, so the centre is
$$s = -\frac{-12}{2 \cdot 4} = \frac{3}{2}$$.

Now $$b^2 - 4ac = 144 - 16 = 128$$, and $$\sqrt{128} = \sqrt{64}\sqrt{2} = 8\sqrt{2}$$
(:ref{#real-numbers/root-product}). So

```math
x = \frac{12 \pm 8\sqrt{2}}{8} = \frac{3}{2} \pm \sqrt{2}
```

The solutions are $$\frac{3}{2} + \sqrt{2}$$ and $$\frac{3}{2} - \sqrt{2}$$, each $$\sqrt{2}$$ away
from $$\frac{3}{2}$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-formula-negative-a}

Solve $$-x^2 + x + 2 = 0$$ with the formula, and check both answers. In the proof of
:ref{#quadratic-formula}, $$\sqrt{4a^2}$$ is $$-2a$$ when $$a$$ is negative. Why do the answers
still come out right?

:::solution

Here $$a = -1$$, $$b = 1$$, $$c = 2$$, so $$b^2 - 4ac = 1 + 8 = 9$$, and

```math
x = \frac{-1 \pm 3}{-2}
```

which is $$\frac{2}{-2} = -1$$ or $$\frac{-4}{-2} = 2$$. **Check:** $$-1 - 1 + 2 = 0$$ and
$$-4 + 2 + 2 = 0$$.

When $$a < 0$$, dividing by $$2a$$ instead of $$-2a$$ swaps which value comes from $$+$$ and which
from $$-$$. Here the $$+$$ gave $$-1$$ and the $$-$$ gave $$2$$. The formula always lists both
values, so swapping them changes nothing: the pair of solutions is the same.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-formula-root-coefficient}

Solve $$x^2 - 2\sqrt{3}\,x + 2 = 0$$.

:::solution

Here $$a = 1$$, $$b = -2\sqrt{3}$$ and $$c = 2$$. Since $$(2\sqrt{3})^2 = 4 \cdot 3 = 12$$,

```math
b^2 - 4ac = 12 - 8 = 4
```

so by :ref{#quadratic-formula},

```math
x = \frac{2\sqrt{3} \pm 2}{2} = \sqrt{3} \pm 1
```

The solutions are $$\sqrt{3} + 1$$ and $$\sqrt{3} - 1$$. **Check** the first:
$$(\sqrt{3} + 1)^2 = 4 + 2\sqrt{3}$$, and taking $$2\sqrt{3}(\sqrt{3} + 1) = 6 + 2\sqrt{3}$$ from it
and adding $$2$$ leaves $$0$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-formula-word}

A number times the number $$3$$ larger than it comes to $$7$$. What can the number be?

:::solution

Call the number $$x$$. Then $$x(x + 3) = 7$$, so $$x^2 + 3x - 7 = 0$$. Here
$$b^2 - 4ac = 9 + 28 = 37$$, which is positive but not a perfect square, so factoring with whole
numbers won't work. By :ref{#quadratic-formula},

```math
x = \frac{-3 \pm \sqrt{37}}{2}
```

about $$1.541$$ or $$-4.541$$. Both are allowed, since nothing in the question rules out a negative
number. Check the first: $$1.541 \times 4.541 \approx 7.00$$.

:::
