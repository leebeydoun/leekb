Put a square coaster on a table and hold one corner down with a finger. Spin the coaster half a turn
about that corner. Now move your finger to the corner diagonally across from it, and spin half a
turn again. The coaster ends up with its picture the right way up, facing exactly the way it
started, but it has moved along the table by twice the distance between the two corners you held.

Two half turns have made a slide. Doing one move after another is called combining, or composing,
the moves (:ref{#isometries/composite}). In this guide we'll combine and undo the moves from
[Moves as formulas](./moves-as-formulas.mdx) by calculating with points.

## Slides combine by adding

Slide a coaster $$3$$ cm to the right, then $$2$$ cm up. Every point of it has moved $$3$$ right and
$$2$$ up, and doing the two slides in the other order would have the same effect. With slides
written as formulas, this is adding steps. The slide by $$A$$ is $$T_A(P) = P + A$$
(:ref{#slide-formula}), so sliding by $$A$$ and then by $$B$$ adds $$A$$ and then $$B$$.

For example, take $$A = (3, 0)$$, $$B = (0, 2)$$ and $$P = (1, 1)$$. The first slide sends $$P$$ to
$$(4, 1)$$, and the second sends that to $$(4, 3)$$. That is $$P + (3, 2)$$, and $$(3, 2) = A + B$$.

:::proposition[Slides combine by adding]{#slide-compose}

For any points $$A$$ and $$B$$, first sliding by $$A$$ and then by $$B$$ is sliding by $$A + B$$:

```math
T_B \circ T_A = T_{A + B}
```

In particular $$T_B \circ T_A = T_A \circ T_B$$, so the order of two slides doesn't matter.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translation_(geometry)#Matrix_representation"}

Take any point $$P$$. By the definition of a composite (:ref{#isometries/composite}) and of a slide
(:ref{#slide-formula}),

```math
(T_B \circ T_A)(P) = T_B(P + A) = (P + A) + B = P + (A + B) = T_{A + B}(P)
```

The regrouping in the middle is rule 2 of :ref{#sum-rules}. The two maps agree at every point, so
they are equal (:ref{#isometries/map-equality}).

For the order, the same calculation gives $$T_A \circ T_B = T_{B + A}$$. Since $$B + A = A + B$$, by
rule 1 of :ref{#sum-rules}, this is $$T_{A + B}$$ again.

:::

The Isometries chapter took as given that two slides make a slide: rule 2 of
:ref{#isometries/translation-rules}. For slides written in coordinates, that rule is now proved. The
proof also says which slide you get, the one by the sum of the two steps.

### Undoing a slide

A slide by $$A$$ is undone by taking the same step backwards, which is the slide by $$-A$$. In
numbers, the slide by $$(3, -5)$$ is undone by the slide by $$(-3, 5)$$, since the two steps add up
to $$(0, 0)$$.

:::proposition[Undoing a slide]{#slide-inverse}

For any point $$A$$, the slide $$T_A$$ has an inverse (:ref{#isometries/inverse-map}), the slide by
$$-A$$:

```math
T_A^{-1} = T_{-A}
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translation_(geometry)#Matrix_representation"}

By :ref{#slide-compose}, and rules 1 and 4 of :ref{#sum-rules},

```math
T_{-A} \circ T_A = T_{A + (-A)} = T_O \qquad T_A \circ T_{-A} = T_{-A + A} = T_O
```

The slide by $$O$$ moves nothing, since $$T_O(P) = P + O = P$$ by rule 3 of :ref{#sum-rules}. So
$$T_O$$ is the identity $$I$$ (:ref{#isometries/map}), and both composites are $$I$$. That is what
:ref{#isometries/inverse-map} asks of an inverse.

:::

## Zooms about the origin combine by multiplying

Enlarge a photo to twice its size, then enlarge the copy to three times its size. The result is six
times the size of the original, because each length was doubled and then tripled. Zooms about the
origin behave the same way, with the factors multiplied.

Write $$Z_r$$ for the zoom by $$r > 0$$ about the origin, $$Z_r(P) = rP$$. This is the zoom of
:ref{#zoom-formula} with centre $$O$$, since $$O + r(P - O) = rP$$. For example, $$Z_2$$ sends
$$(1, -2)$$ to $$(2, -4)$$, and $$Z_3$$ sends that to $$(6, -12)$$, which is $$6 \cdot (1, -2)$$.

:::proposition[Zooms about the origin]{#zoom-compose}

For any numbers $$r > 0$$ and $$s > 0$$,

```math
Z_s \circ Z_r = Z_{rs} \qquad \text{and} \qquad Z_r^{-1} = Z_{1/r}
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homothety#Composition_of_two_homotheties_with_the_same_center"}

Take any point $$P$$. By :ref{#scale-twice} and rule 1 of :ref{#real-numbers/real-rules},

```math
(Z_s \circ Z_r)(P) = s(rP) = (sr)P = (rs)P = Z_{rs}(P)
```

so $$Z_s \circ Z_r = Z_{rs}$$ (:ref{#isometries/map-equality}).

For the inverse, the number $$\frac{1}{r}$$ is positive, and $$r \cdot \frac{1}{r} = 1$$. So
$$Z_{1/r} \circ Z_r$$ and $$Z_r \circ Z_{1/r}$$ are both $$Z_1$$. The zoom by $$1$$ moves nothing,
since $$1P = P$$ (:ref{#scalar-multiple}), so both composites are the identity $$I$$.

:::

## Two half turns make a slide

Go back to the coaster. Each half turn leaves its picture upside down, so the second half turn puts
it the right way up again. The coaster has moved, but it hasn't turned, and that is how a slide
looks. The formula for a half turn about $$Q$$, $$H_Q(P) = 2Q - P$$ (:ref{#half-turn-formula}),
tells us exactly which slide it is.

Try it with numbers. Take the centres $$Q = (1, 1)$$ and $$M = (3, 2)$$, and the point
$$P = (0, 4)$$. The half turn about $$Q$$ sends $$P$$ to $$P' = (2, 2) - (0, 4) = (2, -2)$$. The
half turn about $$M$$ sends that to $$P'' = (6, 4) - (2, -2) = (4, 6)$$. So $$P$$ has moved by
$$(4, 2)$$, which is twice the step $$M - Q = (2, 1)$$ from $$Q$$ to $$M$$.


:::theorem[Two half turns make a slide]{#two-half-turns}

For any points $$Q$$ and $$M$$, the half turn about $$Q$$ followed by the half turn about $$M$$ is
the slide by twice the step from $$Q$$ to $$M$$:

```math
H_M \circ H_Q = T_{2(M - Q)}
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_reflection#Point_reflection_group"}

Take any point $$P$$. By :ref{#half-turn-formula},

```math
(H_M \circ H_Q)(P) = H_M(2Q - P) = 2M - (2Q - P)
```

Sums and multiples of points can be rearranged as with numbers (:ref{#sum-rules} and
:ref{#scale-sum-rules}), so

```math
2M - (2Q - P) = P + 2M - 2Q = P + 2(M - Q) = T_{2(M - Q)}(P)
```

The two maps agree at every point, so they are equal (:ref{#isometries/map-equality}).

:::

Swapping the order swaps the roles of $$Q$$ and $$M$$, so $$H_Q \circ H_M = T_{2(Q - M)}$$, the
slide by the same distance in the opposite direction. When the two centres are the same point, the
step from $$Q$$ to $$M$$ is $$O$$, and

```math
H_Q \circ H_Q = T_O = I
```

So a half turn undoes itself: $$H_Q^{-1} = H_Q$$. This fits the picture of a reflection through
$$Q$$ (:ref{#isometries/point-reflection}): reflecting through the same point twice puts every point
back.

The Isometries chapter found a slide from two parallel mirrors (:ref{#isometries/parallel-mirrors}),
and that result agrees with this one. Suppose $$Q \ne M$$, and let $$N$$ be the line through them. A
half turn about $$Q$$ is a flip in the mirror square to $$N$$ at $$Q$$ followed by a flip in $$N$$
(:ref{#isometries/corner-mirrors}). In the same way, a half turn about $$M$$ is a flip in $$N$$
followed by a flip in the mirror square to $$N$$ at $$M$$. The two flips in $$N$$ meet in the middle
and cancel (:ref{#isometries/reflection-twice}), which leaves two parallel mirrors a distance
$$d(Q, M)$$ apart. They make a slide by $$2\,d(Q, M)$$ from $$Q$$ towards $$M$$, which is the slide
by $$2(M - Q)$$.

### A half turn from moves about the origin

The half turn about the origin is the one with the shortest formula, $$H_O(P) = -P$$
(:ref{#negative-half-turn}). Every other half turn is that one followed by a slide. To turn $$P$$
half a turn about $$Q$$, first send it to $$-P$$, then slide by $$2Q$$.

:::proposition[A half turn from moves about the origin]{#half-turn-composite}

For any point $$Q$$, the half turn about $$Q$$ is the half turn about $$O$$ followed by the slide by
$$2Q$$:

```math
H_Q = T_{2Q} \circ H_O
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homothety#Composition_of_a_homothety_and_a_translation"}

For any point $$P$$, $$(T_{2Q} \circ H_O)(P) = T_{2Q}(-P) = -P + 2Q = 2Q - P$$, using rule 1 of
:ref{#sum-rules}. That is $$H_Q(P)$$ by :ref{#half-turn-formula}.

:::

The same fact also follows from :ref{#two-half-turns}. Taking $$M = Q$$ and the first centre to be
$$O$$ there gives $$H_Q \circ H_O = T_{2Q}$$. Doing $$H_O$$ first on both sides, and using
$$H_O \circ H_O = I$$ and :ref{#isometries/composite-associative}, turns this into
$$H_Q = T_{2Q} \circ H_O$$.

## Doing a move about another point

A zoom about the origin is one multiplication, but a zoom about another point $$Q$$ has a longer
formula. There is a way to build it from moves we already have: slide everything so that $$Q$$ lands
on the origin, zoom about the origin, and then slide everything back. The centre $$Q$$ goes to
$$O$$, stays there during the zoom, and comes back to $$Q$$, so it doesn't move at all.

Try it with the zoom by $$2$$ about $$Q = (1, 3)$$ and the point $$P = (2, 5)$$. Sliding by $$-Q$$
sends $$P$$ to $$(1, 2)$$. Zooming by $$2$$ sends that to $$(2, 4)$$, and sliding back by $$Q$$
gives $$(3, 7)$$. The formula of :ref{#zoom-formula} gives the same point:
$$Q + 2(P - Q) = (1, 3) + 2(1, 2) = (3, 7)$$.

:::proposition[A zoom about any point]{#zoom-composite}

Let $$Q$$ be a point and $$r > 0$$. Then

```math
Z_{r,Q} = T_Q \circ Z_r \circ T_{-Q} \qquad \text{and} \qquad H_Q = T_Q \circ H_O \circ T_{-Q}
```

The zoom about $$Q$$ is undone by the zoom about $$Q$$ by $$\frac{1}{r}$$:
$$Z_{r,Q}^{-1} = Z_{1/r,Q}$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homothety#Composition_of_a_homothety_and_a_translation"}

**The zoom.** Follow any point $$P$$ through the three moves, starting with the one on the right:

```math
P \;\mapsto\; P - Q \;\mapsto\; r(P - Q) \;\mapsto\; Q + r(P - Q)
```

The last point is $$Z_{r,Q}(P)$$ (:ref{#zoom-formula}), so the two maps are equal
(:ref{#isometries/map-equality}).

**The half turn.** In the same way, $$P$$ goes to $$P - Q$$, then to $$-(P - Q) = Q - P$$, then to
$$Q + (Q - P) = 2Q - P$$, which is $$H_Q(P)$$ (:ref{#half-turn-formula}).

**The inverse.** Undoing three moves means undoing them in reverse order, by
:ref{#isometries/inverse-composite} used twice. Each move here has an inverse: $$T_Q^{-1} = T_{-Q}$$
and $$T_{-Q}^{-1} = T_Q$$ by :ref{#slide-inverse}, and $$Z_r^{-1} = Z_{1/r}$$ by
:ref{#zoom-compose}. So

```math
Z_{r,Q}^{-1} = T_{-Q}^{-1} \circ Z_r^{-1} \circ T_Q^{-1} = T_Q \circ Z_{1/r} \circ T_{-Q} = Z_{1/r,Q}
```

where the last step is the first part of this proposition with $$\frac{1}{r}$$ in place of $$r$$.

:::

So a move about $$Q$$ is the same move about the origin, with $$Q$$ carried to the origin first and
back afterwards. The same trick works for a turn about $$Q$$, so a formula for a turn about the
origin is enough to describe the same turn about any point.

## A slide seen through another move

Picture a walk of $$1$$ km east on a map printed at twice its size. On the enlarged map the walk
shows up as a step twice as long, still pointing east. Now turn the map half a turn on the table:
the walk still shows up as a step of the same length, but it points west. A slide seen through a
zoom is a longer slide, and seen through a half turn it is a slide the other way.

In symbols, seeing $$T_A$$ through a move $$F$$ means undoing $$F$$, sliding by $$A$$, and then
doing $$F$$ again, which is the composite $$F \circ T_A \circ F^{-1}$$.

:::proposition[A slide seen through another move]{#conjugate-slide}

For any point $$A$$ and any $$r > 0$$,

```math
Z_r \circ T_A \circ Z_r^{-1} = T_{rA} \qquad \text{and} \qquad H_O \circ T_A \circ H_O = T_{-A}
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_group#Subgroup_structure,_matrix_and_vector_representation"}

**The zoom.** By :ref{#zoom-compose}, $$Z_r^{-1} = Z_{1/r}$$. For any point $$P$$, using
:ref{#scale-sum-rules} and :ref{#scale-twice},

```math
(Z_r \circ T_A \circ Z_{1/r})(P) = r\Big(\frac{1}{r}P + A\Big) = \Big(r \cdot \frac{1}{r}\Big)P + rA = P + rA
```

which is $$T_{rA}(P)$$.

**The half turn.** The half turn about $$O$$ undoes itself (:ref{#two-half-turns}), so here
$$H_O = H_O^{-1}$$. For any point $$P$$, $$P$$ goes to $$-P$$, then to $$-P + A$$, then to
$$-(-P + A) = P - A$$, which is $$T_{-A}(P)$$.

:::

For example, with $$r = 2$$ and $$A = (1, 0)$$, the composite $$Z_2 \circ T_A \circ Z_{1/2}$$ sends
$$(5, 7)$$ to $$(\frac{5}{2}, \frac{7}{2})$$, then to $$(\frac{7}{2}, \frac{7}{2})$$, then to
$$(7, 7)$$. That is a slide by $$(2, 0) = 2A$$.

The half turn part also explains why the two formulas for $$H_Q$$ agree. With $$-Q$$ in place of
$$A$$, it says $$H_O \circ T_{-Q} \circ H_O = T_Q$$. Doing $$H_O$$ first on both sides, and using
$$H_O \circ H_O = I$$, gives $$H_O \circ T_{-Q} = T_Q \circ H_O$$, so

```math
T_Q \circ H_O \circ T_{-Q} = T_Q \circ T_Q \circ H_O = T_{2Q} \circ H_O
```

by :ref{#slide-compose}. The left side is $$H_Q$$ by :ref{#zoom-composite}, and the right side is
$$H_Q$$ by :ref{#half-turn-composite}.

## Checking the mirror results

The Isometries chapter found what two mirrors make by looking at pictures. Two mirrors that meet
square on make a half turn, and two mirrors at $$45^\circ$$ make a quarter turn. With the flips and
turns written as formulas, each of these is a line of algebra, and we can check them.

The formulas are the reflections in the axes, $$R_1(x, y) = (x, -y)$$ and $$R_2(x, y) = (-x, y)$$
(:ref{#axis-reflections}), the reflection in the line $$y = x$$, $$S(x, y) = (y, x)$$
(:ref{#diagonal-reflection}), and the quarter turn $$G_{90}(x, y) = (-y, x)$$ (:ref{#quarter-turn}).

:::proposition[Flips and quarter turns combined]{#combining-moves-flips}

1. $$R_2 \circ R_1 = H_O$$
2. $$G_{90} \circ G_{90} = H_O$$, and $$G_{90}^4 = I$$
3. $$S \circ R_1 = G_{90}$$, but $$R_1 \circ S = G_{90}^{-1}$$, where
   $$G_{90}^{-1}(x, y) = (y, -x)$$

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_plane_isometry#Mirror_combinations"}

Follow a point $$(x, y)$$ through each composite, starting with the move on the right.

1. $$R_1$$ sends it to $$(x, -y)$$, and $$R_2$$ sends that to $$(-x, -y)$$, which is $$H_O(x, y)$$.
2. $$G_{90}$$ sends it to $$(-y, x)$$, and $$G_{90}$$ again sends that to $$(-x, -y)$$. So
   $$G_{90}^2 = H_O$$, and $$G_{90}^4 = H_O \circ H_O = I$$ by :ref{#two-half-turns}.
3. $$R_1$$ sends it to $$(x, -y)$$, and $$S$$ swaps the coordinates to give $$(-y, x)$$, which is
   $$G_{90}(x, y)$$. In the other order, $$S$$ gives $$(y, x)$$ and $$R_1$$ gives $$(y, -x)$$. The
   map $$(x, y) \mapsto (y, -x)$$ undoes $$G_{90}$$: it sends $$(-y, x)$$ back to $$(x, y)$$, and
   $$G_{90}$$ sends $$(y, -x)$$ to $$(x, y)$$. So it is $$G_{90}^{-1}$$
   (:ref{#isometries/inverse-map}).

:::

Each part matches the Isometries chapter:

- **Part 1**: the $$x$$-axis and the $$y$$-axis meet square on at $$O$$, so flipping in one and then
  the other is the half turn about the corner (:ref{#isometries/corner-mirrors})
- **Part 2**: two quarter turns make a half turn, and four make a whole turn, which moves nothing
  (:ref{#isometries/map-powers})
- **Part 3**: turning anticlockwise from the $$x$$-axis to the line $$y = x$$ takes $$45^\circ$$, so
  :ref{#isometries/crossing-mirrors} says $$S \circ R_1$$ is a turn by $$90^\circ$$ anticlockwise.
  In the other order, turning anticlockwise from $$y = x$$ to the $$x$$-axis takes $$135^\circ$$, so
  $$R_1 \circ S$$ turns by $$270^\circ$$ anticlockwise, which is a quarter turn clockwise.

Part 3 also shows that the order of two flips matters, as in :ref{#isometries/order-matters}. And it
fills a gap in :ref{#quarter-turn}, which checked the direction of $$G_{90}$$ at $$E_1$$ alone. By
:ref{#isometries/crossing-mirrors}, $$S \circ R_1$$ turns every point a quarter turn anticlockwise,
and part 3 says that this map is $$G_{90}$$. That theorem's proof measures angles in the picture, so
this step leans on the picture too.

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on combining and undoing moves, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-combining-moves-slides}

Let $$A = (3, 2)$$ and $$B = (-1, 4)$$.

1. Which single slide is $$T_B \circ T_A$$? Where does it send the origin?
2. Which slide undoes $$T_B \circ T_A$$?

:::solution

1. By :ref{#slide-compose}, $$T_B \circ T_A = T_{A + B}$$, and $$A + B = (3 - 1, 2 + 4) = (2, 6)$$.
   It sends $$O$$ to $$O + (2, 6) = (2, 6)$$.
2. By :ref{#slide-inverse}, the slide by $$(2, 6)$$ is undone by the slide by $$(-2, -6)$$. Undoing
   the two slides one at a time in reverse order gives the same answer:
   $$T_{-A} \circ T_{-B} = T_{-B - A} = T_{(-2, -6)}$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-combining-moves-half-turns}

Let $$Q = (1, 2)$$ and $$M = (4, -1)$$.

1. Follow the origin $$O$$ through $$H_Q$$ and then $$H_M$$.
2. Check your answer against :ref{#two-half-turns}.
3. Which slide is $$H_Q \circ H_M$$?

:::solution

1. $$H_Q(O) = 2Q - O = (2, 4)$$, and $$H_M(2, 4) = 2M - (2, 4) = (8, -2) - (2, 4) = (6, -6)$$.
2. The step from $$Q$$ to $$M$$ is $$M - Q = (3, -3)$$, and twice that is $$(6, -6)$$. The slide by
   $$(6, -6)$$ sends $$O$$ to $$(6, -6)$$, as in part 1.
3. With the roles swapped, $$H_Q \circ H_M = T_{2(Q - M)} = T_{(-6, 6)}$$, the slide the other way.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-combining-moves-three-half-turns}

Let $$A$$, $$B$$ and $$C$$ be points.

1. Show that $$H_C \circ H_B \circ H_A = H_D$$, where $$D = A - B + C$$. So three half turns make a
   half turn.
2. Take $$A = (0, 0)$$, $$B = (1, 0)$$ and $$C = (1, 1)$$. Find $$D$$, and check part 1 at the point
   $$(2, 3)$$.

:::solution

1. For any point $$P$$, by :ref{#half-turn-formula},

   ```math
   (H_C \circ H_B \circ H_A)(P) = 2C - \big(2B - (2A - P)\big) = 2A - 2B + 2C - P = 2(A - B + C) - P
   ```

   rearranging with :ref{#sum-rules} and :ref{#scale-sum-rules}. That is $$H_D(P)$$. When $$A$$,
   $$B$$ and $$C$$ are not on one line, $$D$$ is the fourth corner of the parallelogram $$ABCD$$,
   opposite $$B$$.

2. $$D = (0, 0) - (1, 0) + (1, 1) = (0, 1)$$. Following $$(2, 3)$$: $$H_A$$ gives $$(-2, -3)$$,
   $$H_B$$ gives $$(2, 0) - (-2, -3) = (4, 3)$$, and $$H_C$$ gives $$(2, 2) - (4, 3) = (-2, -1)$$.
   Directly, $$H_D(2, 3) = (0, 2) - (2, 3) = (-2, -1)$$. They agree.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-combining-moves-zoom-about}

Let $$Q = (1, 3)$$.

1. Use :ref{#zoom-composite} to write $$Z_{2,Q}(x, y)$$ as a formula, and check that it leaves $$Q$$
   where it is.
2. Write $$Z_{1/2,Q}(x, y)$$ as a formula, and check that it sends $$(3, 7)$$ back to $$(2, 5)$$.

:::solution

1. Slide by $$-Q$$, zoom by $$2$$, slide by $$Q$$: $$(x, y)$$ goes to $$(x - 1, y - 3)$$, then to
   $$(2x - 2, 2y - 6)$$, then to $$(2x - 1, 2y - 3)$$. So $$Z_{2,Q}(x, y) = (2x - 1, 2y - 3)$$. At
   $$Q$$ this gives $$(2 - 1, 6 - 3) = (1, 3) = Q$$.
2. In the same way, $$(x, y)$$ goes to $$(x - 1, y - 3)$$, then to
   $$(\frac{1}{2}(x - 1), \frac{1}{2}(y - 3))$$, then to
   $$(\frac{1}{2}(x + 1), \frac{1}{2}(y + 3))$$. At $$(3, 7)$$ this gives
   $$(\frac{4}{2}, \frac{10}{2}) = (2, 5)$$. That fits the worked example under
   [Doing a move about another point](#doing-a-move-about-another-point), where $$Z_{2,Q}$$ sent
   $$(2, 5)$$ to $$(3, 7)$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-combining-moves-flips}

Work out $$R_2 \circ S$$ and $$S \circ R_2$$ as formulas, and name each one. Then check your answers
against :ref{#isometries/crossing-mirrors}.

:::solution

$$S$$ sends $$(x, y)$$ to $$(y, x)$$, and $$R_2$$ sends that to $$(-y, x)$$. So
$$R_2 \circ S = G_{90}$$, the quarter turn anticlockwise.

In the other order, $$R_2$$ gives $$(-x, y)$$ and $$S$$ gives $$(y, -x)$$. So
$$S \circ R_2 = G_{90}^{-1}$$, the quarter turn clockwise (:ref{#combining-moves-flips}).

For $$R_2 \circ S$$, the first mirror is $$y = x$$ and the second is the $$y$$-axis. Turning
anticlockwise from the first to the second takes $$45^\circ$$, so the composite turns by
$$90^\circ$$ anticlockwise. For $$S \circ R_2$$, turning anticlockwise from the $$y$$-axis to
$$y = x$$ takes $$135^\circ$$, so the composite turns by $$270^\circ$$ anticlockwise, which is a
quarter turn clockwise.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-combining-moves-undo-order}

Let $$A = (2, 0)$$ and $$F = G_{90} \circ T_A$$: first slide by $$A$$, then make a quarter turn
about $$O$$.

1. Write $$F(x, y)$$ as a formula.
2. By :ref{#isometries/inverse-composite}, $$F^{-1} = T_{-A} \circ G_{90}^{-1}$$. Write it as a
   formula, and check that it undoes $$F$$ at the point $$(1, 1)$$.
3. Show that undoing in the wrong order, $$G_{90}^{-1} \circ T_{-A}$$, doesn't send $$F(O)$$ back to
   $$O$$.

:::solution

1. $$T_A$$ gives $$(x + 2, y)$$, and $$G_{90}$$ gives $$(-y, x + 2)$$. So $$F(x, y) = (-y, x + 2)$$.
2. $$G_{90}^{-1}$$ gives $$(y, -x)$$, and $$T_{-A}$$ gives $$(y - 2, -x)$$. So
   $$F^{-1}(x, y) = (y - 2, -x)$$. Now $$F(1, 1) = (-1, 3)$$, and
   $$F^{-1}(-1, 3) = (3 - 2, 1) = (1, 1)$$.
3. $$F(O) = (0, 2)$$. The wrong order slides first, to $$(-2, 2)$$, and then turns to $$(2, 2)$$,
   which is not $$O$$. The right order gives $$F^{-1}(0, 2) = (0, 0)$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-combining-moves-quarter-conjugate}

1. Show that $$G_{90}(P + A) = G_{90}(P) + G_{90}(A)$$ for any points $$P$$ and $$A$$.
2. Use part 1 to show that $$G_{90} \circ T_A \circ G_{90}^{-1} = T_{G_{90}(A)}$$: a slide seen
   through a quarter turn is the slide by the turned step.
3. Which slide is it when $$A = (3, 1)$$?

:::solution

1. Write $$P = (x, y)$$ and $$A = (a_1, a_2)$$. Then

   ```math
   G_{90}(P + A) = \big(-(y + a_2), x + a_1\big) = (-y, x) + (-a_2, a_1) = G_{90}(P) + G_{90}(A)
   ```

2. Take any point $$P$$ and write $$X = G_{90}^{-1}(P)$$, so that $$G_{90}(X) = P$$. By part 1,

   ```math
   (G_{90} \circ T_A \circ G_{90}^{-1})(P) = G_{90}(X + A) = G_{90}(X) + G_{90}(A) = P + G_{90}(A)
   ```

   which is $$T_{G_{90}(A)}(P)$$.

3. $$G_{90}(3, 1) = (-1, 3)$$, so the composite is the slide by $$(-1, 3)$$. A step of $$3$$ right
   and $$1$$ up, turned a quarter turn, becomes $$1$$ left and $$3$$ up.
