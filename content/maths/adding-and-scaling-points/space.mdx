Imagine sending a parcel by drone to a rooftop down the road. The flight plan is a list of steps in
metres: climb $$50$$ straight up, fly $$120$$ east and $$40$$ north, then drop $$20$$ onto the roof.
Each step has three numbers, one for east, one for north and one for up. You'd want to know where
the drone ends up, and how far that is from the start in a straight line.

Adding the three steps answers the first question, and the length of their sum answers the second.
Adding and scaling work in space as they do in the plane, with one more coordinate. In this guide
we'll carry the plane's rules over to triples, and then look at the one place where space behaves
differently.

## Steps in space

A step in space says how far to go east, north and up, so it is a triple of numbers. Taking one step
after another adds the matching numbers, and taking the same step twice doubles each of them. The
coordinates never mix: what happens to the heights has nothing to do with what happens east or
north.

So the definitions from [Scaling points](./scaling-points.mdx) and
[Adding points](./adding-points.mdx) carry over with one more coordinate. Points of space live in
$$\mathbb{R}^3$$ (:ref{#coordinates/coordinate-space}), and the origin is $$O = (0, 0, 0)$$.

:::definition[Adding and scaling in space]{#space-operations}

Let $$A = (a_1, a_2, a_3)$$ and $$B = (b_1, b_2, b_3)$$ be points of $$\mathbb{R}^3$$, and $$c$$ a
number. Then:

- the **sum** is $$A + B = (a_1 + b_1, a_2 + b_2, a_3 + b_3)$$
- the **multiple** is $$cA = (ca_1, ca_2, ca_3)$$
- the **negative** is $$-A = (-1)A = (-a_1, -a_2, -a_3)$$
- the **difference** is $$A - B = A + (-B) = (a_1 - b_1, a_2 - b_2, a_3 - b_3)$$

:::

:::example[Where the drone lands]

The three steps of the flight plan are $$(0, 0, 50)$$, $$(120, 40, 0)$$ and $$(0, 0, -20)$$. Adding
them, coordinate by coordinate,

```math
(0, 0, 50) + (120, 40, 0) + (0, 0, -20) = (0 + 120 + 0,\ 0 + 40 + 0,\ 50 + 0 - 20) = (120, 40, 30)
```

So the drone lands $$120$$ m east, $$40$$ m north and $$30$$ m up from where it took off. The drop
is a step with a negative height, and the sum takes care of it like any other number.

:::

<DroneSteps caption="The flight plan as three steps: up 50, across (120, 40, 0), down 20. The straight arrow from O to the rooftop at (120, 40, 30) runs through the box with those sides, and it is 130 m long." />

The rules for adding and scaling points in the plane came from the rules for numbers, one coordinate
at a time. A third coordinate is one more number obeying the same rules, so every one of them holds
in space too.

:::proposition[The rules carry over]{#space-rules}

For all points $$A$$, $$B$$ and $$C$$ of $$\mathbb{R}^3$$ and all numbers $$b$$ and $$c$$:

1. **Order doesn't matter**: $$A + B = B + A$$
2. **Grouping doesn't matter**: $$(A + B) + C = A + (B + C)$$
3. **Adding the origin**: $$A + O = A$$
4. **Adding the negative**: $$A + (-A) = O$$
5. **Scaling twice**: $$b(cA) = (bc)A$$
6. **Scaling a sum**: $$c(A + B) = cA + cB$$
7. **Adding multiples**: $$(b + c)A = bA + cA$$

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_vector#Addition_and_subtraction"}

Each rule says that two triples are equal, and two triples are equal exactly when their three
coordinates are (:ref{#coordinates/coordinate-space}). So it is enough to check one coordinate, say
the first, since the other two work the same way. In the first coordinate each rule is a rule for
real numbers (:ref{#real-numbers/real-rules}):

1. $$a_1 + b_1 = b_1 + a_1$$, since order doesn't matter in a sum
2. $$(a_1 + b_1) + c_1 = a_1 + (b_1 + c_1)$$, since grouping doesn't matter in a sum
3. $$a_1 + 0 = a_1$$, by the rule for zero
4. $$a_1 + (-a_1) = 0$$, by the rule for opposites
5. $$b(ca_1) = (bc)a_1$$, since grouping doesn't matter in a product
6. $$c(a_1 + b_1) = ca_1 + cb_1$$, by distributivity
7. $$(b + c)a_1 = ba_1 + ca_1$$, by distributivity and order not mattering in a product

These are the lines from the proofs of :ref{#sum-rules}, :ref{#scale-twice} and
:ref{#scale-sum-rules}. Space only adds a third coordinate, which works the same way.

:::

Anything the plane guides proved from these rules alone holds in space as well. For example,
:ref{#difference-step} carries over: $$A - B$$ is the step that takes you from $$B$$ to $$A$$, since
$$B + (A - B) = A$$. And you can rearrange sums and multiples of triples as freely as sums and
products of numbers.

A 3D game or animation uses these rules many times a second. A character in the game is stored as a
list of corners, each a triple, often thousands of them, joined up into a surface. To move the
character $$2$$ units east and lift it $$1$$ unit, the program adds $$(2, 0, 1)$$ to every corner.
The steps between the corners don't change, so the character keeps its shape and lands in its new
place in one piece.

## Length in space

The length of a step is how far it carries you in a straight line. For a step in space that is the
distance from $$O$$ to the point the step reaches, which :ref{#coordinates/space-distance} already
gives: square the three coordinates, add, and take the square root.

:::definition[Length in space]{#space-norm}

The **length** of a point $$A = (a_1, a_2, a_3)$$ of $$\mathbb{R}^3$$ is

```math
|A| = \sqrt{a_1^2 + a_2^2 + a_3^2} = d(O, A)
```

As in the plane (:ref{#norm}), it is also called the **norm** of $$A$$.

:::

For the drone, the straight line from the take-off point to the rooftop has length

```math
|(120, 40, 30)| = \sqrt{14\,400 + 1600 + 900} = \sqrt{16\,900} = 130
```

metres. The flight itself is $$50 + \sqrt{120^2 + 40^2} + 20 = 70 + \sqrt{16\,000}$$, about
$$196.5$$ metres, because the climb and the drop are a detour. The three facts about length from
[Length and direction](./length.mdx) carry over too.

:::proposition[Length in space works as in the plane]{#space-norm-rules}

For all points $$A$$ and $$B$$ of $$\mathbb{R}^3$$ and every number $$c$$:

1. **Distance as a length**: $$d(A, B) = |A - B|$$
2. **Length of a multiple**: $$|cA| = |c|\,|A|$$
3. **The triangle inequality**: $$|A + B| \le |A| + |B|$$

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norm_(mathematics)#Euclidean_norm"}

Write $$A = (a_1, a_2, a_3)$$ and $$B = (b_1, b_2, b_3)$$.

**Part 1.** The coordinates of $$A - B$$ are $$a_1 - b_1$$, $$a_2 - b_2$$ and $$a_3 - b_3$$.
Squaring them and adding gives the sum under the square root in :ref{#coordinates/space-distance},
so $$|A - B| = d(B, A) = d(A, B)$$, using rule 2 of :ref{#coordinates/space-distance-rules}.

**Part 2.** The coordinates of $$cA$$ are $$ca_1$$, $$ca_2$$ and $$ca_3$$, so

```math
|cA| = \sqrt{c^2a_1^2 + c^2a_2^2 + c^2a_3^2} = \sqrt{c^2}\,\sqrt{a_1^2 + a_2^2 + a_3^2} = |c|\,|A|
```

by :ref{#real-numbers/root-product} and $$\sqrt{c^2} = |c|$$ (:ref{#real-numbers/abs-basics}).

**Part 3.** By the triangle inequality in space (rule 3 of :ref{#coordinates/space-distance-rules})
for the points $$O$$, $$A$$ and $$A + B$$,

```math
d(O, A + B) \le d(O, A) + d(A, A + B)
```

The left side is $$|A + B|$$ and the first term on the right is $$|A|$$. The last term is
$$d(A + B, A)$$ (rule 2), which by part 1 is $$|(A + B) - A| = |B|$$, since $$(A + B) - A = B$$ by
:ref{#space-rules}.

:::

Part 1 explains why the game character in [Steps in space](#steps-in-space) keeps its shape. Adding
the same triple $$C$$ to two corners $$P$$ and $$Q$$ leaves the step between them alone, since
$$(Q + C) - (P + C) = Q - P$$, so their distance $$|Q - P|$$ doesn't change. Part 2 says the same
for scaling: $$d(cA, cB) = |cA - cB| = |c(A - B)| = |c|\,d(A, B)$$. For $$c > 0$$ that is
:ref{#coordinates/space-zoom}, and now it holds for negative $$c$$ as well.

## Unit points and midpoints

Every step in space is so far east, so far north and so far up. Each of those three moves is a
multiple of a step of length $$1$$ along an axis, and together they make the whole step.

:::proposition[Every point from three unit points]{#space-unit-combination}

Let $$E_1 = (1, 0, 0)$$, $$E_2 = (0, 1, 0)$$ and $$E_3 = (0, 0, 1)$$, the **unit points** of space
(:ref{#unit-points}). Every point $$A = (a_1, a_2, a_3)$$ of $$\mathbb{R}^3$$ is

```math
A = a_1E_1 + a_2E_2 + a_3E_3
```

and these are the only three numbers that work.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_basis"}

For any numbers $$s$$, $$t$$ and $$u$$, :ref{#space-operations} gives

```math
sE_1 + tE_2 + uE_3 = (s, 0, 0) + (0, t, 0) + (0, 0, u) = (s, t, u)
```

This equals $$A$$ exactly when $$s = a_1$$, $$t = a_2$$ and $$u = a_3$$
(:ref{#coordinates/coordinate-space}). So $$a_1$$, $$a_2$$ and $$a_3$$ work, and no other numbers
do.

:::

This is :ref{#unit-combination} with a third direction. The drone's rooftop is
$$(120, 40, 30) = 120E_1 + 40E_2 + 30E_3$$, and the box in the figure under
[Steps in space](#steps-in-space) has those three steps as its sides.

Halfway between two points is still their average. In the plane, :ref{#midpoint-formula} rested on
rule 1 of :ref{#distance-and-angles/segment-rules}: a point $$Q$$ lies on the segment $$PM$$ exactly
when $$d(P, M) = d(P, Q) + d(Q, M)$$. We take that rule from the picture in space too, as the plane
did, and then the same proof works. The next chapter finds the midpoint in space without it
(:ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/space-line-facts}).

:::proposition[The midpoint in space]{#space-midpoint}

For different points $$A$$ and $$B$$ of $$\mathbb{R}^3$$, the midpoint
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/midpoint}) of the segment $$AB$$ is $$\frac{1}{2}(A + B)$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midpoint#Formula"}

Let $$M = \frac{1}{2}(A + B)$$. Rearranging with :ref{#space-rules}, $$M - A = \frac{1}{2}(B - A)$$
and $$B - M = \frac{1}{2}(B - A)$$. So by :ref{#space-norm-rules},

```math
d(A, M) = |M - A| = \tfrac{1}{2}\,|B - A| = \tfrac{1}{2}\,d(A, B)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
d(M, B) = |B - M| = \tfrac{1}{2}\,d(A, B)
```

These add up to $$d(A, B)$$, so $$M$$ lies on the segment $$AB$$, by rule 1 of the segment rules. It
is the same distance from both ends, so it is the midpoint.

:::

Halfway along the straight line from the take-off point to the rooftop is
$$\frac{1}{2}(120, 40, 30) = (60, 20, 15)$$, $$65$$ metres from each end.

## Spheres are slid copies

Imagine blowing up a balloon to a fixed size and then carrying it across a room without squeezing
it. The balloon has moved, but its shape hasn't changed. A sphere anywhere in space is the same as a
sphere about the origin, carried across by one step.

:::proposition[Spheres are slid copies]{#sphere-slide}

Let $$A$$ be a point of $$\mathbb{R}^3$$ and $$r > 0$$. The sphere with centre $$A$$ and radius
$$r$$ (:ref{#coordinates/sphere}) is the set of points $$X$$ with $$|X - A| = r$$. It is the image
of the sphere with centre $$O$$ and radius $$r$$ under the slide $$P \mapsto P + A$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sphere#Equations"}

By :ref{#space-norm-rules}, $$d(A, X) = |X - A|$$, which gives the first sentence. For the second,
there are two things to show.

**Every slid point is on the sphere.** If $$|P| = r$$, then $$(P + A) - A = P$$ by
:ref{#space-rules}, so $$|(P + A) - A| = r$$.

**Every point of the sphere is a slid point.** If $$|X - A| = r$$, let $$P = X - A$$. Then
$$|P| = r$$, so $$P$$ is on the sphere with centre $$O$$, and $$P + A = X$$ by :ref{#space-rules}.

:::

:::example[Sliding a point of a sphere]

The point $$P = (1, 2, 2)$$ has $$|P| = \sqrt{1 + 4 + 4} = 3$$, so it is on the sphere with centre
$$O$$ and radius $$3$$. Sliding by $$A = (2, -1, 3)$$ takes it to $$P + A = (3, 1, 5)$$. The step
from $$A$$ to that point is $$(3, 1, 5) - (2, -1, 3) = (1, 2, 2)$$, of length $$3$$, so
$$(3, 1, 5)$$ is on the sphere with centre $$A$$ and radius $$3$$.

:::

This is :ref{#circle-slide} with one more coordinate, and the same holds for balls, with $$\le$$ in
place of $$=$$. Scaling works as it did for circles in :ref{#circle-scale}: by part 2 of
:ref{#space-norm-rules}, $$P \mapsto cP$$ with $$c \ne 0$$ sends the sphere with centre $$O$$ and
radius $$1$$ onto the one with radius $$|c|$$. So every sphere is the sphere of radius $$1$$ about
$$O$$, scaled and then slid.

## The negative in space

In the plane, sending each point $$A$$ to $$-A$$ is a half turn about $$O$$
(:ref{#negative-half-turn}). In space, $$A \mapsto -A$$ still sends every point straight through
$$O$$ to the far side, the same distance away. But in space it is not a turn.

Hold up a right-hand glove with its fingers pointing to the ceiling, put $$O$$ in the middle of the
palm, and send each point of the glove to its negative. The fingers now point to the floor, and the
glove has become a left-hand glove. No amount of turning makes a right glove fit a left hand, so
this move is not a turn.

:::remark[The negative in space]{#space-negative}

In $$\mathbb{R}^3$$, the map $$A \mapsto -A$$ keeps every distance, since
$$|(-A) - (-B)| = |A - B|$$. The origin $$O$$ is the midpoint of $$A$$ and $$-A$$, because
$$\frac{1}{2}(A + (-A)) = O$$ (:ref{#space-midpoint}). So $$A \mapsto -A$$ is the reflection through
$$O$$, as in :ref{#isometries/point-reflection}.

But it is not a turn about any line. A turn about a line keeps every point of that line where it is,
and $$A \mapsto -A$$ moves every point except $$O$$. It swaps left-handed shapes for right-handed
ones, like a mirror. In fact it is a half turn about the $$z$$-axis,
$$(x, y, z) \mapsto (-x, -y, z)$$, followed by the mirror in the floor,
$$(x, y, z) \mapsto (x, y, -z)$$.

::see-also{src="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_reflection#Properties"}

:::

So in the plane the half turn and the reflection through a point are one map, and in space they come
apart. A half turn about an axis changes the signs of two coordinates, while the reflection through
$$O$$ changes all three, and the third sign change is a mirror.

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on points in space, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-space-operations}

Let $$A = (2, -1, 3)$$ and $$B = (-1, 4, 2)$$. Find $$A + B$$, $$3A$$, $$A - B$$ and $$2A - 3B$$.
Then check that $$B + (A - B) = A$$.

:::solution

Working coordinate by coordinate (:ref{#space-operations}):

- $$A + B = (2 - 1, -1 + 4, 3 + 2) = (1, 3, 5)$$
- $$3A = (6, -3, 9)$$
- $$A - B = (2 + 1, -1 - 4, 3 - 2) = (3, -5, 1)$$
- $$2A - 3B = (4, -2, 6) - (-3, 12, 6) = (7, -14, 0)$$

Finally, $$B + (A - B) = (-1 + 3, 4 - 5, 2 + 1) = (2, -1, 3)$$, which is $$A$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-space-direction}

Let $$A = (2, -3, 6)$$. Find $$|A|$$ and $$|-2A|$$. Then find the point of length $$1$$ that is a
positive multiple of $$A$$, and check its length.

:::solution

By :ref{#space-norm}, $$|A| = \sqrt{4 + 9 + 36} = \sqrt{49} = 7$$. By part 2 of
:ref{#space-norm-rules}, $$|-2A| = |-2|\,|A| = 14$$.

For a positive multiple $$cA$$ to have length $$1$$ we need $$c\,|A| = 1$$, so $$c = \frac{1}{7}$$,
as in :ref{#direction}. The point is

```math
\tfrac{1}{7}A = \left(\tfrac{2}{7}, -\tfrac{3}{7}, \tfrac{6}{7}\right),
\qquad
\left|\tfrac{1}{7}A\right|^2 = \frac{4 + 9 + 36}{49} = 1
```

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-space-midpoints}

Find the midpoint of $$(1, 5, -2)$$ and $$(7, -1, 4)$$. Then, given $$A = (3, 0, -1)$$, find the
point $$B$$ for which $$(1, 2, 2)$$ is the midpoint of $$A$$ and $$B$$.

:::solution

By :ref{#space-midpoint}, the midpoint is $$\frac{1}{2}(8, 4, 2) = (4, 2, 1)$$.

For the second part, $$\frac{1}{2}(A + B) = M$$ with $$M = (1, 2, 2)$$. Multiplying by $$2$$ and
taking $$A$$ from both sides (:ref{#space-rules}) gives

```math
B = 2M - A = (2, 4, 4) - (3, 0, -1) = (-1, 4, 5)
```

Check: $$\frac{1}{2}\big((3, 0, -1) + (-1, 4, 5)\big) = \frac{1}{2}(2, 4, 4) = (1, 2, 2)$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-space-flight}

A drone flies three steps, in metres east, north and up: $$(30, 0, 20)$$, then $$(0, 40, 0)$$, then
$$(60, 80, -20)$$. Where does it end up, and how far is that from the start in a straight line? How
long is the flight itself?

:::solution

Adding the steps,

```math
(30 + 0 + 60,\ 0 + 40 + 80,\ 20 + 0 - 20) = (90, 120, 0)
```

so it ends at the height it started from. The straight line has length
$$\sqrt{8100 + 14\,400 + 0} = \sqrt{22\,500} = 150$$ metres (:ref{#space-norm}).

The three steps have lengths $$\sqrt{900 + 400} = \sqrt{1300} \approx 36.06$$, $$40$$ and
$$\sqrt{3600 + 6400 + 400} = \sqrt{10\,400} \approx 101.98$$. The flight is about $$178.0$$ metres,
longer than $$150$$, as the triangle inequality in :ref{#space-norm-rules} says it must be.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-space-reverse-triangle}

Show that $$|A| - |B| \le |A - B|$$ for all points $$A$$ and $$B$$ of $$\mathbb{R}^3$$.

:::solution

By :ref{#space-rules}, $$A = (A - B) + B$$. The triangle inequality in :ref{#space-norm-rules} gives

```math
|A| = |(A - B) + B| \le |A - B| + |B|
```

Taking $$|B|$$ from both sides gives $$|A| - |B| \le |A - B|$$. In words, two points at distances
$$|A|$$ and $$|B|$$ from $$O$$ are at least $$|A| - |B|$$ apart, as :ref{#norm-difference} says in
the plane.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-space-sphere-through-origin}

Let $$A = (1, -2, 2)$$. Show that the sphere with centre $$A$$ and radius $$3$$ passes through
$$O$$. Find the point of this sphere on the far side of $$A$$ from $$O$$, so that $$A$$ is the
midpoint of $$O$$ and that point. Then find the point of the sphere with centre $$O$$ and radius
$$3$$ that the slide $$P \mapsto P + A$$ sends to $$O$$.

:::solution

The step from $$A$$ to $$O$$ is $$O - A = (-1, 2, -2)$$, of length $$\sqrt{1 + 4 + 4} = 3$$, so
$$O$$ is on the sphere (:ref{#sphere-slide}).

If $$A$$ is the midpoint of $$O$$ and $$X$$, then $$\frac{1}{2}(O + X) = A$$
(:ref{#space-midpoint}), so $$X = 2A = (2, -4, 4)$$. It is on the sphere, since $$X - A = A$$ has
length $$3$$. Its distance from $$O$$ is $$|2A| = 6$$, the diameter.

The slide sends $$P$$ to $$O$$ exactly when $$P + A = O$$, that is, $$P = -A = (-1, 2, -2)$$. Its
length is $$3$$, so it is on the sphere with centre $$O$$, as :ref{#sphere-slide} promises.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-space-two-directions}

Find numbers $$b$$ and $$c$$ with $$b(1, 1, 0) + c(0, 1, 1) = (2, 5, 3)$$. Then show that no numbers
$$b$$ and $$c$$ give $$(1, 0, 1)$$.

:::solution

The left side is $$(b, b + c, c)$$. For $$(2, 5, 3)$$ the first and last coordinates give $$b = 2$$
and $$c = 3$$, and the middle one checks: $$2 + 3 = 5$$.

For $$(1, 0, 1)$$ the first and last coordinates force $$b = 1$$ and $$c = 1$$. But then the middle
coordinate is $$2$$, not $$0$$. So no numbers work. Two steps can't reach every point of space this
way, which is why :ref{#space-unit-combination} needs three.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-space-negative-split}

Let $$K(x, y, z) = (-x, -y, z)$$, the half turn about the $$z$$-axis, and
$$F(x, y, z) = (x, y, -z)$$, the mirror in the floor. Show that $$F(K(P)) = -P$$ and
$$K(F(P)) = -P$$ for every point $$P$$. Which points does $$P \mapsto -P$$ leave where they are?

:::solution

Write $$P = (x, y, z)$$. Then

```math
F(K(P)) = F(-x, -y, z) = (-x, -y, -z) = -P,
\qquad
K(F(P)) = K(x, y, -z) = (-x, -y, -z) = -P
```

So the two moves give $$-P$$ in either order, as :ref{#space-negative} says.

A point stays put exactly when $$-P = P$$, that is, when $$-x = x$$, $$-y = y$$ and $$-z = z$$. Each
of these says the coordinate is $$0$$, so the only such point is $$O$$. A turn about a line would
leave the whole line where it is, so $$P \mapsto -P$$ is not one.

:::
