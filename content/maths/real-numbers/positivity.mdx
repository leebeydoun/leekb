Picture the number line, with the positive numbers to the right of $$0$$ and the negative ones to
the left. You've long known rules like "minus times minus is plus" and "a square is never negative".
All of them follow from two rules about positive numbers. In this guide we'll state those two rules
and prove the rest, which also sets up the rules for inequalities in
[Order and inequalities](./order.mdx).

## Two rules for positive numbers

Think of the positive numbers as amounts you can actually have: a length, a weight, money in the
bank. Put two of them together and you have a positive amount again. A rectangle with positive sides
has a positive area, so the product of two positive numbers is positive too. And every number is
either one of these amounts, or nothing at all, or the opposite of one, like a debt. These
observations become the two rules below.

The word "positive" isn't defined from anything more basic. Instead, some real numbers are called
**positive**, written $$a > 0$$, and two rules about them are taken as given. Everything else about
signs is proved from these.

:::remark[Rules for positivity]{#positivity}

1. **Positive sums and products:** if $$a$$ and $$b$$ are positive, so are $$a + b$$ and $$ab$$.
2. **Exactly one:** for every real number $$a$$, exactly one of these is true: $$a$$ is positive,
   $$a = 0$$, or $$-a$$ is positive.

These are taken as given, together with the [rules of arithmetic](./real-line.mdx).

::see-also{src="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordered_field#Positive_cone"}

:::

:::definition[Negative numbers]{#negative}

A real number $$a$$ is **negative**, written $$a < 0$$, if $$-a$$ is positive.

:::

So rule 2 splits the real numbers into three parts: every real number is exactly one of positive,
zero and negative. The positive numbers are the ones shaded here:

## One is positive

You might expect $$1$$ to be positive, but nothing so far says so. The two rules never mention any
particular number, so this has to be worked out. The key is that $$1$$ is a square: it is
$$(-1)(-1)$$ as well as $$1 \cdot 1$$. So whichever of $$1$$ and $$-1$$ is positive, multiplying it
by itself gives $$1$$, and rule 1 makes that positive.

:::proposition[One is positive]{#one-positive}

The number $$1$$ is positive.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordered_field#Properties_of_ordered_fields"}

Since $$1 \neq 0$$, rule 2 says that exactly one of $$1$$ and $$-1$$ is positive. The proof shows
that it can't be $$-1$$, by contradiction (:ref{#basic-proofs/contradiction}).

Suppose $$-1$$ were positive. Then by rule 1 the product $$(-1)(-1)$$ would be positive too. But
$$(-1)(-1) = 1$$, because two minus signs cancel (:ref{#numbers/two-minus}). So $$1$$ and $$-1$$
would both be positive, which rule 2 forbids. So $$-1$$ is not positive, and therefore $$1$$ is.

:::

Rule 1 then makes $$2 = 1 + 1$$ positive, then $$3 = 2 + 1$$, and so on: the positive integers are
positive real numbers, as they should be.

This also explains why the remainders on division by $$5$$, from the
[previous page](./real-line.mdx), can't be split into positive and negative in the same way, even
though they follow all the rules of arithmetic. If they could, $$1$$ would be positive, and adding
it up five times would make $$1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1$$ positive. But in that system the sum is $$0$$,
which rule 2 says is not positive.

## Signs of products

Multiplying by a negative number turns things into their opposites. Three lots of a debt of $$2$$ is
a debt of $$6$$, so $$3 \cdot (-2) = -6$$: a positive times a negative is negative. Multiplying by
another negative number turns things round a second time, back to where they started, so
$$(-3) \cdot (-2) = 6$$ is positive. The proof below shows that both facts follow from the two
rules.

:::proposition[Products with negative factors]{#sign-rules}

Let $$a$$ and $$b$$ be real numbers.

1. If $$a$$ is positive and $$b$$ is negative, then $$ab$$ is negative.
2. If $$a$$ and $$b$$ are both negative, then $$ab$$ is positive.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordered_field#Properties_of_ordered_fields"}

**Part 1.** Since $$b$$ is negative, $$-b$$ is positive. By rule 1, $$a(-b)$$ is positive. Moving
the minus sign out (:ref{#numbers/minus-in-product}), $$a(-b) = -(ab)$$. So $$-(ab)$$ is positive,
which says that $$ab$$ is negative.

**Part 2.** Now $$-a$$ and $$-b$$ are both positive, so their product $$(-a)(-b)$$ is positive by
rule 1. Two minus signs cancel (:ref{#numbers/two-minus}), so $$(-a)(-b) = ab$$, and $$ab$$ is
positive.

:::

Together with rule 1 and $$0 \cdot b = 0$$, this settles the sign of every product: it is positive
when the signs match, negative when they differ, and $$0$$ when either factor is $$0$$.

## Squares

A square is a number multiplied by itself, so its two factors always have the same sign. By the
previous section, a product of two numbers with the same sign is positive. So squaring any number
other than $$0$$ gives a positive answer: $$3^2 = 9$$ and $$(-3)^2 = 9$$ alike.

Part 2 of the corollary below is about sums of squares. Two amounts that are never negative add up
to $$0$$ only when each of them is $$0$$.

:::corollary[Squares are never negative]{#squares-positive}

For every real number $$a$$:

1. If $$a \neq 0$$, then $$a^2$$ is positive. If $$a = 0$$, then $$a^2 = 0$$. So $$a^2$$ is never
   negative.
2. If $$a^2 + b^2 = 0$$, then $$a = 0$$ and $$b = 0$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordered_field#Properties_of_ordered_fields"}

**Part 1.** If $$a$$ is positive, $$a^2 = a \cdot a$$ is positive by rule 1. If $$a$$ is negative,
$$a^2$$ is a product of two negative numbers, which is positive by :ref{#sign-rules}. If $$a = 0$$,
then $$a^2 = 0$$.

**Part 2.** This part proves the contrapositive (:ref{#basic-proofs/contrapositive}): if $$a$$ and
$$b$$ are not both $$0$$, then $$a^2 + b^2 \neq 0$$. Say $$a \neq 0$$; the case $$b \neq 0$$ is the
same. Then $$a^2$$ is positive. If $$b = 0$$, the sum is $$a^2$$, which is positive. Otherwise
$$b^2$$ is positive too, and the sum of two positive numbers is positive. Either way the sum is
positive, so it isn't $$0$$.

:::

So an equation like $$x^2 = -4$$ has no real solution at all: the left side is never negative, and
the right side is. Likewise $$x^2 + 1$$ is always at least $$1$$, so it is never $$0$$.

## Signs of inverses and quotients

The inverse of a number is what you multiply it by to get $$1$$. Since $$1$$ is positive, the number
and its inverse must have the same sign, because a mix of signs would give a negative product. So
the inverse of $$4$$ is the positive number $$0.25$$, and the inverse of $$-4$$ is the negative
number $$-0.25$$.

:::proposition[Signs of inverses]{#inverse-sign}

If $$a$$ is positive, so is $$\frac{1}{a}$$. If $$a$$ is negative, so is $$\frac{1}{a}$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inequality_(mathematics)#Multiplicative_inverse"}

By the definition of an inverse, $$a \cdot \frac{1}{a} = 1$$, which is positive by
:ref{#one-positive}. First, $$\frac{1}{a} \neq 0$$, since otherwise the product would be $$0$$
(:ref{#numbers/zero-times}).

Suppose $$a$$ is positive. If $$\frac{1}{a}$$ were negative, the product $$a \cdot \frac{1}{a}$$
would be negative by :ref{#sign-rules}, so $$1$$ would be both positive and negative, which rule 2
forbids. So $$\frac{1}{a}$$ is positive.

Suppose $$a$$ is negative. If $$\frac{1}{a}$$ were positive, the product would again be negative, by
part 1 of :ref{#sign-rules} with the factors swapped; that is impossible for the same reason. So
$$\frac{1}{a}$$ is negative.

:::

A quotient $$\frac{a}{b}$$ means $$a \cdot \frac{1}{b}$$ (:ref{#numbers/quotient}), so it follows
the same sign rules as a product.

:::corollary[Signs of quotients]{#quotient-sign}

If $$b \neq 0$$, then $$\frac{a}{b}$$ is positive when $$a$$ and $$b$$ have the same sign, negative
when they have opposite signs, and $$0$$ when $$a = 0$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inequality_(mathematics)#Multiplicative_inverse"}

Write $$\frac{a}{b} = a \cdot \frac{1}{b}$$. By :ref{#inverse-sign}, $$\frac{1}{b}$$ has the same
sign as $$b$$, so this is a product of $$a$$ with a number of the same sign as $$b$$. Its sign is
then given by rule 1 and :ref{#sign-rules}: positive when the signs match and negative when they
differ. If $$a = 0$$, the product is $$0$$.

:::

For example, $$\frac{-3}{-4}$$ is positive and $$\frac{5}{-2}$$ is negative, without working out
either one.

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on positive and negative numbers, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-positivity-zero}

Use :ref{#positivity} to show that $$0$$ is neither positive nor negative.

:::solution

Rule 2 with $$a = 0$$ says that exactly one of these is true: $$0$$ is positive, $$0 = 0$$, or
$$-0$$ is positive. The middle one is true, so the other two are false. In particular $$0$$ is not
positive.

By :ref{#negative}, $$0$$ would be negative if $$-0$$ were positive. But $$-0 = 0$$, which is not
positive, so $$0$$ is not negative either.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-positivity-minus-sign}

Someone says: "$$-a$$ is always negative, because it has a minus sign." Give a counterexample, and
say exactly when $$-a$$ is negative.

:::solution

Take $$a = -3$$. Then $$-a = 3$$, which is positive.

By :ref{#negative}, $$-a$$ is negative when $$-(-a)$$ is positive. Since $$-(-a) = a$$
(:ref{#numbers/double-opposite}), $$-a$$ is negative exactly when $$a$$ is positive. The minus sign
says "the opposite of", not "negative".

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-positivity-signs}

Suppose $$a < 0 < b$$. Without choosing numbers, decide whether each of these is positive or
negative: $$ab^2$$, $$a^3$$, $$\frac{b}{a}$$ and $$\frac{-a}{-b}$$.

:::solution

- $$ab^2$$: since $$b \neq 0$$, $$b^2$$ is positive (:ref{#squares-positive}). A negative times a
  positive is negative (:ref{#sign-rules}), so $$ab^2$$ is negative.
- $$a^3 = a \cdot a^2$$: here $$a^2$$ is positive and $$a$$ negative, so $$a^3$$ is negative.
- $$\frac{b}{a}$$: the top and bottom have opposite signs, so it is negative (:ref{#quotient-sign}).
- $$\frac{-a}{-b}$$: the top $$-a$$ is positive and the bottom $$-b$$ is negative, so it is
  negative.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-positivity-negative-sum}

Prove that the sum of two negative numbers is negative.

:::solution

Let $$a$$ and $$b$$ be negative, so $$-a$$ and $$-b$$ are positive. The opposite of a sum is the sum
of the opposites (:ref{#numbers/opposite-sum}), so

```math
-(a + b) = (-a) + (-b)
```

This is a sum of two positive numbers, so it is positive by rule 1 of :ref{#positivity}. So
$$a + b$$ is negative.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-positivity-cube}

Prove that if $$a \neq 0$$, then $$a$$ and $$a^3$$ have the same sign.

:::solution

Write $$a^3 = a \cdot a^2$$. Since $$a \neq 0$$, $$a^2$$ is positive (:ref{#squares-positive}).

- If $$a$$ is positive, $$a \cdot a^2$$ is a product of positive numbers, so it is positive by rule
  1 of :ref{#positivity}.
- If $$a$$ is negative, $$a \cdot a^2$$ is a negative number times a positive one, so it is negative
  by :ref{#sign-rules}.

Either way $$a^3$$ has the same sign as $$a$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-positivity-fractions}

Show that $$\frac{1}{2}$$ is positive and that $$-\frac{1}{3}$$ is negative, using only the results
on this page.

:::solution

By :ref{#one-positive}, $$1$$ is positive, so $$2 = 1 + 1$$ and $$3 = 2 + 1$$ are positive by rule 1
of :ref{#positivity}. Their inverses $$\frac{1}{2}$$ and $$\frac{1}{3}$$ are positive by
:ref{#inverse-sign}.

The number $$-\frac{1}{3}$$ is negative because its opposite, $$\frac{1}{3}$$, is positive
(:ref{#negative}).

:::
