  What real numbers are, why the fractions leave gaps, and the rules of arithmetic they obey.

Draw a square with sides of length $$1$$, and then its diagonal. The diagonal has a length whose
square is $$2$$, but no fraction squares to $$2$$ (:ref{#numbers/sqrt-two}, from
[Irrational numbers](../numbers/irrational-numbers.mdx)). So the fractions leave a gap on the number
line, even though they're packed so tightly that between any two there's always another.

The **real numbers** fill every gap, so that every point of the line has a number. In this guide
we'll see what they are, and which rules of arithmetic they share with the fractions.

## A length that isn't a fraction

It is tempting to think that any length can be measured exactly by a fraction, as long as the unit
is cut into small enough pieces. One drawing shows that this is wrong.

Draw a $$2$$ by $$2$$ square, split into four $$1$$ by $$1$$ squares. In each small square, draw the
diagonal that doesn't touch the centre of the big square. The four diagonals join up into a tilted
square, standing on its corner, with the centre of the big square in the middle of it.

::diagonal-square

Each diagonal cuts its small square into two halves, and exactly one half of each lies inside the
tilted square. So the tilted square is made of four halves of a $$1$$ by $$1$$ square, and its area
is

```math
4 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} = 2
```

Its sides are the diagonals. Call their length $$d$$. The area of a square is its side squared, so
$$d^2 = 2$$, and $$d$$ is not a fraction. So a perfectly ordinary length, the diagonal of a $$1$$ by
$$1$$ square, needs a number that the fractions don't have.

## Real numbers

Picture the number line as a solid ruler with no holes in it. The fractions mark a great many of its
points, but not all of them. Lay the diagonal of length $$d$$ along the ruler from $$0$$: its end
lands on a point with no fraction. The idea of the real numbers is to give every point a number.
Decimals are the natural way to name them, since each extra digit says more precisely where the
point is.

:::definition[Real numbers]{#real}

The **real numbers** are the numbers that mark the points of the number line: each point corresponds
to exactly one real number, and each real number to exactly one point. Every real number can be
written as a decimal, which may go on forever, like $$1.41421356\ldots$$.

:::

The rational numbers are real numbers: they are exactly the decimals that stop or repeat, as the
Numbers chapter explained. The rest, whose decimals go on forever without repeating, are the
**irrational** numbers (:ref{#numbers/irrational}). So every real number is either rational or
irrational, and not both.

Building the real numbers carefully from the fractions takes a good deal of work, which belongs to a
later course. This chapter takes two facts about them as given.

:::remark[No gaps in the real line]{#no-gaps}

1. Every decimal, whether it stops or goes on forever, stands for a real number, and its digits pin
   that number down. For example, $$3.14159\ldots$$ stands for a number between $$3.1$$ and $$3.2$$,
   between $$3.14$$ and $$3.15$$, between $$3.141$$ and $$3.142$$, and so on. In general, if a
   decimal that isn't negative is cut off after $$n$$ places, giving $$c$$, the number lies between
   $$c$$ and $$c + \frac{1}{10^n}$$.
2. Every real number lies between two integers that are next to each other: for each real number
   $$x$$ there is an integer $$n$$ with $$n \le x < n + 1$$. The integer $$n$$ is the part of $$x$$
   before the decimal point, when $$x$$ is not negative.

::see-also{src="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_number#Decimal_representation"}

:::

## Decimals as approximations

The decimal for a real number pins it down one digit at a time. Since $$1.4^2 = 1.96$$ and
$$1.5^2 = 2.25$$, the number $$d$$ whose square is $$2$$ lies between $$1.4$$ and $$1.5$$:

::number-line{from=1 to=2 divisions=10 intervals="[1.4, 1.5]" labels="1.4, 1.5"}

Each further digit traps it in a stretch ten times shorter. Since $$1.41^2 = 1.9881$$ and
$$1.42^2 = 2.0164$$, it lies between $$1.41$$ and $$1.42$$, and so on forever. Because $$d$$ is
irrational, the decimal never stops and never settles into a repeating pattern.

A decimal cut off after a few places is an **approximation**, not the number itself. For example,
$$1.414^2 = 1.999396$$, which is close to $$2$$ but not equal to it.

## Is 0.999… equal to 1?

Some real numbers have two different decimals. The
[Numbers](../numbers/rational-numbers.mdx#decimals) chapter turned repeating decimals into fractions
by shifting them. The same trick, applied to $$x = 0.999\ldots$$, gives

```math
10x = 9.999\ldots \qquad \text{so} \qquad 10x - x = 9 \qquad \text{and} \qquad x = 1
```

So $$0.999\ldots$$ and $$1$$ are two decimals for the **same** real number. That can feel wrong:
surely $$0.999\ldots$$ stops a little short of $$1$$? But then there would be a positive gap between
them, and that gap would have to be smaller than $$1 - 0.9 = 0.1$$, smaller than
$$1 - 0.99 = 0.01$$, smaller than $$0.001$$, and so on forever. No positive number is that small.
The careful proof needs the rules for inequalities, so it comes on the
[Order and inequalities](./order.mdx) page, as :ref{#point-nine}.

The same happens for any decimal that ends in endless $$9$$s: $$0.24999\ldots = 0.25$$. This is the
only way a real number can have two different decimals.

## The rules of arithmetic

Real numbers can be added and multiplied, and the results are again real numbers. The rules they
follow are the same ones the fractions follow. They are the habits of everyday arithmetic, such as
$$3 + 5 = 5 + 3$$, or working out $$4 \cdot 7 + 4 \cdot 3$$ as $$4 \cdot 10$$. Writing them down
matters because they are exactly what algebra relies on. Once you know the real numbers obey them,
the familiar steps for rearranging and simplifying expressions still work.

:::remark[Rules for real numbers]{#real-rules}

For all real numbers $$a$$, $$b$$ and $$c$$:

1. **Order doesn't matter:** $$a + b = b + a$$ and $$ab = ba$$.
2. **Grouping doesn't matter:** $$(a + b) + c = a + (b + c)$$ and $$(ab)c = a(bc)$$.
3. **Zero and one:** $$a + 0 = a$$ and $$1 \cdot a = a$$, and $$0 \neq 1$$.
4. **Opposites:** there is a number $$-a$$ with $$a + (-a) = 0$$.
5. **Inverses:** if $$a \neq 0$$, there is a number $$a^{-1}$$, also written $$\frac{1}{a}$$, with
   $$a \cdot a^{-1} = 1$$.
6. **Distributivity:** $$a(b + c) = ab + ac$$.

These are taken as given. Rules for positive and negative numbers come on the next page.

::see-also{src="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_number#Arithmetic"}

:::

These are the rules of arithmetic from the Numbers chapter, plus rule 5: every non-zero number has
an inverse. (The Numbers chapter also had rules about positive numbers; their real-number versions
come on the next page.) So any result the Numbers chapter proved from these rules alone holds for
real numbers too, with the same proof. For example:

- $$0 \cdot a = 0$$ (:ref{#numbers/zero-times}) and $$(-a)(-b) = ab$$ (:ref{#numbers/two-minus}).
- If $$ab = 0$$, then $$a = 0$$ or $$b = 0$$ (:ref{#numbers/zero-product}).
- If $$ab = ac$$ and $$a \neq 0$$, then $$b = c$$ (:ref{#numbers/cancel-product}).
- $$\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d}$$ exactly when $$ad = bc$$
  (:ref{#numbers/cross-multiplying-rational}).
- $$(a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$$ and the other formulas for squares
  (:ref{#numbers/square-formulas}).
- :ref{#numbers/power-rules}, including negative powers (:ref{#numbers/negative-powers}).

Likewise, everything in the [Linear equations](../linear-equations/introduction.mdx) chapter holds
for real numbers: a system like $$\sqrt{2}x + y = 1$$, $$x - y = 3$$ is solved exactly as before.

## Other systems obey the same rules

The six rules in :ref{#real-rules} don't on their own pin down the real numbers. Other collections
of numbers can be added and multiplied and obey all six rules too. A collection of five numbers
shows this.

### Arithmetic with five numbers

Take the numbers $$0, 1, 2, 3, 4$$, and do arithmetic the way a clock with five hours would: add or
multiply as usual, then if the answer is $$5$$ or more, keep only its remainder on division by
$$5$$. For example:

- $$3 + 4 = 7$$, which leaves remainder $$2$$, so here $$3 + 4$$ is $$2$$.
- $$3 \cdot 4 = 12$$, which leaves remainder $$2$$, so here $$3 \cdot 4$$ is $$2$$.
- $$2 \cdot 3 = 6$$, which leaves remainder $$1$$, so here $$2 \cdot 3$$ is $$1$$.

This is the arithmetic of congruences on division by $$5$$ from the Numbers chapter's
[Congruence](../numbers/congruence.mdx) page, where $$7 \equiv 2 \pmod 5$$, for example.

Now check the six rules one by one.

- **Rules 1, 2 and 6** (order, grouping, distributivity). Each answer comes from ordinary
  arithmetic, where these rules hold, followed by taking the remainder. Taking remainders doesn't
  break them, because numbers with the same remainder give sums and products with the same remainder
  (:ref{#numbers/congruence-arithmetic}).
- **Rule 3** (zero and one). Adding $$0$$ or multiplying by $$1$$ changes nothing, and $$0$$ and
  $$1$$ are different numbers.
- **Rule 4** (opposites). Each number has a partner that it adds with to give $$0$$: $$1 + 4 = 5$$
  and $$2 + 3 = 5$$, both of which leave remainder $$0$$, and $$0 + 0 = 0$$. So here $$-1$$ is $$4$$
  and $$-2$$ is $$3$$.
- **Rule 5** (inverses). Each number except $$0$$ has a partner that it multiplies with to give
  $$1$$: $$1 \cdot 1 = 1$$, and $$2 \cdot 3 = 6$$ and $$4 \cdot 4 = 16$$ both leave remainder $$1$$.
  So here $$\frac{1}{2}$$ is $$3$$ and $$\frac{1}{4}$$ is $$4$$.

### What the rules leave out

So these five numbers obey every rule in the box, yet they behave nothing like the number line. Add
$$1$$ to itself five times:

```math
1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 5, \text{ which leaves remainder } 0
```

On the number line, adding $$1$$ always moves to the right, so it never comes back to $$0$$. The
rules of arithmetic can't tell the difference, so the real numbers need more than them. Two more
properties do the job.

- **Order.** Real numbers come with a notion of positive and negative, with rules such as "the sum
  of two positive numbers is positive". The five numbers can't have one. The next page shows that in
  any such order $$1$$ is positive (:ref{#one-positive}), and then $$1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1$$ would be
  positive too, but it is $$0$$.
- **No gaps.** The rational numbers obey the rules of arithmetic and have an order, but they have
  gaps, like the missing length $$d$$ with $$d^2 = 2$$ from the start of this page. Having no gaps
  (:ref{#no-gaps}) is what sets the real numbers apart from them.

The table sums this up. Gaps only make sense for numbers in order along a line, so that column
doesn't apply to the five remainders.

| System                      | Rules of arithmetic       | Order (next page) | No gaps       |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------- | ----------------- | ------------- |
| Integers                    | all but rule 5 (inverses) | yes               | no            |
| Rational numbers            | all                       | yes               | no            |
| Real numbers                | all                       | yes               | yes           |
| Remainders on division by 5 | all                       | no                | doesn't apply |

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on the real line, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-real-line-root-two-digits}

Let $$d$$ be the positive number with $$d^2 = 2$$. Show that $$1.414 < d < 1.415$$. Then explain why
no decimal that stops, however many places it has, squares to exactly $$2$$.

:::solution

Squaring the two decimals, $$1.414^2 = 1.999396$$, which is less than $$2$$, and
$$1.415^2 = 2.002225$$, which is more than $$2$$. All three numbers are positive, and a positive
number with a smaller square is the smaller number (:ref{#power-order-converse}, proved on the
[Order and inequalities](./order.mdx) page). So $$1.414 < d < 1.415$$.

A decimal that stops is a fraction: for example, $$1.4142 = \frac{14142}{10000}$$. No fraction
squares to $$2$$ (:ref{#numbers/sqrt-two}), so no decimal that stops does either. However many
places you take, the square is only close to $$2$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-real-line-integer-part}

For each of $$x = 4.7$$, $$x = -4.7$$ and $$x = -5$$, find the integer $$n$$ with
$$n \le x < n + 1$$ from rule 2 of :ref{#no-gaps}. Is $$n$$ always the part of $$x$$ before the
decimal point?

:::solution

- $$x = 4.7$$: $$n = 4$$, since $$4 \le 4.7 < 5$$.
- $$x = -4.7$$: $$n = -5$$, since $$-5 \le -4.7 < -4$$. The integer $$-4$$ doesn't work, because
  $$-4.7$$ is less than $$-4$$.
- $$x = -5$$: $$n = -5$$, since $$-5 \le -5 < -4$$.

So $$n$$ is not always the part before the decimal point. For $$-4.7$$ that part is $$-4$$, but
$$n = -5$$. This is why the rule says it only for numbers that are not negative.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-real-line-point-nine-claim}

A friend argues: "$$0.9$$, $$0.99$$, $$0.999$$ and so on are all less than $$1$$, so $$0.999\ldots$$
is less than $$1$$ too." What is wrong with the argument?

:::solution

Each decimal that stops is indeed less than $$1$$, but $$0.999\ldots$$ is none of them. It is the
number that all of them approach, and it is at least as big as every one of them.

If $$0.999\ldots$$ were less than $$1$$, the gap $$1 - 0.999\ldots$$ would be a positive number
smaller than $$0.1$$, smaller than $$0.01$$, smaller than $$0.001$$, and so on forever. No positive
real number is that small, so there is no gap, and $$0.999\ldots = 1$$. The careful version of this
argument is :ref{#point-nine}.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-real-line-right-distributive}

Rule 6 of :ref{#real-rules} says $$a(b + c) = ab + ac$$, with the sum on the right. Prove from the
rules that $$(a + b)c = ac + bc$$ as well.

:::solution

Using rule 1 to swap the factors, then rule 6, then rule 1 again on each product:

```math
(a + b)c = c(a + b) = ca + cb = ac + bc
```

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-real-line-five-equation}

In the arithmetic with the five numbers $$0, 1, 2, 3, 4$$, find $$-4$$ and $$\frac{1}{3}$$. Then
solve $$3x + 4 = 2$$, and check your answer.

:::solution

Since $$4 + 1 = 5$$ leaves remainder $$0$$, here $$-4$$ is $$1$$. Since $$3 \cdot 2 = 6$$ leaves
remainder $$1$$, here $$\frac{1}{3}$$ is $$2$$.

The usual steps work, because they only use the rules of arithmetic. Adding $$-4$$, which is $$1$$,
to both sides gives $$3x = 2 + 1 = 3$$. Multiplying both sides by $$\frac{1}{3}$$, which is $$2$$,
gives $$x = 3 \cdot 2 = 6$$, which leaves remainder $$1$$. So $$x = 1$$.

**Check:** $$3 \cdot 1 + 4 = 7$$, which leaves remainder $$2$$.

:::
