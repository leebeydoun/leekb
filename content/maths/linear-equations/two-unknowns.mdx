Many questions have two unknown quantities tied together by two facts. A café sells coffees and
teas: four coffees and two teas cost £14, and one coffee and three teas cost £8.50. What does each
drink cost? Neither fact on its own is enough, but together they pin both prices down. This page
shows how to find them.

## One equation, two unknowns

The café's first fact, four coffees and two teas cost £14, is a single equation tying two unknown
prices together. Each unknown is multiplied by a fixed number, and the results are added: that is
what makes it linear. On its own it doesn't settle the prices. A coffee at £3 and a tea at £1 fits,
and so does a coffee at £2 and a tea at £3.

:::definition[Linear equation in two unknowns]{#linear-two}

A **linear equation** in two unknowns $$x$$ and $$y$$ is one that can be brought to the form

```math
ax + by = c
```

by safe steps (:ref{#safe-steps}), where $$a$$, $$b$$ and $$c$$ are numbers and $$a$$ and $$b$$ are
not both zero. A **solution** is a pair of numbers, one for $$x$$ and one for $$y$$, that makes it
true. We write the pair as $$(x, y)$$, with $$x$$ first.

:::

A single equation like $$x + y = 5$$ has far too many solutions to pin anything down: $$(0, 5)$$,
$$(1, 4)$$, $$(2, 3)$$, $$(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{9}{2})$$, $$(-10, 15)$$ and so on. Any $$x$$ at all
can be chosen, and then $$y = 5 - x$$ is forced. So one equation in two unknowns has infinitely many
solutions.

## Systems of equations

Each equation is a condition the unknowns must meet, and a solution has to meet all of them at once.
So a second equation cuts the possibilities down. Of all the solutions of $$x + y = 5$$, only one
also solves $$x - y = 1$$, namely $$(3, 2)$$, as this page will show.

:::definition[Systems]{#system}

A **system** of equations is a list of equations to be satisfied at the same time. A **solution** of
the system is a choice of values for the unknowns that makes **every** equation in the list true.
Two systems are **equivalent** if they have exactly the same solutions.

:::

Equations in a system are often called **simultaneous equations**, because they must hold
simultaneously. It is usual to number them so they can be referred to:

```math
\begin{aligned}
x + y &= 5 &\quad (1) \\
x - y &= 1 &\quad (2)
\end{aligned}
```

## The idea of elimination

We already know how to solve an equation with one unknown. So the plan is to combine the two
equations in a way that makes one unknown disappear. This is allowed because the equations are facts
about the same two numbers, and equal amounts added to equal amounts give equal totals. So adding
the two equations, side by side, gives a new equation that is also true.

Adding equations (1) and (2), left side to left side and right side to right side, gives

```math
(x + y) + (x - y) = 5 + 1, \qquad\text{that is,}\qquad 2x = 6
```

The $$y$$ has gone, because $$+y$$ and $$-y$$ cancel. What is left is an equation in $$x$$ alone,
which says $$x = 3$$. Putting this back into (1) gives $$3 + y = 5$$, so $$y = 2$$.

Two questions need answering before this can be trusted.

1. **Is it allowed to add two equations?** Yes: if $$(x, y)$$ makes both equations true, then the
   two left sides are the numbers $$5$$ and $$1$$, so their sum is $$6$$.
2. **Could the system have other solutions that this misses, or could $$(3, 2)$$ be a false one?**
   The next proposition shows that neither can happen, as long as each step is reversible.

:::proposition[Combining equations]{#combining}

Take a system of two equations

```math
\begin{aligned}
L_1 &= R_1 &\quad (1) \\
L_2 &= R_2 &\quad (2)
\end{aligned}
```

and a number $$k$$. Replacing equation (2) by "(2) plus $$k$$ times (1)", that is, by

```math
L_2 + kL_1 = R_2 + kR_1
```

and keeping (1) as it is, gives an equivalent system. So does multiplying either equation by a
non-zero number, or swapping the order of the equations.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_elimination#Row_operations"}

We show the two systems have the same solutions, one direction at a time.

**Old solutions solve the new system.** Suppose some values of the unknowns make (1) and (2) true.
Then $$L_1$$ and $$R_1$$ are the same number, so $$kL_1$$ and $$kR_1$$ are equal too. Adding these
equal numbers to the equal numbers $$L_2$$ and $$R_2$$ gives equal results, so
$$L_2 + kL_1 = R_2 + kR_1$$ is true. Equation (1) is unchanged, so it is still true.

**New solutions solve the old system.** Suppose some values make (1) and the new equation true. The
step can be undone by adding $$-k$$ times (1) to the new equation. That is a step of the same kind,
so by the first part it turns true equations into true ones: for those values,

```math
(L_2 + kL_1) - kL_1 = (R_2 + kR_1) - kR_1
```

is true. It simplifies to $$L_2 = R_2$$, so (2) holds, and those values solve the old system.

Multiplying one equation by a non-zero number doesn't change its solutions, by :ref{#safe-steps}, so
it doesn't change the solutions of the system. Swapping the order changes nothing at all, since a
solution must satisfy both equations either way.

:::

Each step of elimination is one of these moves, so it replaces the system by an equivalent one. In
the example, adding (1) to (2) turns the system into $$x + y = 5$$ and $$2x = 6$$, which has exactly
the same solutions. That system has only the solution $$(3, 2)$$, so the original system has only
that solution too. Checking the answer in the original equations is still a good habit, because it
catches arithmetic slips.

## The elimination method

Scaling an equation by a number other than 0 doesn't change what it says. If one coffee and three
teas cost £8.50, then four coffees and twelve teas cost £34: it is the same fact, just for four
times the order. Usually neither unknown cancels straight away. The trick is to multiply the
equations first so that one unknown has matching coefficients.

:::remark[Elimination]{#elimination}

To solve two linear equations in $$x$$ and $$y$$:

1. **Choose** an unknown to eliminate, say $$x$$.
2. **Match** its coefficients: multiply each equation by a non-zero number so that $$x$$ has the
   same coefficient in both, or opposite coefficients.
3. **Eliminate:** subtract the equations if the coefficients are the same, or add them if they are
   opposite. The result is an equation in $$y$$ alone.
4. **Solve** that equation for $$y$$, using :ref{#one-unknown}.
5. **Back-substitute:** put the value of $$y$$ into either original equation and solve for $$x$$.
6. **Check** both values in **both** original equations.

:::

:::example[Eliminating x]

Solve

```math
\begin{aligned}
3x + 2y &= 7 &\quad (1) \\
5x - 3y &= 37 &\quad (2)
\end{aligned}
```

**Match.** The coefficients of $$x$$ are $$3$$ and $$5$$. Multiplying (1) by $$5$$ and (2) by $$3$$
makes both of them $$15$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
15x + 10y &= 35 \\
15x - 9y &= 111
\end{aligned}
```

**Eliminate.** Subtracting the second from the first removes $$x$$:

```math
10y - (-9y) = 35 - 111, \qquad\text{so}\qquad 19y = -76
```

**Solve.** $$y = \frac{-76}{19} = -4$$.

**Back-substitute** into (1): $$3x + 2 \cdot (-4) = 7$$, so $$3x = 15$$ and $$x = 5$$.

**Check.** In (1): $$3 \cdot 5 + 2 \cdot (-4) = 15 - 8 = 7$$. In (2):
$$5 \cdot 5 - 3 \cdot (-4) = 25 + 12 = 37$$. The solution is $$(5, -4)$$.

:::

The same system can be solved by eliminating $$y$$ instead, and it is worth seeing that the answer
doesn't depend on the choice.

:::example[Eliminating y instead]

In the same system, the coefficients of $$y$$ are $$2$$ and $$-3$$. Multiplying (1) by $$3$$ and (2)
by $$2$$ makes them $$6$$ and $$-6$$, which are opposite, so this time we **add**:

```math
\begin{aligned}
9x + 6y &= 21 \\
10x - 6y &= 74
\end{aligned}
\qquad\longrightarrow\qquad 19x = 95
```

So $$x = 5$$, and putting this into (1) gives $$15 + 2y = 7$$, so $$y = -4$$, as before.

:::

Choose whichever unknown makes the numbers smallest. If one coefficient is already a multiple of the
other, only one equation needs multiplying: for $$2x + y = 5$$ and $$6x - 4y = 1$$, multiplying the
first equation by $$3$$ makes both coefficients of $$x$$ equal to $$6$$.

:::example[Fractions in the answer]

Solve

```math
\begin{aligned}
4x + 3y &= 2 &\quad (1) \\
6x - 5y &= 7 &\quad (2)
\end{aligned}
```

Multiplying (1) by $$3$$ and (2) by $$2$$ gives $$12x + 9y = 6$$ and $$12x - 10y = 14$$.
Subtracting, $$19y = -8$$, so $$y = -\frac{8}{19}$$.

Back in (1): $$4x - \frac{24}{19} = 2$$, so $$4x = \frac{38}{19} + \frac{24}{19} = \frac{62}{19}$$
and $$x = \frac{62}{76} = \frac{31}{38}$$.

**Check** in (2): $$6 \cdot \frac{31}{38} = \frac{93}{19}$$ and
$$5 \cdot \left(-\frac{8}{19}\right) = -\frac{40}{19}$$, so
$$6x - 5y = \frac{93}{19} + \frac{40}{19} = \frac{133}{19} = 7$$.

:::

When the answer has fractions, back-substitution gets messy. An alternative is to eliminate a second
time, the other way, to find $$x$$ directly from the original equations. Here, multiplying (1) by
$$5$$ and (2) by $$3$$ and adding gives $$38x = 31$$ straight away.

## Equations with fractions

If the equations themselves contain fractions, clear them first by multiplying each equation by a
common denominator, a safe step by :ref{#combining}.

:::example[Clearing fractions first]

Solve

```math
\begin{aligned}
\tfrac{1}{2}x + \tfrac{1}{3}y &= 4 &\quad (1) \\
\tfrac{1}{4}x - \tfrac{1}{6}y &= 0 &\quad (2)
\end{aligned}
```

Multiplying (1) by $$6$$ and (2) by $$12$$ gives

```math
\begin{aligned}
3x + 2y &= 24 \\
3x - 2y &= 0
\end{aligned}
```

Adding, $$6x = 24$$, so $$x = 4$$. Then $$12 + 2y = 24$$, so $$y = 6$$.

**Check:** $$\frac{4}{2} + \frac{6}{3} = 2 + 2 = 4$$ and $$\frac{4}{4} - \frac{6}{6} = 0$$.

:::

## Back to the café

Let $$c$$ be the price of a coffee and $$t$$ the price of a tea, in pounds. The two facts are

```math
\begin{aligned}
4c + 2t &= 14 &\quad (1) \\
c + 3t &= 8.5 &\quad (2)
\end{aligned}
```

Multiplying (2) by $$4$$ gives $$4c + 12t = 34$$, and subtracting (1) from this gives $$10t = 20$$,
so $$t = 2$$. Then (2) gives $$c = 8.5 - 6 = 2.5$$. A coffee costs £2.50 and a tea £2. Check:
$$4 \cdot 2.5 + 2 \cdot 2 = 14$$ and $$2.5 + 3 \cdot 2 = 8.5$$.

## Common mistakes

- **Multiplying only one side.** Multiplying an equation by $$5$$ means multiplying **every** term
  on both sides, including the right-hand side.
- **Sign slips when subtracting.** Subtracting $$15x - 9y = 111$$ from $$15x + 10y = 35$$ gives
  $$10y - (-9y) = 19y$$, not $$y$$. Writing the brackets in avoids this.
- **Adding when you should subtract.** Same coefficients: subtract. Opposite coefficients: add.
- **Checking in only one equation.** Back-substitution makes the answer satisfy the equation you
  used, so check the **other** one too.
- **Forgetting the second unknown.** Finding $$y$$ is only halfway; the solution is the pair.

## In short

- A linear equation in two unknowns has infinitely many solutions on its own; a system of two
  usually pins down exactly one pair.
- Adding a multiple of one equation to another, multiplying an equation by a non-zero number and
  swapping equations all give equivalent systems.
- Elimination: match the coefficients of one unknown, add or subtract to remove it, solve for the
  other, back-substitute, then check in both equations.
