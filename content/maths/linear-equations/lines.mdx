---
title: Equations as lines
description: Picturing a linear equation as a straight line, and a system as lines that cross.
---

There is a picture behind everything in this guide. Each linear equation in $$x$$ and $$y$$ draws a
straight line, and solving a system means finding where the lines cross. The three cases from the
[previous page](./special-cases.mdx), one solution, none, or infinitely many, become three ways two
lines can sit: crossing once, parallel, or on top of each other.

## Points and coordinates

Draw two number lines at right angles, crossing at their zeros: the **$$x$$-axis** across and the
**$$y$$-axis** up. Every pair of numbers $$(x, y)$$ now names a point: go $$x$$ steps across (left
if $$x$$ is negative) and then $$y$$ steps up (down if $$y$$ is negative). The numbers $$x$$ and
$$y$$ are the point's **coordinates**, and the point where the axes cross, $$(0, 0)$$, is the
**origin**.

A later guide on coordinate geometry builds this up properly. Here we only need the picture.

## The graph of an equation

An equation in two unknowns usually has a whole collection of solutions, each one a pair of numbers.
Since every pair names a point, we can mark all the solutions at once and look at the shape they
make. That shape is the graph.

:::definition[Graph of an equation]{#graph}

The **graph** of an equation in $$x$$ and $$y$$ is the set of all points $$(x, y)$$ whose
coordinates are a solution of the equation.

:::

Take $$2x + y = 4$$. Some of its solutions are $$(0, 4)$$, $$(1, 2)$$ and $$(2, 0)$$, and plotting
them shows they lie in a straight line. So do all the others, like $$(\frac{1}{2}, 3)$$ and
$$(3, -2)$$:

::graph{lines="2x + y = 4" points="0, 4; 1, 2; 2, 0; 3, -2" x="-2 5" y="-3 5"}

:::remark[Linear equations draw lines]{#line}

The graph of a linear equation $$ax + by = c$$ (with $$a$$ and $$b$$ not both zero) is always a
straight line, and every straight line is the graph of such an equation. This is where the name
**linear** comes from. We take it from the picture for now; the guide on coordinate geometry proves
it.

::see-also{src="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_equation#Geometric_interpretation"}

:::

Since two points fix a line, the quickest way to draw one is to find two solutions, usually by
putting $$x = 0$$ and then $$y = 0$$. For $$2x + y = 4$$ these give $$(0, 4)$$ and $$(2, 0)$$, where
the line crosses the axes.

## Systems as crossing lines

A solution of a system makes both equations true, so it is a point on **both** lines: a point where
they cross.

::graph{lines="x + y = 5; x - y = 1" points="3, 2" x="-2 6" y="-2 6"}

Solving by elimination finds the crossing point exactly, even when it is somewhere awkward like
$$(\frac{31}{38}, -\frac{8}{19})$$ that no drawing could read off. The picture explains what is
going on; the algebra gets the answer.

Two lines can sit in three ways, matching the three cases on the previous page.

**Crossing once: exactly one solution.** This is the usual case, as above.

**Parallel: no solution.** The lines $$x + 2y = 3$$ and $$x + 2y = 5$$ never meet, because no point
can have $$x + 2y$$ equal to both $$3$$ and $$5$$.

::graph{lines="x + 2y = 3; x + 2y = 5" x="-3 6" y="-2 4"}

**The same line: infinitely many solutions.** The equations $$x + 2y = 3$$ and $$3x + 6y = 9$$
describe the same line, since the second is the first multiplied by $$3$$. Every point on it is a
solution, which is why there are infinitely many.

::graph{lines="x + 2y = 3; 3x + 6y = 9" points="3, 0; 1, 1; -1, 2" x="-3 6" y="-2 4"}

## Slope

How steeply a line climbs is measured by a single number. Walk along a straight line from left to
right. Because it is straight, each step of $$1$$ to the right takes you up, or down, by the same
amount, wherever you start. That fixed amount is the slope. For example, a line that climbs $$3$$
for every step right has slope $$3$$, and one that drops $$2$$ has slope $$-2$$.

<SlopeExplorer />

:::definition[Slope of a line]{#slope}

If $$b \neq 0$$, the equation $$ax + by = c$$ can be rearranged as

```math
y = mx + k, \qquad\text{where}\quad m = -\frac{a}{b}, \quad k = \frac{c}{b}
```

The number $$m$$ is the **slope** of the line: each step of $$1$$ to the right changes $$y$$ by
$$m$$. The number $$k$$ is where the line crosses the $$y$$-axis, since $$x = 0$$ gives $$y = k$$.

:::

For $$2x + y = 4$$, rearranging gives $$y = -2x + 4$$: the slope is $$-2$$, so the line drops by
$$2$$ for each step right, and it crosses the $$y$$-axis at $$4$$, as in the first picture. When
$$b = 0$$ the equation is $$ax = c$$, which says $$x = \frac{c}{a}$$: a vertical line, which has no
slope.

Two different lines are parallel exactly when they have the same slope (or are both vertical), and
this is what the determinant detects.

:::proposition[Zero determinant means parallel or equal]{#parallel-test}

Take two lines $$ax + by = u$$ and $$cx + dy = v$$, neither vertical, so $$b \neq 0$$ and
$$d \neq 0$$. They have the same slope exactly when $$ad - bc = 0$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slope#Algebra_and_geometry"}

The slopes are $$-\frac{a}{b}$$ and $$-\frac{c}{d}$$. Multiplying both by $$-1$$, a safe step
(:ref{#safe-steps}), shows they are equal exactly when $$\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d}$$. Since $$b$$
and $$d$$ are not zero, cross-multiplying (:ref{#numbers/cross-multiplying-rational}) shows that
this happens exactly when $$ad = bc$$, that is, when $$ad - bc = 0$$.

:::

For example, $$x + 2y = 3$$ and $$-2x - 4y = 7$$ both have slope $$-\frac{1}{2}$$, and their
determinant is $$1 \cdot (-4) - 2 \cdot (-2) = 0$$.

The proposition leaves out vertical lines, but they fit the same pattern. If one line is vertical
and the other isn't, say $$b = 0$$ and $$d \neq 0$$, then $$a \neq 0$$ (since $$a$$ and $$b$$ are
not both zero), so $$ad - bc = ad$$ is a product of non-zero numbers, which is not zero: the lines
cross once, as they should. If both are vertical, $$b = d = 0$$ and $$ad - bc = 0$$: they are
parallel or the same. So in every case, a zero determinant means the lines point the same way, and a
non-zero one means they cross exactly once, matching :ref{#determinant-test} and
:ref{#determinant-zero}.

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on equations as lines, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-lines-on-graph}

Which of the points $$(1, 1)$$, $$(3, -1)$$ and $$(-2, 6)$$ lie on the graph of $$2x + y = 3$$?
Where does the line cross each axis?

:::solution

A point is on the graph exactly when its coordinates solve the equation (:ref{#graph}):

- $$(1, 1)$$: $$2 + 1 = 3$$, so it is on the line
- $$(3, -1)$$: $$6 - 1 = 5$$, so it isn't
- $$(-2, 6)$$: $$-4 + 6 = 2$$, so it isn't

Putting $$x = 0$$ gives $$y = 3$$, so the line crosses the $$y$$-axis at $$(0, 3)$$. Putting
$$y = 0$$ gives $$2x = 3$$, so it crosses the $$x$$-axis at $$(\frac{3}{2}, 0)$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-lines-three-slopes}

Find the slope of each line and where it crosses the $$y$$-axis, or explain why it has no slope.

1. $$4x - 2y = 6$$
2. $$3y = 6$$
3. $$x = 4$$

:::solution

1. Rearranging, $$2y = 4x - 6$$, so $$y = 2x - 3$$. The slope is $$2$$ and the line crosses the
   $$y$$-axis at $$-3$$.
2. Here $$a = 0$$ and $$b = 3$$, and the equation says $$y = 2$$. The slope is $$0$$: a step to the
   right doesn't change $$y$$. The line is level and crosses the $$y$$-axis at $$2$$.
3. Here $$b = 0$$, so the equation can't be written as $$y = mx + k$$ (:ref{#slope}). The line is
   vertical and has no slope. It never crosses the $$y$$-axis, since every point on it has
   $$x = 4$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-lines-compare}

Is each line the same as $$y = 3x - 1$$, parallel to it, or crossing it? If they cross, find where.

1. $$6x - 2y = 2$$
2. $$6x - 2y = 5$$
3. $$x + 3y = 7$$

:::solution

1. Rearranging, $$2y = 6x - 2$$, so $$y = 3x - 1$$: the **same line**.
2. Rearranging, $$y = 3x - \frac{5}{2}$$. The slope is again $$3$$ but it crosses the $$y$$-axis
   somewhere else, so the lines are **parallel**, and the system has no solution.
3. The slope is $$-\frac{1}{3}$$, not $$3$$, so the lines **cross** once. Substituting
   $$y = 3x - 1$$ gives $$x + 9x - 3 = 7$$, so $$x = 1$$ and $$y = 2$$. They cross at $$(1, 2)$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-lines-through-two-points}

Find the numbers $$m$$ and $$k$$ for which the line $$y = mx + k$$ passes through $$(1, 2)$$ and
$$(3, 8)$$.

:::solution

Each point gives an equation in the unknowns $$m$$ and $$k$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
m + k &= 2 &\quad (1) \\
3m + k &= 8 &\quad (2)
\end{aligned}
```

Subtracting (1) from (2), $$2m = 6$$, so $$m = 3$$, and then $$k = -1$$. The line is $$y = 3x - 1$$.
**Check:** $$3 - 1 = 2$$ and $$9 - 1 = 8$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-lines-why-slope}

For the line $$y = mx + k$$, show that moving $$1$$ to the right changes $$y$$ by $$m$$, wherever
you start. How much does moving $$h$$ to the right change $$y$$?

:::solution

At $$x$$, the line is at height $$mx + k$$. At $$x + 1$$ it is at $$m(x + 1) + k = mx + m + k$$. The
difference is

```math
(mx + m + k) - (mx + k) = m
```

which doesn't depend on $$x$$. In the same way, at $$x + h$$ the height is $$mx + mh + k$$, so
moving $$h$$ to the right changes $$y$$ by $$mh$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-lines-triangle}

The lines $$y = x$$, $$y = -x + 4$$ and $$y = 0$$ form a triangle. Find its three corners.

:::solution

Each corner is where two of the lines cross, so solve each pair as a system:

- $$y = x$$ and $$y = -x + 4$$: then $$x = -x + 4$$, so $$x = 2$$ and the corner is $$(2, 2)$$
- $$y = x$$ and $$y = 0$$: the corner is $$(0, 0)$$
- $$y = -x + 4$$ and $$y = 0$$: then $$x = 4$$, and the corner is $$(4, 0)$$

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-lines-vertical-crossing}

Show that the system $$x = 2$$, $$cx + dy = v$$ with $$d \neq 0$$ always has exactly one solution.
Explain what this means in the picture.

:::solution

The first equation is $$1 \cdot x + 0 \cdot y = 2$$, so the determinant is
$$1 \cdot d - 0 \cdot c = d$$, which is not zero. So there is exactly one solution
(:ref{#determinant-test}): $$x = 2$$ and $$y = \frac{v - 2c}{d}$$.

In the picture, $$x = 2$$ is a vertical line, and $$cx + dy = v$$ with $$d \neq 0$$ isn't vertical.
A vertical line and a line that isn't vertical point different ways, so they cross exactly once.

:::
