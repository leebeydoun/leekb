title: Three equations in three unknowns
description: Reducing three equations in three unknowns to two, and what can go wrong.
---

With three unknown quantities, three facts are needed to pin them down. The good news is that no new
idea is needed: eliminating one unknown turns three equations in three unknowns into two equations
in two unknowns, which the earlier pages already solve. This page shows how to organise the work so
it stays manageable.

## Linear equations in three unknowns

:::definition[Linear equation in three unknowns]{#linear-three}

A **linear equation** in three unknowns $$x$$, $$y$$ and $$z$$ is one that can be brought to the
form

```math
ax + by + cz = d
```

where $$a$$, $$b$$, $$c$$ and $$d$$ are numbers and $$a$$, $$b$$ and $$c$$ are not all zero. A
**solution** is a triple of numbers $$(x, y, z)$$ that makes it true.

:::

Everything said about systems on the [Two equations in two unknowns](./two-unknowns.mdx) page works
just as well here. In particular, :ref{#combining} holds for any number of equations, with the same
proof: adding a multiple of one equation to another, multiplying an equation by a non-zero number,
or reordering the equations always gives an equivalent system.

## The method

:::remark[Solving three equations]{#three-method}

1. **Choose** an unknown to eliminate first, ideally one with simple coefficients, such as $$1$$ or
   $$-1$$.
2. **Eliminate it twice:** combine two of the equations to remove it, then combine a **different**
   pair (one of them can be reused) to remove it again. This gives two equations in the other two
   unknowns.
3. **Solve** those two equations, by elimination or substitution.
4. **Back-substitute** both values into an original equation that contains the third unknown, and
   solve for it.
5. **Check** all three values in all three original equations.

:::

:::example[A first example]

Solve

```math
\begin{aligned}
x + y + z &= 6 &\quad (1) \\
2x - y + z &= 3 &\quad (2) \\
x + 2y - z &= 2 &\quad (3)
\end{aligned}
```

**Choose.** The coefficients of $$z$$ are $$1$$, $$1$$ and $$-1$$, so $$z$$ is easiest to remove.

**Eliminate twice.** Adding (1) and (3) removes $$z$$, and so does adding (2) and (3):

```math
\begin{aligned}
2x + 3y &= 8 &\quad (4) \\
3x + y &= 5 &\quad (5)
\end{aligned}
```

**Solve.** From (5), $$y = 5 - 3x$$. Substituting into (4), $$2x + 15 - 9x = 8$$, so $$-7x = -7$$
and $$x = 1$$. Then $$y = 5 - 3 = 2$$.

**Back-substitute** into (1): $$1 + 2 + z = 6$$, so $$z = 3$$.

**Check.** (1): $$1 + 2 + 3 = 6$$. (2): $$2 - 2 + 3 = 3$$. (3): $$1 + 4 - 3 = 2$$. The solution is
$$(1, 2, 3)$$.

:::

Why is it fine to set two of the original equations aside? Take the first example. The new equations
are (4), which is (1) plus (3), and (5), which is (2) plus (3), and (1) is kept for
back-substitution. Nothing is lost, because the originals can be got back: (3) is (4) minus (1), and
then (2) is (5) minus (3). So by :ref{#combining}, the system (1), (4), (5) has exactly the same
solutions as (1), (2), (3).

The same works in general. Each new equation is made from a pair with non-zero multipliers, so
together with one equation of its pair it gives back the other. As long as the two pairs are
different, the originals can be recovered one after another, starting from the equation kept for
back-substitution.

Step 2 is where mistakes happen. Both new equations must be free of the **same** unknown, and they
must come from different pairs. Combining (1) and (3) twice in different ways would only give the
same information twice.

:::example[A fractional answer]

Solve

```math
\begin{aligned}
2x + 3y - z &= 5 &\quad (1) \\
x - y + 2z &= 7 &\quad (2) \\
3x + y + z &= 10 &\quad (3)
\end{aligned}
```

**Eliminate $$z$$ twice.** Twice (1) plus (2), and (1) plus (3):

```math
\begin{aligned}
5x + 5y &= 17 &\quad (4) \\
5x + 4y &= 15 &\quad (5)
\end{aligned}
```

**Solve.** Subtracting (5) from (4), $$y = 2$$. Then (5) gives $$5x = 7$$, so $$x = \frac{7}{5}$$.

**Back-substitute.** Rearranging (1), $$z = 2x + 3y - 5 = \frac{14}{5} + 6 - 5 = \frac{19}{5}$$.

**Check.** (2): $$\frac{7}{5} - 2 + \frac{38}{5} = 9 - 2 = 7$$. (3):
$$\frac{21}{5} + 2 + \frac{19}{5} = 8 + 2 = 10$$. (1) holds by the way $$z$$ was found. The solution
is $$(\frac{7}{5}, 2, \frac{19}{5})$$.

:::

## Clearing fractions first

With fractions in the coefficients, multiply each equation by a common denominator before doing
anything else. Each equation can use its own multiplier.

:::example[Fractions in the equations]

Solve

```math
\begin{aligned}
\tfrac{1}{2}x + \tfrac{1}{3}y + \tfrac{1}{6}z &= 3 &\quad (1) \\
x - \tfrac{1}{3}y + \tfrac{1}{2}z &= 4 &\quad (2) \\
\tfrac{1}{4}x + \tfrac{1}{3}y - \tfrac{1}{6}z &= \tfrac{1}{2} &\quad (3)
\end{aligned}
```

Multiplying (1) and (2) by $$6$$ and (3) by $$12$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
3x + 2y + z &= 18 &\quad (4) \\
6x - 2y + 3z &= 24 &\quad (5) \\
3x + 4y - 2z &= 6 &\quad (6)
\end{aligned}
```

**Eliminate $$y$$ twice.** (4) plus (5) gives $$9x + 4z = 42$$. Twice (4) minus (6) gives
$$3x + 4z = 30$$.

**Solve.** Subtracting the second from the first, $$6x = 12$$, so $$x = 2$$, and then
$$4z = 30 - 6 = 24$$, so $$z = 6$$.

**Back-substitute** into (4): $$6 + 2y + 6 = 18$$, so $$y = 3$$.

**Check** in the **original** equations. (1): $$1 + 1 + 1 = 3$$. (2): $$2 - 1 + 3 = 4$$. (3):
$$\frac{1}{2} + 1 - 1 = \frac{1}{2}$$. The solution is $$(2, 3, 6)$$.

:::

## No solution, or infinitely many

Just as with two unknowns, a system of three can have no solution or infinitely many, and
elimination shows it the same way: a line like $$0 = 3$$ means none, and a line like $$0 = 0$$ means
one equation was saying nothing new.

:::example[No solution]

In

```math
\begin{aligned}
x + y + z &= 2 &\quad (1) \\
x - y + z &= 0 &\quad (2) \\
2x + 2z &= 5 &\quad (3)
\end{aligned}
```

adding (1) and (2) gives $$2x + 2z = 2$$. Subtracting this from (3) gives $$0 = 3$$, so there is no
solution: (1) and (2) together force $$2x + 2z$$ to be $$2$$, but (3) says it is $$5$$.

:::

:::example[Infinitely many solutions]

Change the last equation to $$2x + 2z = 2$$. Now subtracting gives $$0 = 0$$: equation (3) is just
(1) plus (2), and says nothing new. The system has the same solutions as (1) and (2) alone.
Subtracting (2) from (1) gives $$2y = 2$$, so $$y = 1$$, and then $$x + z = 1$$. Choosing any number
$$t$$ for $$z$$, the solutions are

```math
(1 - t,\ 1,\ t) \qquad\text{for every number } t
```

:::

There is a determinant for three equations too, which decides in advance whether there is exactly
one solution. It is a longer formula, and it belongs to a later guide on matrices.

## Common mistakes

- **Eliminating different unknowns in step 2.** If one new equation has no $$z$$ and the other no
  $$y$$, you still have three unknowns between them. Remove the **same** unknown both times.
- **Reusing the same pair.** Two combinations of (1) and (3) alone can't replace the information in
  (2). Each original equation must be used at least once.
- **Checking in the cleared equations only.** A slip while clearing fractions won't show up there.
  Check in the original equations.
- **Losing track of which equation is which.** Number every new equation, and say how it was made.

## In short

- Three unknowns need three equations. Eliminate the same unknown from two different pairs, to get
  two equations in two unknowns.
- Solve those, back-substitute to find the third unknown, and check in all three original equations.
- Clear fractions first. A line like $$0 = 3$$ means no solution; $$0 = 0$$ means an equation was
  redundant, and there may be infinitely many.
