title: Three equations in three unknowns
description: Reducing three equations in three unknowns to two, and what can go wrong.
---

With three unknown quantities, three facts are needed to pin them down. The good news is that no new
idea is needed: eliminating one unknown turns three equations in three unknowns into two equations
in two unknowns, which the earlier pages already solve. This page shows how to organise the work so
it stays manageable.

## Linear equations in three unknowns

Nothing changes except the number of unknowns. If a café also sells cake, a fact like "two coffees,
a tea and a cake cost £9" is a linear equation in three unknowns: each price is multiplied by a
fixed number, and the results are added. One such fact leaves a lot of freedom, and it takes three
of them to pin down three prices, when they can be pinned down at all.

:::definition[Linear equation in three unknowns]{#linear-three}

A **linear equation** in three unknowns $$x$$, $$y$$ and $$z$$ is one that can be brought to the
form

```math
ax + by + cz = d
```

where $$a$$, $$b$$, $$c$$ and $$d$$ are numbers and $$a$$, $$b$$ and $$c$$ are not all zero. A
**solution** is a triple of numbers $$(x, y, z)$$ that makes it true.

:::

Everything said about systems on the [Two equations in two unknowns](./two-unknowns.mdx) page works
equally well here. In particular, combining equations works for any number of them
(:ref{#combining}), with the same proof: adding a multiple of one equation to another, multiplying
an equation by a non-zero number, or reordering the equations always gives an equivalent system.

## The method

The plan is to peel off one unknown at a time. Getting rid of one unknown turns the problem into two
equations in two unknowns, which we already know how to solve. For that we need two new equations,
both free of the same unknown. Each should be built from a different pair of the original equations,
so that together with one original kept aside, they still carry everything the three originals said.

:::remark[Solving three equations in three unknowns]{#three-method}

1. **Choose** an unknown to eliminate first, ideally one with simple coefficients, such as $$1$$ or
   $$-1$$.
2. **Eliminate it twice:** combine two of the equations to remove it, then combine a **different**
   pair (one of them can be reused) to remove it again. This gives two equations in the other two
   unknowns.
3. **Solve** those two equations, by elimination or substitution.
4. **Back-substitute** both values into an original equation that contains the third unknown, and
   solve for it.
5. **Check** all three values in all three original equations.

:::

:::example[A first example]

Solve

```math
\begin{aligned}
x + y + z &= 6 &\quad (1) \\
2x - y + z &= 3 &\quad (2) \\
x + 2y - z &= 2 &\quad (3)
\end{aligned}
```

**Choose.** The coefficients of $$z$$ are $$1$$, $$1$$ and $$-1$$, so $$z$$ is easiest to remove.

**Eliminate twice.** Adding (1) and (3) removes $$z$$, and so does adding (2) and (3):

```math
\begin{aligned}
2x + 3y &= 8 &\quad (4) \\
3x + y &= 5 &\quad (5)
\end{aligned}
```

**Solve.** From (5), $$y = 5 - 3x$$. Substituting into (4), $$2x + 15 - 9x = 8$$, so $$-7x = -7$$
and $$x = 1$$. Then $$y = 5 - 3 = 2$$.

**Back-substitute** into (1): $$1 + 2 + z = 6$$, so $$z = 3$$.

**Check.** (1): $$1 + 2 + 3 = 6$$. (2): $$2 - 2 + 3 = 3$$. (3): $$1 + 4 - 3 = 2$$. The solution is
$$(1, 2, 3)$$.

:::

Why is it fine to set two of the original equations aside? Take the first example. The new equations
are (4), which is (1) plus (3), and (5), which is (2) plus (3), and (1) is kept for
back-substitution. Nothing is lost, because the originals can be got back: (3) is (4) minus (1), and
then (2) is (5) minus (3). So by :ref{#combining}, the system (1), (4), (5) has exactly the same
solutions as (1), (2), (3).

The same works in general. Each new equation is made from a pair with non-zero multipliers, so
together with one equation of its pair it gives back the other. As long as the two pairs are
different, the originals can be recovered one after another, starting from the equation kept for
back-substitution.

Step 2 is where mistakes happen. Both new equations must be free of the **same** unknown, and they
must come from different pairs. Combining (1) and (3) twice in different ways would only give the
same information twice.

:::example[A fractional answer]

Solve

```math
\begin{aligned}
2x + 3y - z &= 5 &\quad (1) \\
x - y + 2z &= 7 &\quad (2) \\
3x + y + z &= 10 &\quad (3)
\end{aligned}
```

**Eliminate $$z$$ twice.** Twice (1) plus (2), and (1) plus (3):

```math
\begin{aligned}
5x + 5y &= 17 &\quad (4) \\
5x + 4y &= 15 &\quad (5)
\end{aligned}
```

**Solve.** Subtracting (5) from (4), $$y = 2$$. Then (5) gives $$5x = 7$$, so $$x = \frac{7}{5}$$.

**Back-substitute.** Rearranging (1), $$z = 2x + 3y - 5 = \frac{14}{5} + 6 - 5 = \frac{19}{5}$$.

**Check.** (2): $$\frac{7}{5} - 2 + \frac{38}{5} = 9 - 2 = 7$$. (3):
$$\frac{21}{5} + 2 + \frac{19}{5} = 8 + 2 = 10$$. (1) holds by the way $$z$$ was found. The solution
is $$(\frac{7}{5}, 2, \frac{19}{5})$$.

:::

## Clearing fractions first

With fractions in the coefficients, multiply each equation by a common denominator before doing
anything else. Each equation can use its own multiplier.

:::example[Fractions in the equations]

Solve

```math
\begin{aligned}
\tfrac{1}{2}x + \tfrac{1}{3}y + \tfrac{1}{6}z &= 3 &\quad (1) \\
x - \tfrac{1}{3}y + \tfrac{1}{2}z &= 4 &\quad (2) \\
\tfrac{1}{4}x + \tfrac{1}{3}y - \tfrac{1}{6}z &= \tfrac{1}{2} &\quad (3)
\end{aligned}
```

Multiplying (1) and (2) by $$6$$ and (3) by $$12$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
3x + 2y + z &= 18 &\quad (4) \\
6x - 2y + 3z &= 24 &\quad (5) \\
3x + 4y - 2z &= 6 &\quad (6)
\end{aligned}
```

**Eliminate $$y$$ twice.** (4) plus (5) gives $$9x + 4z = 42$$. Twice (4) minus (6) gives
$$3x + 4z = 30$$.

**Solve.** Subtracting the second from the first, $$6x = 12$$, so $$x = 2$$, and then
$$4z = 30 - 6 = 24$$, so $$z = 6$$.

**Back-substitute** into (4): $$6 + 2y + 6 = 18$$, so $$y = 3$$.

**Check** in the **original** equations. (1): $$1 + 1 + 1 = 3$$. (2): $$2 - 1 + 3 = 4$$. (3):
$$\frac{1}{2} + 1 - 1 = \frac{1}{2}$$. The solution is $$(2, 3, 6)$$.

:::

## No solution, or infinitely many

Just as with two unknowns, a system of three can have no solution or infinitely many, and
elimination shows it the same way: a line like $$0 = 3$$ means none, and a line like $$0 = 0$$ means
one equation was saying nothing new.

:::example[No solution]

In

```math
\begin{aligned}
x + y + z &= 2 &\quad (1) \\
x - y + z &= 0 &\quad (2) \\
2x + 2z &= 5 &\quad (3)
\end{aligned}
```

adding (1) and (2) gives $$2x + 2z = 2$$. Subtracting this from (3) gives $$0 = 3$$, so there is no
solution: (1) and (2) together force $$2x + 2z$$ to be $$2$$, but (3) says it is $$5$$.

:::

:::example[Infinitely many solutions]

Change the last equation to $$2x + 2z = 2$$. Now subtracting gives $$0 = 0$$: equation (3) is just
(1) plus (2), and says nothing new. The system has the same solutions as (1) and (2) alone.
Subtracting (2) from (1) gives $$2y = 2$$, so $$y = 1$$, and then $$x + z = 1$$. Choosing any number
$$t$$ for $$z$$, the solutions are

```math
(1 - t,\ 1,\ t) \qquad\text{for every number } t
```

:::

There is a determinant for three equations too, which decides in advance whether there is exactly
one solution. It is a longer formula, and it belongs to a later guide on matrices.

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on three equations in three unknowns, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-three-unknowns-check}

Is $$(1, -1, 2)$$ a solution of this system? Is $$(0, 0, 2)$$?

```math
\begin{aligned}
x + y + z &= 2 \\
2x - y + z &= 5 \\
x + 3y - z &= -4
\end{aligned}
```

:::solution

For $$(1, -1, 2)$$: $$1 - 1 + 2 = 2$$, $$2 + 1 + 2 = 5$$ and $$1 - 3 - 2 = -4$$. All three are true,
so it is a solution.

For $$(0, 0, 2)$$: the first equation holds, since $$0 + 0 + 2 = 2$$, but the second gives
$$0 - 0 + 2 = 2$$, not $$5$$. A solution of a system must make **every** equation true, so it isn't
a solution.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-three-unknowns-staircase}

Solve $$z = 2$$, $$y + 3z = 5$$ and $$x - y + z = 4$$.

:::solution

These equations are already in the shape elimination aims for: each one brings in one new unknown.
So back-substitution alone solves them. The first gives $$z = 2$$. The second gives
$$y = 5 - 6 = -1$$. The third gives $$x = 4 + y - z = 4 - 1 - 2 = 1$$.

**Check:** $$-1 + 6 = 5$$ and $$1 + 1 + 2 = 4$$. The solution is $$(1, -1, 2)$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-three-unknowns-solve}

Solve

```math
\begin{aligned}
x + 2y - z &= 3 &\quad (1) \\
2x - y + 3z &= 6 &\quad (2) \\
3x + y + z &= 8 &\quad (3)
\end{aligned}
```

:::solution

**Eliminate $$z$$ twice.** Adding (1) and (3) gives $$4x + 3y = 11$$. Three times (1) plus (2) gives
$$5x + 5y = 15$$, that is, $$x + y = 3$$.

**Solve.** From $$x + y = 3$$, $$y = 3 - x$$. Substituting, $$4x + 9 - 3x = 11$$, so $$x = 2$$ and
$$y = 1$$.

**Back-substitute** into (1): $$2 + 2 - z = 3$$, so $$z = 1$$.

**Check.** (2): $$4 - 1 + 3 = 6$$. (3): $$6 + 1 + 1 = 8$$. The solution is $$(2, 1, 1)$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-three-unknowns-same-pair}

A student solving

```math
\begin{aligned}
x + y + z &= 6 &\quad (1) \\
x - y + z &= 2 &\quad (2) \\
2x + y - z &= 1 &\quad (3)
\end{aligned}
```

eliminates $$y$$ with (1) plus (2), getting $$2x + 2z = 8$$, and then with twice (1) plus twice (2),
getting $$4x + 4z = 16$$. Why can't they solve these two equations for $$x$$ and $$z$$? Solve the
system correctly.

:::solution

Both new equations come from the same pair, (1) and (2), and the second is twice the first. So they
say the same thing, $$x + z = 4$$, and give infinitely many pairs $$(x, z)$$. Equation (3) was never
used, so its information is missing. Step 2 of :ref{#three-method} asks for a **different** pair the
second time.

Correctly: (1) plus (2) gives $$x + z = 4$$, and (2) plus (3) gives $$3x = 3$$, so $$x = 1$$. Then
$$z = 3$$, and (1) gives $$y = 6 - 1 - 3 = 2$$.

**Check.** (2): $$1 - 2 + 3 = 2$$. (3): $$2 + 2 - 3 = 1$$. The solution is $$(1, 2, 3)$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-three-unknowns-numbers}

Three numbers add up to $$24$$. The first is twice the second, and the third is $$4$$ more than the
first. Find the numbers.

:::solution

Call the numbers $$x$$, $$y$$ and $$z$$. The facts are $$x + y + z = 24$$, $$x = 2y$$ and
$$z = x + 4$$. Substituting, $$z = 2y + 4$$, and then

```math
2y + y + (2y + 4) = 24, \qquad\text{so}\qquad 5y = 20
```

So $$y = 4$$, $$x = 8$$ and $$z = 12$$. **Check:** $$8 + 4 + 12 = 24$$, $$8 = 2 \cdot 4$$ and
$$12 = 8 + 4$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-three-unknowns-redundant}

Show that this system has infinitely many solutions, and describe them all:

```math
\begin{aligned}
x + y + z &= 3 &\quad (1) \\
x - y &= 1 &\quad (2) \\
2x + z &= 4 &\quad (3)
\end{aligned}
```

:::solution

Adding (1) and (2) gives $$2x + z = 4$$, which is (3). So (3) says nothing new, and the system has
the same solutions as (1) and (2) alone (:ref{#combining}).

From (2), $$y = x - 1$$. Putting this into (1), $$x + x - 1 + z = 3$$, so $$z = 4 - 2x$$. Choosing
any number $$t$$ for $$x$$, the solutions are

```math
(t,\ t - 1,\ 4 - 2t) \qquad\text{for every number } t
```

For example, $$t = 0$$ gives $$(0, -1, 4)$$: **check** $$0 - 1 + 4 = 3$$, $$0 + 1 = 1$$ and
$$0 + 4 = 4$$.

:::
