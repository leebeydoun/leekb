Imagine standing in a dark room and shining a torch straight at a wall. The patch of light is a
round disc. Now tilt the torch upwards, a little at a time. The patch stretches into an oval, and
the oval grows longer the more you tilt. Tilt until the top edge of the beam runs parallel to the
wall, and the patch no longer closes up: it runs up the wall without end. Tilt further and its edge
opens out even more widely.

The beam of light is a cone, and the wall slices through it. The edges of the patches are the curves
of this chapter: a circle, then ellipses, then a parabola at the moment the beam's edge is parallel
to the wall, and then a hyperbola. In this guide we'll prove that slicing a cone gives exactly these
curves, and then find one rule, with a single number $$e$$, that draws all three.

## A cone in space

Picture two ice-cream cones balanced tip to tip, with their axes on one upright line, and imagine
each one going on upwards or downwards forever. That is a **double cone**. Cut it across at any
height and the cut is a circle, which grows as you move away from the tip. In the cone we'll use,
the radius of that circle equals the height above or below the tip.

Put the tip at the origin $$O$$ of space (:ref{#coordinates/coordinate-space}) and the axis along
the $$z$$-axis. At height $$z$$ the cut is the circle of radius $$\lvert z \rvert$$ around the axis,
and by :ref{#coordinates/circle-equation} its points are the $$(x, y, z)$$ with $$x^2 + y^2 = z^2$$.

:::definition[The double cone]{#cone}

The **double cone** is the set of all points $$(x, y, z)$$ of space with

```math
x^2 + y^2 = z^2
```

The points with $$z \ge 0$$ form the **upper half** and the points with $$z \le 0$$ the **lower
half**. The two halves meet at the **tip** $$O = (0, 0, 0)$$.

:::

As a check, the point $$(3, 4, 5)$$ is on the cone, since $$9 + 16 = 25$$, and so is $$(3, 4, -5)$$
on the lower half. The point $$(1, 1, 1)$$ is not, since $$1 + 1 = 2$$ and $$1^2 = 1$$.

The torch beam is one half of a double cone, with the bulb at the tip. In the cone of the
definition, each side line climbs one unit for each unit it moves away from the axis: the points
$$(x, 0, x)$$ make a line of slope $$1$$, at $$45^\circ$$ to the floor. The pictures below cut the
cone with three tilted planes, and each cut is one of the curves we have met.

## Laying a tilted plane flat

A tilted sheet of card can be lifted off and laid flat on the table without bending or stretching
it. A curve drawn on the card keeps its shape when you do that. So to see what shape a slice of the
cone is, we lay the slicing plane flat, and read off the curve in ordinary coordinates of the plane
$$\mathbb{R}^2$$.

The planes we slice with are tilted like a ramp: they climb as you move in the $$x$$ direction and
stay level in the $$y$$ direction. For a number $$m$$, the points $$(x, y, z)$$ with $$z = mx + 1$$
make the **plane** $$z = mx + 1$$. It passes $$1$$ unit above the tip, and it climbs $$m$$ units for
each unit along $$x$$, so $$m$$ is its slope, as for a line.

Distances on a ramp are longer than on the floor below it. Take $$m = \frac{1}{2}$$ and the two
points $$P = (0, 0, 1)$$ and $$Q = (2, 0, 2)$$ of the plane. On the floor they are $$2$$ apart
across, but by :ref{#coordinates/space-distance}

```math
d(P, Q) = \sqrt{2^2 + 0^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{5} = 2 \cdot \frac{\sqrt5}{2}
```

So every step along $$x$$ on this ramp is $$\frac{\sqrt5}{2} \approx 1.12$$ times as long as its
shadow on the floor. In general the factor is $$\sqrt{1 + m^2}$$, and measuring with it gives
coordinates on the plane that keep distances.

:::lemma[Flat coordinates on a tilted plane]{#slicing-a-cone-flat-coordinates}

Let $$m$$ be a number and $$k = \sqrt{1 + m^2}$$. Give each point $$(x, y, mx + 1)$$ of the plane
$$z = mx + 1$$ the **flat coordinates**

```math
u = kx \qquad v = y
```

Then:

1. **One point for each pair**: each pair $$(u, v)$$ is the flat coordinates of exactly one point of
   the plane, namely $$\left(\frac{u}{k}, v, \frac{mu}{k} + 1\right)$$
2. **Distances kept**: for any two points $$P$$ and $$Q$$ of the plane, the distance from $$P$$ to
   $$Q$$ in space equals the distance between their flat coordinates in $$\mathbb{R}^2$$

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_distance#Higher_dimensions"}

**Part 1.** Since $$k > 0$$, the equation $$u = kx$$ has the one solution $$x = \frac{u}{k}$$, and
then $$y = v$$ and $$z = mx + 1 = \frac{mu}{k} + 1$$.

**Part 2.** Let $$P = (x_1, y_1, mx_1 + 1)$$ and $$Q = (x_2, y_2, mx_2 + 1)$$. The difference of
their heights is $$(mx_2 + 1) - (mx_1 + 1) = m(x_2 - x_1)$$. By :ref{#coordinates/space-distance},

```math
\begin{aligned}
d(P, Q)^2 &= (x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2 + m^2(x_2 - x_1)^2 && \text{distance in space} \\
&= (1 + m^2)(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2 && \text{collect the } x \text{ terms} \\
&= (kx_2 - kx_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2 && \text{since } k^2 = 1 + m^2 \\
&= (u_2 - u_1)^2 + (v_2 - v_1)^2 && \text{flat coordinates}
\end{aligned}
```

The last line is the square of the distance between $$(u_1, v_1)$$ and $$(u_2, v_2)$$, by
:ref{#coordinates/distance-formula}. Both distances are not negative, so they are equal.

:::

So a slice of the cone, written in flat coordinates, is a curve in $$\mathbb{R}^2$$ with exactly the
distances of the slice itself. From here on, "the slice is an ellipse" means that in flat
coordinates it is the graph of an ellipse (:ref{#ellipse-definition}), and the same for the other
curves.

## Three slices with numbers

**Less steep than the side.** Take $$m = \frac{1}{2}$$. A point $$(x, y, \frac{x}{2} + 1)$$ of the
plane is on the cone exactly when $$x^2 + y^2 = \left(\frac{x}{2} + 1\right)^2$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
x^2 + y^2 &= \tfrac{1}{4}x^2 + x + 1 && \text{expand the square} \\
\tfrac{3}{4}x^2 - x + y^2 &= 1 && \text{take } \tfrac{1}{4}x^2 + x \text{ from both sides} \\
\tfrac{3}{4}\left(x - \tfrac{2}{3}\right)^2 - \tfrac{1}{3} + y^2 &= 1 && \text{complete the square} \\
\tfrac{3}{4}\left(x - \tfrac{2}{3}\right)^2 + y^2 &= \tfrac{4}{3} && \text{add } \tfrac{1}{3} \\
\frac{\left(x - \frac{2}{3}\right)^2}{16/9} + \frac{y^2}{4/3} &= 1 && \text{divide by } \tfrac{4}{3}
\end{aligned}
```

The last step is the one people tend to skip. An ellipse's equation has $$1$$ on the right, so
divide by whatever is there first. Now switch to flat coordinates. Here $$k = \frac{\sqrt5}{2}$$, so
$$x = \frac{2u}{\sqrt5}$$, and
$$x - \frac{2}{3} = \frac{2}{\sqrt5}\left(u - \frac{\sqrt5}{3}\right)$$. Squaring brings a factor
$$\frac{4}{5}$$, which turns $$\frac{16}{9}$$ into
$$\frac{16}{9} \cdot \frac{5}{4} = \frac{20}{9}$$:

```math
\frac{\left(u - \frac{\sqrt5}{3}\right)^2}{20/9} + \frac{v^2}{4/3} = 1
```

By :ref{#functions/slide-graph}, this is the ellipse with half-widths
$$a = \frac{2\sqrt5}{3} \approx 1.49$$ and $$b = \frac{2}{\sqrt3} \approx 1.15$$, slid right by
$$\frac{\sqrt5}{3}$$. As a check, the ends of the long axis are where $$y = 0$$, so
$$\frac{3}{4}x^2 - x - 1 = 0$$, that is $$(3x + 2)(x - 2) = 0$$. They are $$(2, 0, 2)$$ and
$$\left(-\frac{2}{3}, 0, \frac{2}{3}\right)$$, and by :ref{#coordinates/space-distance} their
distance apart is

```math
\sqrt{\left(\tfrac{8}{3}\right)^2 + \left(\tfrac{4}{3}\right)^2} = \sqrt{\tfrac{80}{9}} = \tfrac{4\sqrt5}{3} = 2a
```

**Steeper than the side.** Take $$m = 2$$. The same steps give

```math
\begin{aligned}
x^2 + y^2 &= 4x^2 + 4x + 1 && \text{the cone, with } z = 2x + 1 \\
-3x^2 - 4x + y^2 &= 1 && \text{take } 4x^2 + 4x \text{ from both sides} \\
-3\left(x + \tfrac{2}{3}\right)^2 + \tfrac{4}{3} + y^2 &= 1 && \text{complete the square} \\
9\left(x + \tfrac{2}{3}\right)^2 - 3y^2 &= 1 && \text{take } \tfrac{4}{3} \text{, multiply by } -3
\end{aligned}
```

Here $$k = \sqrt5$$ and $$x = \frac{u}{\sqrt5}$$, so $$9\left(x + \frac{2}{3}\right)^2$$ becomes
$$\frac{9}{5}\left(u + \frac{2\sqrt5}{3}\right)^2$$, and the slice in flat coordinates is

```math
\frac{\left(u + \frac{2\sqrt5}{3}\right)^2}{5/9} - \frac{v^2}{1/3} = 1
```

That is a hyperbola (:ref{#hyperbola-definition}), with $$a = \frac{\sqrt5}{3}$$ and
$$b = \frac{1}{\sqrt3}$$, slid left by $$\frac{2\sqrt5}{3}$$. Its two vertices are where $$y = 0$$:
$$3x^2 + 4x + 1 = 0$$ gives $$x = -\frac{1}{3}$$ or $$x = -1$$, at the points
$$\left(-\frac{1}{3}, 0, \frac{1}{3}\right)$$ and $$(-1, 0, -1)$$. One is on the upper half of the
cone and one on the lower half. The steep plane cuts both halves, and each half holds one branch.

**As steep as the side.** Take $$m = 1$$. Now the $$x^2$$ terms cancel:

```math
\begin{aligned}
x^2 + y^2 &= x^2 + 2x + 1 && \text{the cone, with } z = x + 1 \\
y^2 &= 2x + 1 && \text{take } x^2 \text{ from both sides}
\end{aligned}
```

With $$k = \sqrt2$$, put $$x = \frac{u}{\sqrt2}$$ and $$y = v$$: then $$v^2 = \sqrt2\,u + 1$$, so
$$u = \frac{v^2 - 1}{\sqrt2}$$, a parabola lying on its side. As a check, $$(4, 3, 5)$$ is on the
cone and on the plane $$z = x + 1$$. Its flat coordinates are $$u = 4\sqrt2$$ and $$v = 3$$, and
$$\frac{9 - 1}{\sqrt2} = \frac{8}{\sqrt2} = 4\sqrt2$$.

The torch scene fits these numbers. The torch lights only one half of the cone, so the patch on the
wall shows one branch of the hyperbola. The other branch would need light shining backwards out of
the torch.

## Every slice

The three cases follow one pattern, decided by how $$m$$ compares with $$1$$, the slope of the
cone's side. Changing $$m$$ changes the equation, and the picture changes in the way the equation
predicts: the sign in front of $$x^2$$ flips as $$m$$ passes $$1$$.

You can see the three cases side on. In the upright plane $$y = 0$$ the cone is $$x^2 = z^2$$, the
two lines $$z = x$$ and $$z = -x$$, and the plane $$z = mx + 1$$ is the line of slope $$m$$ through
$$(0, 1)$$. For $$m = \frac{1}{2}$$, the line crosses the two sides at $$(2, 2)$$ and
$$\left(-\frac{2}{3}, \frac{2}{3}\right)$$, the ends of the long axis we found with numbers.

:::theorem[Slices of a cone]{#cone-slices}

Let $$m \ge 0$$, let $$k = \sqrt{1 + m^2}$$, and give the plane $$z = mx + 1$$ the flat coordinates
$$(u, v)$$ of :ref{#slicing-a-cone-flat-coordinates}. For $$m \ne 1$$ write
$$u_0 = \frac{km}{1 - m^2}$$. In flat coordinates, the points where the plane meets the double cone
(:ref{#cone}) make:

1. **When m = 0**: the circle $$u^2 + v^2 = 1$$
2. **When 0 < m < 1**: an ellipse, the graph of $$\frac{(u - u_0)^2}{a^2} + \frac{v^2}{b^2} = 1$$
   with $$a = \frac{k}{1 - m^2}$$ and $$b = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - m^2}}$$, where $$a \ge b$$
3. **When m = 1**: a parabola, the graph of $$u = \frac{v^2 - 1}{\sqrt2}$$
4. **When m > 1**: a hyperbola, the graph of $$\frac{(u - u_0)^2}{a^2} - \frac{v^2}{b^2} = 1$$ with
   $$a = \frac{k}{m^2 - 1}$$ and $$b = \frac{1}{\sqrt{m^2 - 1}}$$

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conic_section#Definition"}

**Step 1: the equation of the slice.** A point of the plane is $$(x, y, mx + 1)$$, and by
:ref{#cone} it is on the cone exactly when

```math
\begin{aligned}
x^2 + y^2 &= (mx + 1)^2 && \text{the cone, with } z = mx + 1 \\
x^2 + y^2 &= m^2x^2 + 2mx + 1 && \text{expand the square} \\
(1 - m^2)x^2 - 2mx + y^2 &= 1 && \text{take } m^2x^2 + 2mx \text{ from both sides}
\end{aligned}
```

**Step 2: the level plane.** When $$m = 0$$, $$k = 1$$, so $$u = x$$ and $$v = y$$, and the equation
is $$u^2 + v^2 = 1$$. By :ref{#coordinates/circle-equation} that is the circle with centre
$$(0, 0)$$ and radius $$1$$.

**Step 3: the plane as steep as the side.** When $$m = 1$$, the equation is $$-2x + y^2 = 1$$. Put
$$x = \frac{u}{\sqrt2}$$ and $$y = v$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
-\sqrt2\,u + v^2 &= 1 && \text{since } 2x = \sqrt2\,u \\
u &= \frac{v^2 - 1}{\sqrt2} && \text{solve for } u
\end{aligned}
```

Swapping the letters gives $$v = \frac{u^2}{\sqrt2} - \frac{1}{\sqrt2}$$, which by
:ref{#functions/slide-graph} is the graph of $$v = \frac{u^2}{\sqrt2}$$ slid down by
$$\frac{1}{\sqrt2}$$. By :ref{#functions/flip-graph}, the slice is the image of that graph under the
flip in the line $$v = u$$. So the slice is the graph of $$v = \frac{u^2}{\sqrt2}$$ moved by a slide
and then a flip, which is a parabola (:ref{#parabola-definition}).

**Step 4: complete the square.** From now on $$m \ne 1$$, so $$1 - m^2 \ne 0$$. Write
$$h = \frac{m}{1 - m^2}$$, so that $$2m = 2h(1 - m^2)$$. Then

```math
\begin{aligned}
(1 - m^2)x^2 - 2mx &= (1 - m^2)(x^2 - 2hx) && \text{take out } 1 - m^2 \\
&= (1 - m^2)(x - h)^2 - (1 - m^2)h^2 && \text{complete the square} \\
&= (1 - m^2)(x - h)^2 - \frac{m^2}{1 - m^2} && \text{since } (1 - m^2)h^2 = \frac{m^2}{1 - m^2}
\end{aligned}
```

Put this into the equation from step 1, and use $$1 + \frac{m^2}{1 - m^2} = \frac{1}{1 - m^2}$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
(1 - m^2)(x - h)^2 + y^2 &= \frac{1}{1 - m^2} && \text{add } \frac{m^2}{1 - m^2} \\
(1 - m^2)^2(x - h)^2 + (1 - m^2)y^2 &= 1 && \text{multiply by } 1 - m^2
\end{aligned}
```

**Step 5: into flat coordinates.** Put $$x = \frac{u}{k}$$ and $$y = v$$. Since $$u_0 = kh$$,
$$x - h = \frac{u - u_0}{k}$$, and

```math
(1 - m^2)^2(x - h)^2 = \frac{(1 - m^2)^2}{k^2}(u - u_0)^2 = \frac{(u - u_0)^2}{a^2}
\qquad \text{where } a = \frac{k}{\lvert 1 - m^2 \rvert}
```

So the slice is the graph of

```math
\frac{(u - u_0)^2}{a^2} + (1 - m^2)v^2 = 1
```

**Step 6: less steep than the side.** When $$0 < m < 1$$, $$1 - m^2 > 0$$ and
$$1 - m^2 = \frac{1}{b^2}$$ with $$b = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - m^2}}$$. The equation is
$$\frac{(u - u_0)^2}{a^2} + \frac{v^2}{b^2} = 1$$, and by :ref{#functions/slide-graph} its graph is
the slide by $$(u_0, 0)$$ of the graph of $$\frac{u^2}{a^2} + \frac{v^2}{b^2} = 1$$. A slide is an
isometry, so the slice is an ellipse (:ref{#ellipse-definition}). Also $$a \ge b$$, since
$$a^2 = \frac{1 + m^2}{(1 - m^2)^2}$$ and $$b^2 = \frac{1 - m^2}{(1 - m^2)^2}$$.

**Step 7: steeper than the side.** When $$m > 1$$, $$1 - m^2 = -(m^2 - 1)$$ is negative and equals
$$-\frac{1}{b^2}$$ with $$b = \frac{1}{\sqrt{m^2 - 1}}$$. The equation is
$$\frac{(u - u_0)^2}{a^2} - \frac{v^2}{b^2} = 1$$, the slide by $$(u_0, 0)$$ of the graph of
$$\frac{u^2}{a^2} - \frac{v^2}{b^2} = 1$$. So the slice is a hyperbola
(:ref{#hyperbola-definition}).

:::

With $$m = \frac{1}{2}$$ the theorem gives $$k = \frac{\sqrt5}{2}$$,
$$u_0 = \frac{\sqrt5}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{4}{3} = \frac{\sqrt5}{3}$$ and
$$a = \frac{\sqrt5}{2} \cdot \frac{4}{3} = \frac{2\sqrt5}{3}$$, as we found with numbers. The
theorem only uses slopes $$m \ge 0$$, and that loses nothing: a plane sloping the other way is the
mirror image of one of these in the upright plane $$x = 0$$, and the cone is its own mirror image
there, since $$(-x)^2 = x^2$$.

The level plane, $$m = 0$$, is the torch held straight at the wall. As $$m$$ grows towards $$1$$ the
ellipse gets longer: its half-width $$a = \frac{k}{1 - m^2}$$ grows without limit, because
$$1 - m^2$$ shrinks towards $$0$$. At $$m = 1$$ the ellipse has, in effect, run off to infinity and
become a parabola, which is the moment in the scene when the beam's top edge runs parallel to the
wall.

## Slices through the tip

A plane through the tip itself cuts something smaller. Take the plane $$z = mx$$, with $$m \ge 0$$.
A point $$(x, y, mx)$$ is on the cone exactly when $$x^2 + y^2 = m^2x^2$$, that is,
$$(1 - m^2)x^2 + y^2 = 0$$. When $$m < 1$$ both terms are at least $$0$$, so both are $$0$$, and the
slice is the tip alone. When $$m = 1$$ the equation is $$y^2 = 0$$, and the slice is the line of
points $$(x, 0, x)$$, one of the cone's side lines. When $$m > 1$$ it is $$y^2 = (m^2 - 1)x^2$$, so
$$y = \sqrt{m^2 - 1}\,x$$ or $$y = -\sqrt{m^2 - 1}\,x$$: two lines crossing at the tip. These are
the **degenerate** slices, and they turn up again in :ref{#degenerate-conic}.

## One rule for all three

The guide [The focus of a parabola](./focus-and-directrix.mdx) drew a parabola from a point $$F$$,
its focus, and a line $$L$$, its directrix: the parabola is the set of points equally far from both
(:ref{#focus-directrix}). Now loosen the rule. Ask for the points whose distance to $$F$$ is half
their distance to $$L$$, or twice it. A single number, the ratio of the two distances, then picks
the curve.

Here it is with numbers. Put $$F = (0, 0)$$ and let $$L$$ be the line $$x = -3$$. By
:ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/distance-to-line}, the distance from $$(x, y)$$ to $$L$$ is
$$\lvert x + 3 \rvert$$. For the points whose distance to $$F$$ is half their distance to $$L$$,
square both sides of $$\sqrt{x^2 + y^2} = \frac{1}{2}\lvert x + 3 \rvert$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
x^2 + y^2 &= \tfrac{1}{4}(x + 3)^2 && \text{square both sides} \\
4x^2 + 4y^2 &= x^2 + 6x + 9 && \text{multiply by } 4 \text{ and expand} \\
3x^2 - 6x + 4y^2 &= 9 && \text{take } x^2 + 6x \text{ from both sides} \\
3(x - 1)^2 + 4y^2 &= 12 && \text{complete the square} \\
\frac{(x - 1)^2}{4} + \frac{y^2}{3} &= 1 && \text{divide by } 12
\end{aligned}
```

That is an ellipse with $$a = 2$$ and $$b = \sqrt3$$, slid right by $$1$$. Its $$c$$ is
$$\sqrt{4 - 3} = 1$$, so its foci are $$1$$ either side of the centre $$(1, 0)$$, and one of them is
$$F$$. Its eccentricity is $$\frac{c}{a} = \frac{1}{2}$$ (:ref{#ellipse-eccentricity}), the ratio we
started with. As a check, the point $$(1, \sqrt3)$$ is on it, at distance $$\sqrt{1 + 3} = 2$$ from
$$F$$ and $$1 + 3 = 4$$ from $$L$$.

Ask instead for distance to $$F$$ equal to twice the distance to $$L$$. The same steps give

```math
\begin{aligned}
x^2 + y^2 &= 4(x + 3)^2 && \text{square both sides} \\
3x^2 + 24x - y^2 + 36 &= 0 && \text{expand and collect on the right} \\
3(x + 4)^2 - y^2 &= 12 && \text{complete the square} \\
\frac{(x + 4)^2}{4} - \frac{y^2}{12} &= 1 && \text{divide by } 12
\end{aligned}
```

a hyperbola with $$a = 2$$ and $$b = 2\sqrt3$$, slid left by $$4$$. Its $$c$$ is
$$\sqrt{4 + 12} = 4$$, so $$F$$ is a focus again, and $$\frac{c}{a} = 2$$ is the ratio once more.
The vertex $$(-2, 0)$$ is $$2$$ from $$F$$ and $$1$$ from $$L$$; the vertex $$(-6, 0)$$, on the
other branch, is $$6$$ from $$F$$ and $$3$$ from $$L$$.

To prove this for every ratio, we first move $$F$$ and $$L$$ into the position of the examples. An
isometry can do that, and it keeps the distance from each point to the line as well as to $$F$$.

The guide [The focus of a parabola](./focus-and-directrix.mdx) did the same kind of move, with a
slide and a turn that put the directrix level below the focus. Its lemma
:ref{#focus-and-directrix-moves} showed that every isometry keeps distances to a line. Here we want
the line upright instead, on the left of the focus.

:::lemma[Moving a point and a line into place]{#slicing-a-cone-move-into-place}

Let $$F$$ be a point and $$L$$ a line not through $$F$$, and let $$d$$ be the distance from $$F$$ to
$$L$$ (:ref{#distance-and-angles/point-line-distance}). There is an isometry $$M$$ with $$M(F) = O$$
and $$M(L)$$ the line $$x = -d$$. For every point $$P$$, the distance from $$M(P)$$ to $$M(L)$$
equals the distance from $$P$$ to $$L$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_plane_isometry#Classification"}

**Step 1: slide F to O.** Let $$N$$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $$F$$ to $$L$$, so
$$d(F, N) = d$$, and $$d > 0$$ since $$F$$ is not on $$L$$. The slide $$T_{-F}$$ sends $$F$$ to
$$O$$ (:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/slide-formula}). Write $$N_1 = (p, q)$$ for the image of
$$N$$. Slides keep distances (:ref{#isometries/basic-isometries}), so $$d(O, N_1) = d$$ and
$$p^2 + q^2 = d^2$$.

**Step 2: turn N₁ onto the x-axis.** The point $$U = \left(\frac{p}{d}, \frac{q}{d}\right)$$ has
$$\frac{p^2 + q^2}{d^2} = 1$$, so it is on the unit circle, and by
:ref{#trigonometry/unit-circle-point} it is $$(\cos t, \sin t) = G_t(E_1)$$ for some $$t$$
(:ref{#trigonometry/sine-cosine}). By parts 1 and 5 of :ref{#trigonometry/radian-turns},

```math
G_{\pi - t}(U) = G_{\pi - t}\big(G_t(E_1)\big) = G_\pi(E_1) = (-1, 0)
```

In :ref{#trigonometry/rotation-formula}, multiplying both coordinates of a point by $$d$$ multiplies
both coordinates of its image by $$d$$. The point $$N_1 = (p, q)$$ is $$U$$ with both coordinates
multiplied by $$d$$, so $$G_{\pi - t}(N_1) = (-d, 0)$$.

**Step 3: the line.** Let $$M = G_{\pi - t} \circ T_{-F}$$. It is an isometry, since turns and
slides are (:ref{#isometries/basic-isometries}) and so are composites
(:ref{#isometries/composite-isometry}). It sends $$F$$ to $$O$$ and $$N$$ to $$(-d, 0)$$. By
:ref{#isometries/isometry-lines}, it sends $$L$$ to a line through $$(-d, 0)$$, and the line $$FN$$
to the line through $$O$$ and $$(-d, 0)$$, which is the $$x$$-axis. The lines $$FN$$ and $$L$$ are
perpendicular, so their images are too (:ref{#isometries/isometry-perpendicular}). The line
$$x = -d$$ passes through $$(-d, 0)$$ and is perpendicular to the $$x$$-axis
(:ref{#coordinates/coordinate-rules}). By rule 1 of :ref{#distance-and-angles/perpendicular-rules},
it is the only line through $$(-d, 0)$$ perpendicular to the $$x$$-axis. So $$M(L)$$ is the line
$$x = -d$$.

**Step 4: distances to the line.** Part 1 of :ref{#focus-and-directrix-moves} says that every
isometry keeps the distance from a point to a line. So for every point $$P$$, the distance from
$$M(P)$$ to $$M(L)$$ equals the distance from $$P$$ to $$L$$.

:::

Now the theorem. Its proof is the two examples above, with $$e$$ and $$d$$ in place of the numbers.

:::theorem[One rule for all three]{#focus-directrix-e}

Let $$e > 0$$, let $$F$$ be a point and $$L$$ a line not through $$F$$, and let $$C$$ be the set of
points $$P$$ whose distance to $$F$$ is $$e$$ times their distance to $$L$$. Then $$C$$ is

1. an ellipse with eccentricity $$e$$ when $$e < 1$$
2. a parabola when $$e = 1$$
3. a hyperbola with eccentricity $$e$$ when $$e > 1$$

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conic_section#Eccentricity,_focus_and_directrix"}

**Step 1: move into place.** Let $$d$$ be the distance from $$F$$ to $$L$$, and $$M$$ the isometry
of :ref{#slicing-a-cone-move-into-place}. It keeps the distance to $$F$$, since
$$d(M(P), O) = d(M(P), M(F)) = d(P, F)$$, and the lemma says it keeps the distance to $$L$$. Every
point is $$M(P)$$ for exactly one $$P$$ (:ref{#isometries/isometry-inverse}). So $$M(C)$$ is the set
of points whose distance to $$O$$ is $$e$$ times their distance to the line $$x = -d$$. If $$M(C)$$
is an ellipse, the image of a standard ellipse under an isometry $$J$$, then $$C$$ is its image
under $$M^{-1}$$, an isometry too (:ref{#isometries/inverse-isometry}). So $$C$$ is the image of the
standard ellipse under $$M^{-1} \circ J$$, an isometry by :ref{#isometries/composite-isometry}, and
$$C$$ is an ellipse. The same goes for the other two curves.

**Step 2: the equation.** By :ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/distance-to-line}, the distance from
$$(x, y)$$ to the line $$x = -d$$ is $$\lvert x + d \rvert$$. Both distances are not negative, so
$$(x, y)$$ is in $$M(C)$$ exactly when their squares match:

```math
\begin{aligned}
x^2 + y^2 &= e^2(x + d)^2 && \text{square both distances} \\
x^2 + y^2 &= e^2x^2 + 2e^2dx + e^2d^2 && \text{expand the square} \\
(1 - e^2)x^2 - 2e^2dx + y^2 &= e^2d^2 && \text{take } e^2x^2 + 2e^2dx \text{ from both sides}
\end{aligned}
```

**Step 3: e = 1.** The equation is $$-2dx + y^2 = d^2$$, so $$x = \frac{y^2}{2d} - \frac{d}{2}$$.
Swapping the letters gives $$y = \frac{x^2}{2d} - \frac{d}{2}$$, the graph of $$y = \frac{x^2}{2d}$$
slid down by $$\frac{d}{2}$$ (:ref{#functions/slide-graph}). By :ref{#functions/flip-graph},
$$M(C)$$ is its image under the flip in $$y = x$$, so it is a parabola (:ref{#parabola-definition}).

**Step 4: complete the square.** From now on $$e \ne 1$$. Write $$h = \frac{e^2d}{1 - e^2}$$, so
that $$2e^2d = 2h(1 - e^2)$$. As in step 4 of :ref{#cone-slices},

```math
\begin{aligned}
(1 - e^2)(x - h)^2 - \frac{e^4d^2}{1 - e^2} + y^2 &= e^2d^2 && \text{complete the square} \\
(1 - e^2)(x - h)^2 + y^2 &= \frac{e^2d^2}{1 - e^2} && \text{since } e^2d^2 + \frac{e^4d^2}{1 - e^2} = \frac{e^2d^2}{1 - e^2} \\
\frac{(x - h)^2}{e^2d^2/(1 - e^2)^2} + \frac{y^2}{e^2d^2/(1 - e^2)} &= 1 && \text{divide by } \frac{e^2d^2}{1 - e^2}
\end{aligned}
```

**Step 5: e < 1.** Both bottoms are positive, so this is the graph of
$$\frac{(x - h)^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$$ with

```math
a = \frac{ed}{1 - e^2} \qquad b = \frac{ed}{\sqrt{1 - e^2}}
```

By :ref{#functions/slide-graph} it is a slid ellipse, and $$a \ge b$$ since
$$1 - e^2 \le \sqrt{1 - e^2}$$. Then

```math
c^2 = a^2 - b^2 = \frac{e^2d^2 - e^2d^2(1 - e^2)}{(1 - e^2)^2} = \frac{e^4d^2}{(1 - e^2)^2}
```

so $$c = \frac{e^2d}{1 - e^2}$$, and $$\frac{c}{a} = \frac{e^2d}{ed} = e$$. The eccentricity is
$$e$$ (:ref{#ellipse-eccentricity}).

**Step 6: e > 1.** Now $$1 - e^2 < 0$$, so the second bottom is negative. Writing
$$\frac{y^2}{e^2d^2/(1 - e^2)} = -\frac{y^2}{e^2d^2/(e^2 - 1)}$$, the equation is
$$\frac{(x - h)^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$$ with

```math
a = \frac{ed}{e^2 - 1} \qquad b = \frac{ed}{\sqrt{e^2 - 1}}
```

a slid hyperbola. Then

```math
c^2 = a^2 + b^2 = \frac{e^2d^2 + e^2d^2(e^2 - 1)}{(e^2 - 1)^2} = \frac{e^4d^2}{(e^2 - 1)^2}
```

so $$c = \frac{e^2d}{e^2 - 1}$$, and $$\frac{c}{a} = e$$ again (:ref{#hyperbola-eccentricity}).

:::

The proof also shows where $$F$$ sits. For $$e < 1$$ the centre is $$(h, 0)$$ with $$h = c$$, so
$$F = O$$ is $$c$$ to the left of the centre, at a focus. For $$e > 1$$, $$h = -c$$, and $$F$$ is
$$c$$ to the right of the centre, again at a focus. For $$e = 1$$ the vertex
$$\left(-\frac{d}{2}, 0\right)$$ is halfway between $$F$$ and $$L$$, as in :ref{#focus-parabola}:
the focus-and-directrix parabola is the case $$e = 1$$ of this rule.

So eccentricity has a second meaning. Besides $$\frac{c}{a}$$, a measure of how stretched the curve
is, it is the ratio of two distances, and it sorts the three curves: below $$1$$ an ellipse, exactly
$$1$$ a parabola, above $$1$$ a hyperbola. Turn the dial below through $$1$$ and watch the curve
change.

<EccentricityDial />

As $$e$$ grows towards $$1$$, the ellipse stretches to the right without limit, and at $$e = 1$$ it
opens into a parabola. Past $$1$$, a second branch appears on the far side of $$L$$. That is the
same story the cone told as the slicing plane steepened, and one of the exercises finds the
eccentricity of each slice from its slope.

## Comets

A comet moving under the pull of the Sun follows one of these curves, with the Sun at a focus. That
is a fact of physics, which Newton proved from his law of gravity, and we take it as given here.
Which curve it is depends on the comet's eccentricity, and it decides whether the comet ever comes
back.

Halley's comet has an orbit of eccentricity about $$0.967$$: an ellipse, long and thin, since
$$0.967$$ is close to $$1$$. An ellipse closes up, so the comet returns, about every $$75$$ years.
In 2017 astronomers tracked an object called 'Oumuamua passing the Sun on a path of eccentricity
about $$1.2$$. That is a hyperbola, which never closes, so 'Oumuamua came in from between the stars,
swung once round the Sun, and left for good.

Name the slice of the double cone made by each plane:

1. $$z = 1$$
2. $$z = 0.3x + 1$$
3. $$z = x + 1$$
4. $$z = 3x + 1$$
5. $$z = \frac{1}{2}x$$

   $$x = y = 0$$
6. $$m = 2 > 1$$ through the tip: $$y^2 = 3x^2$$, the two lines $$y = \sqrt3\,x$$ and
   $$y = -\sqrt3\,x$$ (with $$z = 2x$$), crossing at the tip

::::exercise{#ex-slicing-a-cone-three-quarters}

Slice the cone with the plane $$z = \frac{3}{4}x + 1$$.

1. Show that the slice is $$\frac{7}{16}x^2 - \frac{3}{2}x + y^2 = 1$$, and complete the square.
2. Write it in flat coordinates and find the half-widths $$a$$ and $$b$$.
3. Find the two ends of the long axis as points of space, and check that they are $$2a$$ apart.

:::solution

1. Here $$(mx + 1)^2 = \frac{9}{16}x^2 + \frac{3}{2}x + 1$$, so $$x^2 + y^2 = (mx + 1)^2$$ becomes
   $$\frac{7}{16}x^2 - \frac{3}{2}x + y^2 = 1$$. With $$h = \frac{3/4}{7/16} = \frac{12}{7}$$,

   ```math
   \begin{aligned}
   \tfrac{7}{16}\left(x - \tfrac{12}{7}\right)^2 - \tfrac{9}{7} + y^2 &= 1 && \text{complete the square} \\
   \tfrac{7}{16}\left(x - \tfrac{12}{7}\right)^2 + y^2 &= \tfrac{16}{7} && \text{add } \tfrac{9}{7}
   \end{aligned}
   ```

2. Here $$k = \sqrt{1 + \frac{9}{16}} = \frac{5}{4}$$, so $$x = \frac{4u}{5}$$ and
   $$x - \frac{12}{7} = \frac{4}{5}\left(u - \frac{15}{7}\right)$$. Then
   $$\frac{7}{16} \cdot \frac{16}{25}\left(u - \frac{15}{7}\right)^2 = \frac{7}{25}\left(u - \frac{15}{7}\right)^2$$,
   and dividing by $$\frac{16}{7}$$ gives

   ```math
   \frac{\left(u - \frac{15}{7}\right)^2}{400/49} + \frac{v^2}{16/7} = 1
   ```

   So $$a = \frac{20}{7} \approx 2.86$$ and $$b = \frac{4}{\sqrt7} \approx 1.51$$. These agree with
   :ref{#cone-slices}: $$a = \frac{5/4}{7/16} = \frac{20}{7}$$ and
   $$b = \frac{1}{\sqrt{7/16}} = \frac{4}{\sqrt7}$$.

3. With $$y = 0$$: $$\frac{7}{16}x^2 - \frac{3}{2}x - 1 = 0$$, so $$7x^2 - 24x - 16 = 0$$, which is
   $$(7x + 4)(x - 4) = 0$$. The ends are $$(4, 0, 4)$$ and
   $$\left(-\frac{4}{7}, 0, \frac{4}{7}\right)$$. Their differences are $$\frac{32}{7}$$ across and
   $$\frac{24}{7}$$ up, so by :ref{#coordinates/space-distance} they are
   $$\frac{\sqrt{32^2 + 24^2}}{7} = \frac{40}{7}$$ apart, which is $$2a$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-slicing-a-cone-eccentricity-of-slice}

Let $$m \ge 0$$ with $$m \ne 1$$. Use :ref{#cone-slices} to show that the slice by the plane
$$z = mx + 1$$ has eccentricity

```math
e = \frac{\sqrt2\,m}{\sqrt{1 + m^2}}
```

Check it against the slices with $$m = \frac{1}{2}$$ and $$m = 2$$ worked in the guide. What does
the formula give at $$m = 1$$, and which number does $$e$$ stay below however steep the plane?

:::solution

In both cases $$a^2 = \frac{1 + m^2}{(1 - m^2)^2}$$. For an ellipse
$$b^2 = \frac{1}{1 - m^2} = \frac{1 - m^2}{(1 - m^2)^2}$$, and $$c^2 = a^2 - b^2$$. For a hyperbola
$$b^2 = \frac{1}{m^2 - 1} = \frac{m^2 - 1}{(1 - m^2)^2}$$, and $$c^2 = a^2 + b^2$$. Either way

```math
c^2 = \frac{(1 + m^2) - (1 - m^2)}{(1 - m^2)^2} = \frac{2m^2}{(1 - m^2)^2}
\qquad
e^2 = \frac{c^2}{a^2} = \frac{2m^2}{1 + m^2}
```

and $$e$$ is the positive square root.

For $$m = \frac{1}{2}$$: $$e = \frac{\sqrt2/2}{\sqrt5/2} = \sqrt{\frac{2}{5}} \approx 0.63$$. From
the guide, $$a^2 = \frac{20}{9}$$ and $$b^2 = \frac{4}{3} = \frac{12}{9}$$, so $$c^2 = \frac{8}{9}$$
and $$\frac{c^2}{a^2} = \frac{8}{20} = \frac{2}{5}$$. For $$m = 2$$:
$$e = \frac{2\sqrt2}{\sqrt5} \approx 1.26$$. From the guide, $$a^2 = \frac{5}{9}$$ and
$$b^2 = \frac{1}{3} = \frac{3}{9}$$, so $$c^2 = \frac{8}{9}$$ and $$\frac{c^2}{a^2} = \frac{8}{5}$$.

At $$m = 1$$ the formula gives $$\frac{\sqrt2}{\sqrt2} = 1$$, the parabola's place in
:ref{#focus-directrix-e}. Since $$m^2 < 1 + m^2$$, $$e^2 = \frac{2m^2}{1 + m^2} < 2$$, so
$$e < \sqrt2 \approx 1.41$$ for every slope.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-slicing-a-cone-one-third}

Let $$F = (0, 0)$$ and let $$L$$ be the line $$x = -4$$. Find the equation of the set of points
whose distance to $$F$$ is a third of their distance to $$L$$. Name the curve, find $$a$$, $$b$$ and
$$c$$, and check that $$\frac{c}{a} = \frac{1}{3}$$.

:::solution

The distance to $$L$$ is $$\lvert x + 4 \rvert$$ (:ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/distance-to-line}).
Square both sides of $$\sqrt{x^2 + y^2} = \frac{1}{3}\lvert x + 4 \rvert$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
9x^2 + 9y^2 &= x^2 + 8x + 16 && \text{square, multiply by } 9 \\
8x^2 - 8x + 9y^2 &= 16 && \text{take } x^2 + 8x \text{ from both sides} \\
8\left(x - \tfrac{1}{2}\right)^2 + 9y^2 &= 18 && \text{complete the square} \\
\frac{\left(x - \frac{1}{2}\right)^2}{9/4} + \frac{y^2}{2} &= 1 && \text{divide by } 18
\end{aligned}
```

An ellipse with $$a = \frac{3}{2}$$, $$b = \sqrt2$$ and centre $$\left(\frac{1}{2}, 0\right)$$. Then
$$c = \sqrt{\frac{9}{4} - 2} = \frac{1}{2}$$, and $$\frac{c}{a} = \frac{1}{3}$$, as
:ref{#focus-directrix-e} says. As a check, the vertex $$(2, 0)$$ is $$2$$ from $$F$$ and $$6$$ from
$$L$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-slicing-a-cone-directrix-of-ellipse}

The ellipse $$\frac{x^2}{25} + \frac{y^2}{9} = 1$$ has $$a = 5$$, $$b = 3$$, $$c = 4$$ and
$$e = \frac{4}{5}$$. Let $$F = (4, 0)$$ and let $$L$$ be the line $$x = \frac{25}{4}$$. Show that
every point $$P$$ of the ellipse has distance to $$F$$ equal to $$\frac{4}{5}$$ of its distance to
$$L$$. (Use $$y^2 = 9 - \frac{9x^2}{25}$$ on the ellipse.)

:::solution

Let $$P = (x, y)$$ be on the ellipse. Then

```math
\begin{aligned}
d(P, F)^2 &= (x - 4)^2 + y^2 && \text{distance formula} \\
&= x^2 - 8x + 16 + 9 - \tfrac{9}{25}x^2 && \text{put in } y^2 \\
&= \tfrac{16}{25}x^2 - 8x + 25 && \text{collect terms} \\
&= \left(5 - \tfrac{4}{5}x\right)^2 && \text{a perfect square}
\end{aligned}
```

On the ellipse $$\frac{x^2}{25} \le 1$$, so $$x \le 5$$ and $$5 - \frac{4}{5}x \ge 5 - 4 > 0$$. So
$$d(P, F) = 5 - \frac{4}{5}x = \frac{4}{5}\left(\frac{25}{4} - x\right)$$. Also
$$x \le 5 < \frac{25}{4}$$, so the distance from $$P$$ to $$L$$ is $$\frac{25}{4} - x$$. Hence the
distance to $$F$$ is $$\frac{4}{5}$$ of the distance to $$L$$. The line
$$x = \frac{a}{e} = \frac{25}{4}$$ is a directrix of the ellipse.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-slicing-a-cone-comet}

A comet moves on an ellipse with eccentricity $$0.8$$, with the Sun at a focus. At its closest it is
$$1$$ unit from the Sun. How far is it from the Sun at its farthest? (Take the ellipse as
$$\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$$ with the Sun at $$(c, 0)$$, and show as in the last
exercise that a point of the ellipse is $$a - \frac{c}{a}x$$ from the Sun.)

:::solution

On the ellipse, $$y^2 = b^2 - \frac{b^2}{a^2}x^2$$ and $$b^2 = a^2 - c^2$$, so

```math
\begin{aligned}
(x - c)^2 + y^2 &= x^2 - 2cx + c^2 + b^2 - \tfrac{b^2}{a^2}x^2 && \text{put in } y^2 \\
&= \tfrac{c^2}{a^2}x^2 - 2cx + a^2 && \text{since } 1 - \tfrac{b^2}{a^2} = \tfrac{c^2}{a^2},\ c^2 + b^2 = a^2 \\
&= \left(a - \tfrac{c}{a}x\right)^2 && \text{a perfect square}
\end{aligned}
```

Since $$\lvert x \rvert \le a$$ and $$c < a$$, $$a - \frac{c}{a}x > 0$$, so the distance is
$$a - \frac{c}{a}x$$. It is smallest at $$x = a$$, where it is $$a - c$$, and largest at $$x = -a$$,
where it is $$a + c$$.

With $$c = 0.8a$$, the closest distance is $$a - 0.8a = 0.2a = 1$$, so $$a = 5$$ and $$c = 4$$. The
farthest distance is $$a + c = 9$$ units.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-slicing-a-cone-shadow}

A friend works out the slice of the cone by the plane $$z = \frac{1}{2}x + 1$$, gets
$$\frac{(x - 2/3)^2}{16/9} + \frac{y^2}{4/3} = 1$$, and says the slice is an ellipse with long
half-width $$\frac{4}{3}$$.

1. Explain what your friend's ellipse is a picture of, and why its long half-width is too short.
2. Show that the eccentricity of your friend's ellipse is $$\frac{1}{2}$$, while the slice has
   eccentricity $$\sqrt{\frac{2}{5}} \approx 0.63$$.

:::solution

1. Your friend used $$x$$ and $$y$$ as coordinates on the tilted plane. Their ellipse is the set of
   points $$(x, y)$$ on the floor straight below the slice: its shadow, with the light overhead.
   Along $$y$$ the plane is level, so widths in that direction are right. Along $$x$$ the plane
   climbs, and each step is $$k = \frac{\sqrt5}{2}$$ times as long as its shadow
   (:ref{#slicing-a-cone-flat-coordinates}). So the true long half-width is
   $$\frac{4}{3} \cdot \frac{\sqrt5}{2} = \frac{2\sqrt5}{3} \approx 1.49$$, as in the guide.
2. For the shadow, $$a^2 = \frac{16}{9}$$ and $$b^2 = \frac{4}{3} = \frac{12}{9}$$, so
   $$c^2 = \frac{4}{9}$$, $$c = \frac{2}{3}$$ and $$\frac{c}{a} = \frac{2/3}{4/3} = \frac{1}{2}$$.
   For the slice, $$a^2 = \frac{20}{9}$$ and $$b^2 = \frac{12}{9}$$, so $$c^2 = \frac{8}{9}$$ and
   $$\frac{c^2}{a^2} = \frac{2}{5}$$. The shadow is rounder than the slice.

:::
