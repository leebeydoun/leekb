---
title: Recognising a curve from its equation
description:
  Any equation in x², xy, y², x and y draws an ellipse, a parabola, a hyperbola or a degenerate
  case. Completing squares, turning away the xy term, and the number B² − 4AC that sorts them.
---

Imagine the coin sorter at a bank. You tip a jar of mixed coins into the top, and a moment later
they sit in neat stacks, one stack for each kind of coin. The machine never reads what is stamped on
a coin. It measures one thing, the width, and that one measurement tells every kind of coin from
every other.

Equations of curves can be sorted in the same way. Suppose someone hands you the equation
$$5x^2 - 6xy + 5y^2 = 8$$ and asks what it draws. It looks like none of the standard equations of
this chapter, but three of its numbers, the ones in front of $$x^2$$, $$xy$$ and $$y^2$$, are enough
to say that its graph is an ellipse. In this guide we'll tidy any equation of this kind into a
standard one by sliding and turning, and then find the single number that tells the family at a
glance.

## Equations of the second degree

Every curve in this chapter has an equation made of the same few pieces: $$x^2$$, $$xy$$, $$y^2$$,
$$x$$, $$y$$ and a number. The circle of radius $$3$$ about $$(1, 0)$$ is the graph of
$$(x - 1)^2 + y^2 = 9$$ (:ref{#coordinates/circle-equation}), and multiplying out gives
$$x^2 + y^2 - 2x - 8 = 0$$. The parabola $$y = x^2$$ can be written $$x^2 - y = 0$$, and the
hyperbola $$xy = 1$$ can be written $$xy - 1 = 0$$.

:::definition[Second-degree equation]{#general-equation-second-degree}

A **second-degree equation** in $$x$$ and $$y$$ is an equation

```math
Ax^2 + Bxy + Cy^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0
```

where $$A, B, C, D, E, F$$ are numbers and $$A$$, $$B$$, $$C$$ are not all $$0$$. The number
$$B^2 - 4AC$$ is its **discriminant**.

:::

In this guide $$F$$ is the number at the end of the equation, not a focus. For the circle
$$x^2 + y^2 - 2x - 8 = 0$$, $$A = C = 1$$, $$B = 0$$, $$D = -2$$, $$E = 0$$ and $$F = -8$$. For
$$xy - 1 = 0$$, the only numbers that aren't $$0$$ are $$B = 1$$ and $$F = -1$$.

Moving a curve keeps its equation of this kind. By :ref{#functions/slide-graph},
:ref{#functions/flip-graph} and :ref{#turn-graph}, a move puts first-degree expressions such as
$$x - 3$$ or $$x\cos\varphi + y\sin\varphi$$ in place of $$x$$ and $$y$$, so multiplying out never
makes a power higher than $$2$$.

The squared terms can't all cancel either. If they did, the new equation would have no $$x^2$$,
$$xy$$ or $$y^2$$ at all. Undoing the move puts first-degree expressions in again, which would give
back an equation with no squared terms, and not the one we started from.

So every ellipse, parabola and hyperbola has a second-degree equation. The three curves together are
called **conics**, after the cone, whose slices give all three (:ref{#cone-slices}). The rest of
this guide goes the other way, from the equation to the curve.

## Equations with no xy term

When $$B = 0$$, the equation splits into an $$x$$ part and a $$y$$ part that don't mix. Completing
the square in each part (:ref{#quadratics/complete-square}) gathers each letter into one bracket,
such as $$(x - 3)^2$$. Then :ref{#functions/slide-graph} reads the brackets as a slide: $$x - 3$$
means the curve has been slid $$3$$ to the right. What is left is a standard equation from the
earlier guides, and the numbers in the brackets say where it went.

:::example[An ellipse]

Take $$x^2 + 4y^2 - 6x + 8y + 9 = 0$$. Gather the $$x$$ terms and the $$y$$ terms, and complete each
square:

```math
\begin{aligned}
(x^2 - 6x) + 4(y^2 + 2y) + 9 &= 0 &&\qquad \text{group the } x \text{ terms and the } y \text{ terms} \\
\big((x - 3)^2 - 9\big) + 4\big((y + 1)^2 - 1\big) + 9 &= 0 &&\qquad \text{complete each square} \\
(x - 3)^2 + 4(y + 1)^2 - 4 &= 0 &&\qquad -9 - 4 + 9 = -4 \\
(x - 3)^2 + 4(y + 1)^2 &= 4 &&\qquad \text{add } 4 \text{ to both sides} \\
\frac{(x - 3)^2}{4} + \frac{(y + 1)^2}{1} &= 1 &&\qquad \text{divide both sides by } 4
\end{aligned}
```

By :ref{#functions/slide-graph}, this is the ellipse $$\frac{x^2}{4} + y^2 = 1$$ slid by
$$(3, -1)$$: an ellipse centred at $$(3, -1)$$, reaching $$2$$ either side across and $$1$$ up and
down. As a check, the point $$(5, -1)$$ is $$2$$ to the right of the centre, and
$$25 + 4 - 30 - 8 + 9 = 0$$.

:::

The last step matters. The standard equation of an ellipse has $$1$$ on the right
(:ref{#ellipse-definition}), and only after dividing by $$4$$ can you read the half-widths
$$\sqrt4 = 2$$ and $$\sqrt1 = 1$$. Reading them off $$(x - 3)^2 + 4(y + 1)^2 = 4$$ instead gives
wrong numbers.

:::example[A hyperbola opening up and down]

Take $$9x^2 - 4y^2 + 18x + 16y + 29 = 0$$. This time the $$y^2$$ term has a minus sign:

```math
\begin{aligned}
9(x^2 + 2x) - 4(y^2 - 4y) + 29 &= 0 &&\qquad \text{group the } x \text{ terms and the } y \text{ terms} \\
9\big((x + 1)^2 - 1\big) - 4\big((y - 2)^2 - 4\big) + 29 &= 0 &&\qquad \text{complete each square} \\
9(x + 1)^2 - 4(y - 2)^2 + 36 &= 0 &&\qquad -9 + 16 + 29 = 36 \\
9(x + 1)^2 - 4(y - 2)^2 &= -36 &&\qquad \text{subtract } 36 \\
\frac{(y - 2)^2}{9} - \frac{(x + 1)^2}{4} &= 1 &&\qquad \text{divide both sides by } -36
\end{aligned}
```

Dividing by $$-36$$ changes both signs, so the minus sign moves to the $$x$$ term. By
:ref{#functions/flip-graph}, swapping the letters in $$\frac{x^2}{9} - \frac{y^2}{4} = 1$$ gives the
flip of that hyperbola in the line $$y = x$$, which opens up and down. Then
:ref{#functions/slide-graph} slides it by $$(-1, 2)$$. So the curve is a hyperbola centred at
$$(-1, 2)$$ with its vertices $$3$$ above and below the centre, at $$(-1, 5)$$ and $$(-1, -1)$$. As
a check, $$(-1, 5)$$ gives $$9 - 100 - 18 + 80 + 29 = 0$$.

:::

:::example[A sideways parabola]

Take $$y^2 - 4x + 2y + 9 = 0$$. There is no $$x^2$$ term, so only the $$y$$ square needs completing:

```math
\begin{aligned}
(y^2 + 2y) - 4x + 9 &= 0 &&\qquad \text{group the } y \text{ terms} \\
(y + 1)^2 - 1 - 4x + 9 &= 0 &&\qquad \text{complete the square} \\
(y + 1)^2 &= 4x - 8 = 4(x - 2) &&\qquad \text{add } 4x - 8 \text{ to both sides} \\
x - 2 &= \frac{(y + 1)^2}{4} &&\qquad \text{divide by } 4 \text{ and swap the sides}
\end{aligned}
```

The graph of $$x = \frac{y^2}{4}$$ is the flip of $$y = \frac{x^2}{4}$$ in the line $$y = x$$
(:ref{#functions/flip-graph}), a parabola opening to the right. Sliding it by $$(2, -1)$$ gives our
curve, with its vertex at $$(2, -1)$$. As a check, the point $$(3, 1)$$ gives
$$1 - 12 + 2 + 9 = 0$$.

:::

### When the curve falls apart

Keep the $$x^2 + 4y^2 - 6x + 8y$$ of the first example, but change the number at the end to $$c$$.
The same steps give $$(x - 3)^2 + 4(y + 1)^2 = 13 - c$$, so changing $$c$$ changes only the right
side. For $$c = 9$$ the right side is $$4$$, and we have the ellipse from before. As $$c$$ grows the
right side shrinks, and the ellipse shrinks with it about the same centre.

<ShrinkingEllipse caption="The graphs of x² + 4y² − 6x + 8y + c = 0 for four values of c. Each is (x − 3)² + 4(y + 1)² = 13 − c, so they share the centre (3, −1). At c = 13 only the centre is left, and from c = 14 on there are no points at all." />

At $$c = 13$$ the right side is $$0$$. A sum of two squares is $$0$$ exactly when both squares are
$$0$$, so the only point is $$(3, -1)$$. At $$c = 14$$ the right side is $$-1$$, and a sum of
squares is never negative, so no point fits and the graph is empty. Both still count as graphs of
second-degree equations, so they get a name.

:::definition[Degenerate cases]{#degenerate-conic}

The **degenerate cases** of a second-degree equation are the graphs that are not conics. They are:

- the empty set, with no points
- a single point
- one line
- two parallel lines
- two lines crossing at a point

:::

By :ref{#no-xy-term} and :ref{#conic-discriminant} below, these are the only graphs a second-degree
equation can have besides the conics. Each case has a short equation of its own:

- $$x^2 + y^2 = 0$$ holds only at $$(0, 0)$$, so its graph is the single point $$O$$
- $$x^2 + y^2 = -1$$ has no solutions, since squares are never negative, so its graph is empty
- $$x^2 - y^2 = 0$$ says $$(x - y)(x + y) = 0$$, and a product is $$0$$ exactly when one of its
  factors is, so its graph is the two lines $$y = x$$ and $$y = -x$$
- $$x^2 = 4$$ holds exactly when $$x = 2$$ or $$x = -2$$, so its graph is two vertical lines
- $$x^2 - 2x + 1 = 0$$ says $$(x - 1)^2 = 0$$, so its graph is the one line $$x = 1$$

<DegenerateCases caption="Four degenerate cases: x² + y² = 0 is the point O, x² + y² = −1 has no points, x² − y² = 0 is the lines y = x and y = −x, and x² = 4 is the lines x = 2 and x = −2." />

The ellipse, hyperbola and parabola examples already show the rule. When $$A$$ and $$C$$ had the
same sign, we got an ellipse. Opposite signs gave a hyperbola, and with $$A = 0$$ we got a parabola.

:::proposition[No xy term: complete the squares]{#no-xy-term}

Let $$A$$ and $$C$$ be numbers, not both $$0$$, and let $$D$$, $$E$$, $$F$$ be any numbers. The
graph of $$Ax^2 + Cy^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0$$ is:

1. an ellipse, a single point or empty, when $$A$$ and $$C$$ have the same sign
2. a hyperbola or two crossing lines, when $$A$$ and $$C$$ have opposite signs
3. a parabola, two parallel lines, one line or empty, when one of $$A$$ and $$C$$ is $$0$$

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degenerate_conic#Classification"}

Multiplying both sides of an equation by $$-1$$ doesn't change its solutions, so we may assume that
$$A > 0$$, or that $$A = 0$$ and $$C > 0$$.

**Neither is 0.** By :ref{#quadratics/complete-square} with $$\frac{D}{A}$$ in place of $$b$$,

```math
Ax^2 + Dx = A\Big(x^2 + \frac{D}{A}x\Big) = A\Big(x + \frac{D}{2A}\Big)^2 - \frac{D^2}{4A}
```

and in the same way $$Cy^2 + Ey = C\big(y + \frac{E}{2C}\big)^2 - \frac{E^2}{4C}$$. Write
$$h = -\frac{D}{2A}$$, $$k = -\frac{E}{2C}$$ and $$K = \frac{D^2}{4A} + \frac{E^2}{4C} - F$$. Then
the equation says $$A(x - h)^2 + C(y - k)^2 = K$$, so by :ref{#functions/slide-graph} its graph is
the slide by $$(h, k)$$ of the graph of $$Ax^2 + Cy^2 = K$$. A slide is an isometry
(:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/formula-isometries}), and an isometry after an isometry is one
(:ref{#isometries/composite-isometry}). So a slide sends ellipses to ellipses, hyperbolas to
hyperbolas, a point to a point and lines to lines (:ref{#isometries/isometry-lines}), and it is
enough to sort the graph of $$Ax^2 + Cy^2 = K$$.

_Same signs_, so $$A > 0$$ and $$C > 0$$. If $$K > 0$$, dividing by $$K$$ gives

```math
\frac{x^2}{K/A} + \frac{y^2}{K/C} = 1
```

the ellipse with half-widths $$\sqrt{K/A}$$ and $$\sqrt{K/C}$$ (:ref{#ellipse-definition}). If
$$K = 0$$, the left side is a sum of two terms that are never negative, so it is $$0$$ exactly when
$$x = y = 0$$: one point. If $$K < 0$$, no point fits.

_Opposite signs_, so $$A > 0$$ and $$C < 0$$. Write $$C = -c$$ with $$c > 0$$. If $$K > 0$$,
dividing by $$K$$ gives $$\frac{x^2}{K/A} - \frac{y^2}{K/c} = 1$$, a hyperbola
(:ref{#hyperbola-definition}). If $$K < 0$$, dividing by $$K$$ gives

```math
\frac{y^2}{-K/c} - \frac{x^2}{-K/A} = 1
```

which by :ref{#functions/flip-graph} is the flip in $$y = x$$ of the hyperbola
$$\frac{x^2}{-K/c} - \frac{y^2}{-K/A} = 1$$, so a hyperbola again. If $$K = 0$$, the equation is
$$Ax^2 - cy^2 = 0$$, which factorises as

```math
\big(\sqrt{A}\,x - \sqrt{c}\,y\big)\big(\sqrt{A}\,x + \sqrt{c}\,y\big) = 0
```

A product is $$0$$ exactly when a factor is, so the graph is the two lines
$$\sqrt{A}\,x = \sqrt{c}\,y$$ and $$\sqrt{A}\,x = -\sqrt{c}\,y$$
(:ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/equation-line}). Their slopes $$\sqrt{A/c}$$ and $$-\sqrt{A/c}$$
differ, and both pass through $$O$$, so they cross there.

**One of them is 0.** Say $$C = 0$$, so $$A > 0$$ and the equation is $$Ax^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0$$. If
$$E \ne 0$$, it says

```math
y = -\frac{A}{E}x^2 - \frac{D}{E}x - \frac{F}{E}
```

whose graph is a parabola by :ref{#quadratic-parabola}. If $$E = 0$$, the equation
$$Ax^2 + Dx + F = 0$$ doesn't involve $$y$$. By :ref{#quadratics/root-count} it has two solutions,
one or none, and the graph is the vertical lines through them: two parallel lines, one line, or
nothing. The case $$A = 0$$ is the same with the letters swapped, which by
:ref{#functions/flip-graph} flips everything in $$y = x$$. That flip is an isometry
(:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/diagonal-reflection}), so it sends a parabola to a parabola and
parallel lines to parallel lines.

:::

## Turning away the xy term

An $$xy$$ term means the curve is tilted. The curves in the standard equations have their axes along
the $$x$$-axis and the $$y$$-axis, and an $$xy$$ term appears when the curve has been turned off
them, as in :ref{#hyperbola-turn}, where turning $$xy = c$$ by $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$ gave
$$y^2 - x^2 = 2c$$. So the plan is to turn the curve back until its axes line up again. In the
equation, that means new letters $$u$$ and $$v$$ measured along turned axes.

<TurnedEllipse caption="The graph of 5x² − 6xy + 5y² = 8, in blue, is the dashed ellipse x²/4 + y² = 1 turned by π/4. The green u-axis and v-axis are the x-axis and y-axis turned by π/4. The end (2, 0) of the dashed ellipse turns to (√2, √2) on the blue one." />

Here is the opening equation $$5x^2 - 6xy + 5y^2 = 8$$ worked with numbers. Put in

```math
x = \frac{u - v}{\sqrt2} \qquad y = \frac{u + v}{\sqrt2}
```

which is $$(x, y) = G_{\pi/4}(u, v)$$, the point $$(u, v)$$ turned by $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$ about $$O$$,
by :ref{#trigonometry/rotation-formula} with
$$\cos\frac{\pi}{4} = \sin\frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}$$. Then the three squared terms are

```math
\begin{aligned}
x^2 &= \tfrac{1}{2}(u^2 - 2uv + v^2) &&\qquad \text{square } u - v \text{ and divide by } 2 \\
xy &= \tfrac{1}{2}(u^2 - v^2) &&\qquad (u - v)(u + v) = u^2 - v^2 \\
y^2 &= \tfrac{1}{2}(u^2 + 2uv + v^2) &&\qquad \text{square } u + v \text{ and divide by } 2
\end{aligned}
```

and the equation becomes

```math
\begin{aligned}
5x^2 + 5y^2 - 6xy &= \tfrac{5}{2}(2u^2 + 2v^2) - 3(u^2 - v^2) &&\qquad \text{the } uv \text{ terms of } x^2 \text{ and } y^2 \text{ cancel} \\
&= 5u^2 + 5v^2 - 3u^2 + 3v^2 &&\qquad \text{multiply out} \\
&= 2u^2 + 8v^2 &&\qquad \text{collect}
\end{aligned}
```

So the equation in $$u$$ and $$v$$ is $$2u^2 + 8v^2 = 8$$, or $$\frac{u^2}{4} + v^2 = 1$$ after
dividing by $$8$$. The $$uv$$ term is gone. By :ref{#remove-xy} below, the curve is the ellipse
$$\frac{x^2}{4} + y^2 = 1$$ turned by $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$, with half-widths $$2$$ and $$1$$ and its
long axis along the line $$y = x$$.

Check it with a point. The end $$(2, 0)$$ of the untilted ellipse turns to
$$G_{\pi/4}(2, 0) = (\sqrt2, \sqrt2)$$, and $$5 \cdot 2 - 6 \cdot 2 + 5 \cdot 2 = 8$$. The top
$$(0, 1)$$ turns to $$\big({-\frac{\sqrt2}{2}}, \frac{\sqrt2}{2}\big)$$, and
$$\frac{5}{2} + 3 + \frac{5}{2} = 8$$. Both fit.

Which way the turn goes is the place to be careful. Putting $$(x, y) = G_\theta(u, v)$$ into the
equation describes the curve turned **back** by $$\theta$$, so the curve we were given is the tidy
one turned **forward** by $$\theta$$. Mixing up the signs in the substitution gives the turn the
other way, and a point checked in the original equation catches that at once.

:::theorem[Turning away the xy term]{#remove-xy}

Let $$f(x, y) = Ax^2 + Bxy + Cy^2 + Dx + Ey + F$$ with $$B \ne 0$$, and choose $$\theta$$ as
follows:

1. if $$A = C$$, then $$\theta = \frac{\pi}{4}$$
2. if $$A \ne C$$, then $$2\theta$$ is an angle with $$0 < 2\theta < \pi$$ and
   $$\tan 2\theta = \frac{B}{A - C}$$, which exists by the proof below

Then $$0 < \theta < \frac{\pi}{2}$$, and

```math
f(u\cos\theta - v\sin\theta,\ u\sin\theta + v\cos\theta) = A'u^2 + C'v^2 + D'u + E'v + F
```

with no $$uv$$ term, where

```math
\begin{aligned}
A' &= A\cos^2\theta + B\sin\theta\cos\theta + C\sin^2\theta \\
C' &= A\sin^2\theta - B\sin\theta\cos\theta + C\cos^2\theta \\
D' &= D\cos\theta + E\sin\theta \\
E' &= -D\sin\theta + E\cos\theta
\end{aligned}
```

The graph of $$f(x, y) = 0$$ is the turn $$G_\theta$$ about $$O$$ of the graph of
$$A'x^2 + C'y^2 + D'x + E'y + F = 0$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_of_axes_in_two_dimensions#Rotation_of_conic_sections"}

Write $$c = \cos\theta$$ and $$s = \sin\theta$$ for short, and $$x = uc - vs$$, $$y = us + vc$$.

**The new equation.** Multiplying out the squared terms,

```math
\begin{aligned}
x^2 &= u^2c^2 - 2uv\,cs + v^2s^2 &&\qquad \text{square } uc - vs \\
xy &= u^2cs + uv(c^2 - s^2) - v^2cs &&\qquad \text{multiply } (uc - vs)(us + vc) \\
y^2 &= u^2s^2 + 2uv\,cs + v^2c^2 &&\qquad \text{square } us + vc
\end{aligned}
```

Multiply these by $$A$$, $$B$$ and $$C$$ and add. The $$u^2$$ terms give $$A'u^2$$ and the $$v^2$$
terms give $$C'v^2$$. The $$uv$$ terms give

```math
\begin{aligned}
&-2A\,cs + B(c^2 - s^2) + 2C\,cs &&\qquad \text{one } uv \text{ term from each line} \\
&= (C - A)\cdot 2cs + B(c^2 - s^2) &&\qquad \text{collect the } cs \text{ terms} \\
&= (C - A)\sin 2\theta + B\cos 2\theta &&\qquad \text{double angles}
\end{aligned}
```

where the last step is :ref{#trigonometry/double-angle}. The first-degree terms give
$$Dx + Ey = (Dc + Es)u + (-Ds + Ec)v = D'u + E'v$$, and $$F$$ stays as it is.

**The uv term is 0.** If $$A = C$$, then $$C - A = 0$$ and $$\cos 2\theta = \cos\frac{\pi}{2} = 0$$
(:ref{#trigonometry/quarter-values}), so both parts of the $$uv$$ coefficient are $$0$$, and
$$\theta = \frac{\pi}{4}$$ is between $$0$$ and $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$.

If $$A \ne C$$, the number $$t = \frac{B}{A - C}$$ is not $$0$$. By
:ref{#trigonometry/tangent-one-angle} there is exactly one angle $$\beta$$ with
$$-\frac{\pi}{2} < \beta < \frac{\pi}{2}$$ and $$\tan\beta = t$$, and $$\beta \ne 0$$. If
$$\beta > 0$$, take $$2\theta = \beta$$. If $$\beta < 0$$, take $$2\theta = \beta + \pi$$, which has
the same tangent, since by :ref{#trigonometry/half-turn-identities} adding $$\pi$$ changes the sign
of both the sine and the cosine. So an angle $$2\theta$$ with $$0 < 2\theta < \pi$$ and tangent
$$t$$ exists. For any such angle, $$\cos 2\theta \ne 0$$ because the tangent is defined there, and

```math
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\sin 2\theta}{\cos 2\theta} &= \frac{B}{A - C} &&\qquad \text{the choice of } \theta \\
(A - C)\sin 2\theta &= B\cos 2\theta &&\qquad \text{multiply both sides by } (A - C)\cos 2\theta \\
(C - A)\sin 2\theta + B\cos 2\theta &= 0 &&\qquad \text{subtract } (A - C)\sin 2\theta
\end{aligned}
```

**The two graphs.** Write $$g(u, v) = A'u^2 + C'v^2 + D'u + E'v + F$$, so that
$$g(u, v) = f\big(G_\theta(u, v)\big)$$ by :ref{#trigonometry/rotation-formula}. By
:ref{#turn-graph}, the turn $$G_\theta$$ sends the graph of $$g(x, y) = 0$$ onto the graph of

```math
g(x\cos\theta + y\sin\theta,\ -x\sin\theta + y\cos\theta) = 0
```

The point $$(x\cos\theta + y\sin\theta,\ -x\sin\theta + y\cos\theta)$$ is $$G_{-\theta}(x, y)$$, by
:ref{#trigonometry/rotation-formula} with $$-\theta$$ and :ref{#trigonometry/negative-angle}. By
:ref{#trigonometry/radian-turns}, $$G_\theta \circ G_{-\theta} = G_0$$, which leaves every point
where it is. So

```math
g\big(G_{-\theta}(x, y)\big) = f\big(G_\theta(G_{-\theta}(x, y))\big) = f(x, y)
```

and $$G_\theta$$ sends the graph of $$g(x, y) = 0$$ onto the graph of $$f(x, y) = 0$$.

:::

To find $$2\theta$$ in practice, press $$\tan^{-1}$$ on $$\frac{B}{A - C}$$
(:ref{#trigonometry/inverse-tan}), and add $$\pi$$ if the answer is negative. Then halve it.

:::example[The hyperbola xy = 1 again]

Here $$A = C = 0$$ and $$B = 1$$, so $$\theta = \frac{\pi}{4}$$, with
$$\cos\theta = \sin\theta = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}$$. The formulas give $$A' = \frac{1}{2}$$ and
$$C' = -\frac{1}{2}$$, and $$D' = E' = 0$$, so the equation in $$u$$ and $$v$$ is

```math
\frac{u^2}{2} - \frac{v^2}{2} = 1
```

So $$xy = 1$$ is the hyperbola $$\frac{x^2}{2} - \frac{y^2}{2} = 1$$ turned by $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$.
Its vertex $$(\sqrt2, 0)$$ turns to $$G_{\pi/4}(\sqrt2, 0) = (1, 1)$$, and $$1 \cdot 1 = 1$$.

This agrees with part 2 of :ref{#hyperbola-turn}, which turns $$x^2 - y^2 = 2$$ by $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$
onto $$xy = 1$$.

:::

<TurnedHyperbola caption="The graph of x² + √3xy = 3, in blue, is the hyperbola u²/2 − v²/6 = 1 drawn on the green axes turned by π/6. Its asymptotes, dashed in orange, are the y-axis and the line x + √3y = 0." />

:::example[A turn by π/6]

Take $$x^2 + \sqrt3\,xy = 3$$, so $$A = 1$$, $$B = \sqrt3$$ and $$C = 0$$. Now $$A \ne C$$, and
$$\tan 2\theta = \frac{\sqrt3}{1 - 0} = \sqrt3$$. The angle between $$0$$ and $$\pi$$ with tangent
$$\sqrt3$$ is $$\frac{\pi}{3}$$, so $$\theta = \frac{\pi}{6}$$, with
$$\cos\theta = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$ and $$\sin\theta = \frac{1}{2}$$
(:ref{#trigonometry/sin-cos-30-60}). Then

```math
\begin{aligned}
A' &= 1 \cdot \tfrac{3}{4} + \sqrt3 \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt3}{4} + 0 = \tfrac{3}{4} + \tfrac{3}{4} = \tfrac{3}{2} \\
C' &= 1 \cdot \tfrac{1}{4} - \sqrt3 \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt3}{4} + 0 = \tfrac{1}{4} - \tfrac{3}{4} = -\tfrac{1}{2}
\end{aligned}
```

The equation in $$u$$ and $$v$$ is $$\frac{3}{2}u^2 - \frac{1}{2}v^2 = 3$$, and dividing by $$3$$
gives $$\frac{u^2}{2} - \frac{v^2}{6} = 1$$. So the curve is a hyperbola with $$a = \sqrt2$$ and
$$b = \sqrt6$$, turned by $$\frac{\pi}{6}$$. Its vertex $$(\sqrt2, 0)$$ turns to

```math
G_{\pi/6}(\sqrt2, 0) = \Big(\sqrt2 \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2},\ \sqrt2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}\Big) = \Big(\tfrac{\sqrt6}{2},\ \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\Big)
```

and
$$x^2 + \sqrt3\,xy = \frac{6}{4} + \sqrt3 \cdot \frac{\sqrt{12}}{4} = \frac{3}{2} + \frac{6}{4} = 3$$.

:::

## One number sorts the curve

Turning the curve takes several lines of algebra, more than you need when all you want is its
family. The coin sorter measured one thing, and a second-degree equation has one number that plays
the same part: its discriminant $$B^2 - 4AC$$. Turning changes $$A$$, $$B$$ and $$C$$, but it leaves
this number alone, and once the $$xy$$ term is gone the number is $$-4A'C'$$, whose sign says
whether $$A'$$ and $$C'$$ agree.

Check it on the two examples with $$\theta = \frac{\pi}{4}$$. For $$5x^2 - 6xy + 5y^2 = 8$$, the
discriminant is $$36 - 100 = -64$$, and after the turn it is $$0 - 4 \cdot 2 \cdot 8 = -64$$. For
$$xy = 1$$ it is $$1 - 0 = 1$$, and after the turn it is
$$0 - 4 \cdot \frac{1}{2} \cdot \big({-\frac{1}{2}}\big) = 1$$.

:::theorem[The discriminant tells the family]{#conic-discriminant}

Take a second-degree equation $$Ax^2 + Bxy + Cy^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0$$.

1. For every angle $$\theta$$, putting $$x = u\cos\theta - v\sin\theta$$ and
   $$y = u\sin\theta + v\cos\theta$$ gives an equation $$A'u^2 + B'uv + C'v^2 + \dots = 0$$ with
   $$B'^2 - 4A'C' = B^2 - 4AC$$.
2. If $$B^2 - 4AC < 0$$, the graph is an ellipse, a single point or empty.
3. If $$B^2 - 4AC = 0$$, the graph is a parabola, two parallel lines, one line or empty.
4. If $$B^2 - 4AC > 0$$, the graph is a hyperbola or two crossing lines.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conic_section#Invariants"}

**Part 1.** In the proof of :ref{#remove-xy}, the substitution gave $$A'$$ and $$C'$$ as in that
theorem and $$B' = (C - A)\sin 2\theta + B\cos 2\theta$$, for every $$\theta$$. Write $$c$$ and
$$s$$ for $$\cos\theta$$ and $$\sin\theta$$ again. Adding and subtracting the formulas for $$A'$$
and $$C'$$,

```math
\begin{aligned}
A' + C' &= A(c^2 + s^2) + C(s^2 + c^2) = A + C &&\qquad \cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1 \\
A' - C' &= (A - C)(c^2 - s^2) + 2B\,sc = (A - C)\cos 2\theta + B\sin 2\theta &&\qquad \text{double angles}
\end{aligned}
```

using :ref{#trigonometry/pythagorean-identity} and :ref{#trigonometry/double-angle}. Square
$$A' - C'$$ and $$B'$$ and add. The two cross terms are $$2(A - C)B\cos 2\theta\sin 2\theta$$ and
its negative, so they cancel:

```math
\begin{aligned}
(A' - C')^2 + B'^2 &= (A - C)^2(\cos^2 2\theta + \sin^2 2\theta) + B^2(\sin^2 2\theta + \cos^2 2\theta) &&\qquad \text{cross terms cancel} \\
&= (A - C)^2 + B^2 &&\qquad \cos^2 2\theta + \sin^2 2\theta = 1
\end{aligned}
```

Now $$4A'C' = (A' + C')^2 - (A' - C')^2$$, since multiplying out the right side leaves $$4A'C'$$. So

```math
\begin{aligned}
B'^2 - 4A'C' &= B'^2 + (A' - C')^2 - (A' + C')^2 &&\qquad 4A'C' = (A' + C')^2 - (A' - C')^2 \\
&= (A - C)^2 + B^2 - (A + C)^2 &&\qquad \text{the sum, and the sum of squares} \\
&= B^2 - 4AC &&\qquad (A - C)^2 - (A + C)^2 = -4AC
\end{aligned}
```

**Parts 2 to 4 when B = 0.** The discriminant is $$-4AC$$. It is negative exactly when $$AC > 0$$,
when $$A$$ and $$C$$ have the same sign. It is $$0$$ exactly when one of them is $$0$$, and positive
exactly when they have opposite signs. So :ref{#no-xy-term} gives each part.

**Parts 2 to 4 when B ≠ 0.** Choose $$\theta$$ as in :ref{#remove-xy}, so that $$B' = 0$$, and by
part 1, $$B^2 - 4AC = -4A'C'$$. The numbers $$A'$$ and $$C'$$ are not both $$0$$: the sum of squares
in part 1 gives $$(A' - C')^2 = (A - C)^2 + B^2 > 0$$, so $$A' \ne C'$$. So :ref{#no-xy-term} sorts
the graph of $$A'x^2 + C'y^2 + D'x + E'y + F = 0$$ by the sign of $$-4A'C'$$, which is the sign of
$$B^2 - 4AC$$.

By :ref{#remove-xy}, the graph we want is the turn $$G_\theta$$ of that graph. A turn is an isometry
(:ref{#isometries/basic-isometries}), so by :ref{#isometries/composite-isometry} it sends an
ellipse, a parabola or a hyperbola to another of the same kind, and it sends a point to a point and
lines to lines (:ref{#isometries/isometry-lines}). Two different points stay different, because
their distance is kept. So lines that cross at one point still cross at one point, parallel lines,
which have no point in common, still have none, and the empty set stays empty.

:::

The name echoes the discriminant $$b^2 - 4ac$$ of a quadratic $$ax^2 + bx + c = 0$$
(:ref{#quadratics/discriminant}), where the sign counts the solutions. The two are relatives rather
than the same thing: here the sign of $$B^2 - 4AC$$ sorts a curve into its family, and the numbers
$$D$$, $$E$$, $$F$$ then decide between the conic and its degenerate cases.

In the next diagram, the sliders set $$A$$, $$B$$ and $$C$$ in $$Ax^2 + Bxy + Cy^2 = 4$$, and the
green dashed lines are the $$u$$-axis and the $$v$$-axis of the turn that removes the $$xy$$ term.
It starts at $$2.5x^2 - 3xy + 2.5y^2 = 4$$, the opening equation divided by $$2$$. Since
$$D = E = 0$$ here, the parabola family can only show up as two parallel lines or as nothing: try
$$A = 1$$, $$B = 2$$, $$C = 1$$, where the left side is $$(x + y)^2$$.

## A method for any second-degree equation

Putting the pieces together gives a method that recognises and draws the graph of any second-degree
equation:

1. **Read off the numbers** $$A$$, $$B$$, $$C$$, $$D$$, $$E$$, $$F$$, with signs.
2. **Find the family** from the sign of $$B^2 - 4AC$$ (:ref{#conic-discriminant}).
3. **Turn away the xy term** if $$B \ne 0$$: find $$\theta$$ and the numbers $$A'$$, $$C'$$, $$D'$$,
   $$E'$$ from :ref{#remove-xy}.
4. **Complete the squares** in the equation without an $$xy$$ term, and divide so that the right
   side is $$1$$ (:ref{#no-xy-term}).
5. **Draw it**: draw the standard curve slid by the numbers in the brackets, then turn the picture
   by $$\theta$$.
6. **Check a point** of the drawing in the original equation.

:::example[A tilted parabola]

Take $$x^2 + 2xy + y^2 + x - y = 0$$.

1. The numbers are $$A = 1$$, $$B = 2$$, $$C = 1$$, $$D = 1$$, $$E = -1$$ and $$F = 0$$.
2. The discriminant is $$4 - 4 \cdot 1 \cdot 1 = 0$$, so the graph is a parabola or a degenerate
   case.
3. Since $$A = C$$, $$\theta = \frac{\pi}{4}$$, and $$\cos\theta = \sin\theta = \frac{\sqrt2}{2}$$.
   The formulas give $$A' = \frac{1}{2} + 1 + \frac{1}{2} = 2$$,
   $$C' = \frac{1}{2} - 1 + \frac{1}{2} = 0$$, $$D' = \frac{\sqrt2}{2} - \frac{\sqrt2}{2} = 0$$ and
   $$E' = -\frac{\sqrt2}{2} - \frac{\sqrt2}{2} = -\sqrt2$$.
4. The equation in $$u$$ and $$v$$ is $$2u^2 - \sqrt2\,v = 0$$, which is $$v = \sqrt2\,u^2$$. There
   is no square left to complete, so there is no slide.
5. The graph of $$y = \sqrt2\,x^2$$ is a parabola with its vertex at $$O$$, opening up. Turned by
   $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$, its axis lies along the $$v$$-axis, the line $$y = -x$$, and it opens up and
   to the left.
6. The point $$(1, \sqrt2)$$ is on $$y = \sqrt2\,x^2$$, and it turns to
   $$G_{\pi/4}(1, \sqrt2) = \big(\frac{\sqrt2}{2} - 1, \frac{\sqrt2}{2} + 1\big)$$. For this point
   $$x + y = \sqrt2$$ and $$x - y = -2$$. The first three terms of the equation are
   $$(x + y)^2 = 2$$, and the last two are $$x - y = -2$$, so the left side is $$0$$.

:::

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on recognising a curve from its equation, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-general-equation-circle}

Complete the squares in $$x^2 + y^2 + 4x - 6y - 12 = 0$$ and say what its graph is. Check your
answer with one point.

:::solution

Group the terms and complete each square (:ref{#quadratics/complete-square}):

```math
\begin{aligned}
(x^2 + 4x) + (y^2 - 6y) - 12 &= 0 &&\qquad \text{group} \\
(x + 2)^2 - 4 + (y - 3)^2 - 9 - 12 &= 0 &&\qquad \text{complete each square} \\
(x + 2)^2 + (y - 3)^2 &= 25 &&\qquad 4 + 9 + 12 = 25
\end{aligned}
```

By :ref{#coordinates/circle-equation}, this is the circle of radius $$5$$ about $$(-2, 3)$$. The
point $$(3, 3)$$ is $$5$$ to the right of the centre, and $$9 + 9 + 12 - 18 - 12 = 0$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-general-equation-sort}

Use the discriminant to name the family of each graph. Don't draw them.

1. $$3x^2 + 2xy + y^2 = 5$$
2. $$x^2 + 4xy + y^2 = 1$$
3. $$4x^2 - 4xy + y^2 + 3x = 0$$
4. $$xy + 2x = 7$$

:::solution

By :ref{#conic-discriminant}:

1. $$B^2 - 4AC = 4 - 12 = -8 < 0$$: an ellipse, a point or nothing. Putting $$x = 0$$ gives
   $$y^2 = 5$$, so the two points $$(0, \sqrt5)$$ and $$(0, -\sqrt5)$$ fit. A graph with two points
   is neither empty nor a single point, so it is an ellipse.
2. $$B^2 - 4AC = 16 - 4 = 12 > 0$$: a hyperbola or two crossing lines.
3. $$B^2 - 4AC = 16 - 16 = 0$$: a parabola or a degenerate case.
4. Here $$A = C = 0$$ and $$B = 1$$, so $$B^2 - 4AC = 1 > 0$$: a hyperbola or two crossing lines.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-general-equation-degenerate}

Find the graph of each equation.

1. $$x^2 - 4y^2 - 2x + 1 = 0$$
2. $$x^2 + 2y^2 + 2x + 3 = 0$$

:::solution

1. The $$x$$ terms complete to $$(x - 1)^2 - 1$$, so the equation is $$(x - 1)^2 - 4y^2 = 0$$. The
   left side factorises as $$(x - 1 - 2y)(x - 1 + 2y)$$, and a product is $$0$$ exactly when a
   factor is. So the graph is the two lines $$x - 2y = 1$$ and $$x + 2y = 1$$, crossing at
   $$(1, 0)$$. As a check, $$(3, 1)$$ is on the first line, and $$9 - 4 - 6 + 1 = 0$$.
2. The $$x$$ terms complete to $$(x + 1)^2 - 1$$, so the equation is $$(x + 1)^2 + 2y^2 = -2$$. The
   left side is never negative, so no point fits, and the graph is empty.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-general-equation-last-number}

For which numbers $$k$$ is the graph of $$x^2 + 4y^2 - 2x + k = 0$$ an ellipse, a single point, or
empty?

:::solution

Completing the square in $$x$$ gives $$(x - 1)^2 - 1 + 4y^2 + k = 0$$, so

```math
(x - 1)^2 + 4y^2 = 1 - k
```

The left side is never negative, and it is $$0$$ exactly at $$(1, 0)$$. So by the proof of
:ref{#no-xy-term}:

- for $$k < 1$$ the right side is positive, and the graph is an ellipse centred at $$(1, 0)$$
- for $$k = 1$$ it is the single point $$(1, 0)$$
- for $$k > 1$$ it is empty

For example, $$k = -3$$ gives $$\frac{(x - 1)^2}{4} + y^2 = 1$$, an ellipse with half-widths $$2$$
and $$1$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-general-equation-quarter-turn}

Show that the graph of $$3x^2 - 2xy + 3y^2 = 8$$ is an ellipse turned by $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$, and find
its half-widths. Check one point.

:::solution

Here $$A = C = 3$$ and $$B = -2$$, so :ref{#remove-xy} takes $$\theta = \frac{\pi}{4}$$, with
$$\cos\theta = \sin\theta = \frac{\sqrt2}{2}$$, so
$$\cos^2\theta = \sin^2\theta = \sin\theta\cos\theta = \frac{1}{2}$$. Then

```math
A' = \tfrac{3}{2} - 1 + \tfrac{3}{2} = 2 \qquad C' = \tfrac{3}{2} + 1 + \tfrac{3}{2} = 4
```

and the equation in $$u$$ and $$v$$ is $$2u^2 + 4v^2 = 8$$, or
$$\frac{u^2}{4} + \frac{v^2}{2} = 1$$. So the graph is the ellipse with half-widths $$2$$ and
$$\sqrt2$$ turned by $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$. The discriminant agrees:
$$4 - 36 = -32 = -4 \cdot 2 \cdot 4$$. As a check, the end $$(2, 0)$$ turns to $$(\sqrt2, \sqrt2)$$,
and $$6 - 4 + 6 = 8$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-general-equation-sixth-turn}

Find the angle $$\theta$$ that removes the $$xy$$ term from $$11x^2 + 10\sqrt3\,xy + y^2 = 16$$, and
show that the graph is a hyperbola. Check one point.

:::solution

Here $$A = 11$$, $$B = 10\sqrt3$$ and $$C = 1$$, so
$$\tan 2\theta = \frac{10\sqrt3}{11 - 1} = \sqrt3$$. The angle between $$0$$ and $$\pi$$ with that
tangent is $$\frac{\pi}{3}$$, so $$\theta = \frac{\pi}{6}$$, with $$\cos^2\theta = \frac{3}{4}$$,
$$\sin^2\theta = \frac{1}{4}$$ and $$\sin\theta\cos\theta = \frac{\sqrt3}{4}$$. By :ref{#remove-xy},

```math
\begin{aligned}
A' &= \tfrac{33}{4} + 10\sqrt3 \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt3}{4} + \tfrac{1}{4} = \tfrac{33 + 30 + 1}{4} = 16 \\
C' &= \tfrac{11}{4} - 10\sqrt3 \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt3}{4} + \tfrac{3}{4} = \tfrac{11 - 30 + 3}{4} = -4
\end{aligned}
```

The equation in $$u$$ and $$v$$ is $$16u^2 - 4v^2 = 16$$, or $$u^2 - \frac{v^2}{4} = 1$$: a
hyperbola with $$a = 1$$ and $$b = 2$$, turned by $$\frac{\pi}{6}$$. The discriminant agrees:
$$300 - 44 = 256 = -4 \cdot 16 \cdot (-4)$$. As a check, the vertex $$(1, 0)$$ turns to
$$\big(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}, \frac{1}{2}\big)$$, and
$$11 \cdot \frac{3}{4} + 10\sqrt3 \cdot \frac{\sqrt3}{4} + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{33 + 30 + 1}{4} = 16$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-general-equation-without-turning}

The proof of :ref{#conic-discriminant} showed that a turn keeps $$A + C$$, as well as $$B^2 - 4AC$$.
Use these two facts, without doing the turn, to find the numbers $$A'$$ and $$C'$$ for
$$5x^2 - 6xy + 5y^2 = 8$$ after the $$xy$$ term has been turned away. Compare with the guide.

:::solution

After the turn $$B' = 0$$, so

```math
A' + C' = A + C = 10 \qquad -4A'C' = B^2 - 4AC = 36 - 100 = -64
```

So $$A'C' = 16$$. Two numbers with sum $$10$$ and product $$16$$ are the solutions of
$$t^2 - 10t + 16 = 0$$ (:ref{#quadratics/vieta}), which factorises as $$(t - 2)(t - 8) = 0$$. So
$$A'$$ and $$C'$$ are $$2$$ and $$8$$ in some order, and with $$\theta = \frac{\pi}{4}$$ the guide
found $$A' = 2$$ and $$C' = 8$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-general-equation-always-ellipse}

Let $$A > 0$$ and $$B^2 - 4AC < 0$$. Prove that the graph of $$Ax^2 + Bxy + Cy^2 = 1$$ is an
ellipse, never a single point or empty.

:::solution

First, $$C > 0$$: we have $$4AC > B^2 \ge 0$$, so $$AC > 0$$, and $$A > 0$$. If $$B = 0$$, the
equation is $$Ax^2 + Cy^2 = 1$$ with both numbers positive, which is the ellipse
$$\frac{x^2}{1/A} + \frac{y^2}{1/C} = 1$$.

If $$B \ne 0$$, turn by the $$\theta$$ of :ref{#remove-xy}. The equation becomes
$$A'u^2 + C'v^2 = 1$$, since $$D = E = 0$$ gives $$D' = E' = 0$$. By :ref{#conic-discriminant},
$$-4A'C' = B^2 - 4AC < 0$$, so $$A'C' > 0$$ and the two have the same sign. Their sum is
$$A' + C' = A + C > 0$$, so both are positive. Then $$A'u^2 + C'v^2 = 1$$ is an ellipse, and by
:ref{#remove-xy} our graph is that ellipse turned by $$\theta$$, which is an ellipse
(:ref{#isometries/composite-isometry}).

:::
