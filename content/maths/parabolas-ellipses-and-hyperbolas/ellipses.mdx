Imagine a gardener marking out an oval flower bed. She knocks two pegs into the lawn, drops a loop
of string over them, and pulls it tight with a stick. Then she walks the stick round, keeping the
string taut, and the stick scratches a smooth oval into the soil. Pegs close together give a rounder
bed, pegs far apart a longer and thinner one, and with both pegs in the same hole she draws a
circle.

<GardenerString caption="Two pegs, a loop of string and a stick. Keeping the string tight, the stick traces an oval round the pegs." />

That oval is an **ellipse**. It is also the shape you get by stretching a circle evenly in one
direction, the stretched disc of the [Stretching one way](../area-and-scaling/stretching.mdx) guide,
so a pulled-out circle and a string round two pegs draw the same curve. In this guide we'll define
the ellipse by its equation, prove that the gardener's string draws it, and use the equation to see
how round it is and where a line meets it.

## The stretched circle

Start with the unit circle, and pull the plane out evenly: every point three times as far from the
$$y$$-axis, and then twice as far from the $$x$$-axis. That is the stretch along the axes
$$D_{3,2}(x, y) = (3x, 2y)$$ from :ref{#functions/axis-stretch}, and the circle becomes an oval
three across and two up. The equation changes in a way we can predict, because dividing by $$3$$ and
$$2$$ undoes the stretch.

Take the point $$(0.6, 0.8)$$ of the unit circle, which is on it because $$0.36 + 0.64 = 1$$. The
stretch sends it to $$(1.8, 1.6)$$, which no longer fits $$x^2 + y^2 = 1$$, since
$$3.24 + 2.56 = 5.8$$. Divide the coordinates by $$3$$ and $$2$$ first, and you are back at the
circle: $$\left(\frac{1.8}{3}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{1.6}{2}\right)^2 = 0.36 + 0.64 = 1$$. So the
stretched point fits $$\frac{x^2}{9} + \frac{y^2}{4} = 1$$.

<StretchedCircle caption="The unit circle (dashed) stretched 3 times across and 2 times up. The point (0.6, 0.8) lands on (1.8, 1.6), and the circle becomes the curve x²/9 + y²/4 = 1." />

:::theorem[The stretched circle]{#ellipse-equation}

Let $$a > 0$$ and $$b > 0$$. The stretch along the axes $$D_{a,b}(x, y) = (ax, by)$$ sends:

1. the unit circle onto the graph of $$\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$$
2. the disc of radius $$1$$ with centre $$O$$ onto the set of points with
   $$\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} \le 1$$

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellipse#Standard_parametric_representation"}

**Each point comes from exactly one point.** The point $$(x, y)$$ is $$D_{a,b}$$ of
$$\left(\frac{x}{a}, \frac{y}{b}\right)$$, since $$a \cdot \frac{x}{a} = x$$ and
$$b \cdot \frac{y}{b} = y$$. It comes from no other point: if $$D_{a,b}(u, v) = (x, y)$$, then
$$au = x$$ and $$bv = y$$, so $$u = \frac{x}{a}$$ and $$v = \frac{y}{b}$$. So $$(x, y)$$ is in the
image of a set exactly when $$\left(\frac{x}{a}, \frac{y}{b}\right)$$ is in the set.

**Part 1.** By :ref{#coordinates/circle-equation}, the point
$$\left(\frac{x}{a}, \frac{y}{b}\right)$$ is on the unit circle exactly when

```math
\begin{aligned}
\left(\frac{x}{a}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{y}{b}\right)^2 &= 1 && \text{the circle with centre } O \text{ and radius } 1 \\
\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} &= 1 && \text{square each fraction}
\end{aligned}
```

**Part 2.** The disc is the set of points $$(u, v)$$ at distance at most $$1$$ from $$O$$. By
:ref{#coordinates/distance-formula} that distance is $$\sqrt{u^2 + v^2}$$, and since square roots
keep order (:ref{#real-numbers/root-order}) and $$\sqrt{1} = 1$$, it is at most $$1$$ exactly when
$$u^2 + v^2 \le 1$$. The same two steps as in part 1, with $$\le$$ in place of $$=$$, turn this into
$$\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} \le 1$$.

:::

Part 1 is :ref{#functions/stretch-graph} for the unit circle: put $$\frac{x}{a}$$ and
$$\frac{y}{b}$$ in place of $$x$$ and $$y$$. By :ref{#functions/axis-stretch-two}, $$D_{a,b}$$ is
the stretch by $$a$$ away from the $$y$$-axis followed by the stretch by $$b$$ away from the
$$x$$-axis. So it is the pair of stretches of :ref{#area-and-scaling/ellipse}, with the $$y$$-axis
as the first line and the $$x$$-axis as the second.

So the stretched disc of that guide is the region $$\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} \le 1$$, and
its edge is the curve $$\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$$. Its area is $$\pi ab$$, by
:ref{#area-and-scaling/ellipse-area}. For $$\frac{x^2}{9} + \frac{y^2}{4} = 1$$ that is
$$6\pi \approx 18.85$$, six times the area $$\pi$$ of the disc it came from.

The stretch also gives a way to list every point of the curve. Every point of the unit circle is
$$(\cos t, \sin t)$$ for some angle $$t$$ (:ref{#trigonometry/unit-circle-point}), and the stretch
sends it to $$(a\cos t, b\sin t)$$.

:::proposition[Walking round the curve]{#ellipses-parametrisation}

Let $$a > 0$$ and $$b > 0$$. For every number $$t$$, the point $$(a\cos t, b\sin t)$$ is on the
graph of $$\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$$. Every point of that graph is
$$(a\cos t, b\sin t)$$ for exactly one $$t$$ with $$0 \le t < 2\pi$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellipse#Standard_parametric_representation"}

By :ref{#trigonometry/unit-circle-point}, the point $$(\cos t, \sin t)$$ is on the unit circle, and
every point of the unit circle is $$(\cos t, \sin t)$$ for exactly one $$t$$ with
$$0 \le t < 2\pi$$. By part 1 of :ref{#ellipse-equation}, $$D_{a,b}$$ sends the unit circle onto the
graph, and $$D_{a,b}(\cos t, \sin t) = (a\cos t, b\sin t)$$. Each point of the graph comes from
exactly one point of the circle, as the proof of :ref{#ellipse-equation} showed, so it comes from
exactly one $$t$$ too.

:::

For example, with $$a = 3$$, $$b = 2$$ and $$t = \frac{\pi}{3}$$, the point is
$$\left(3 \cdot \frac{1}{2}, 2 \cdot \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right) = (1.5, \sqrt3) \approx (1.5, 1.73)$$
(:ref{#trigonometry/sin-cos-30-60}). As a check, $$\frac{2.25}{9} + \frac{3}{4} = 0.25 + 0.75 = 1$$.
The number $$t$$ is the angle of the circle's point before the stretch, not the angle of the new
point seen from $$O$$. Here the ray from $$O$$ to $$(1.5, 1.73)$$ makes about $$49.1^\circ$$ with
the $$x$$-axis, not $$60^\circ$$.

## What an ellipse is

The curve $$\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$$ has its centre at $$O$$ and its widths along the
axes. A flower bed in a real garden can sit anywhere and point any way, but sliding or turning it
doesn't change its shape. So we count every moved copy as an ellipse too, in the same way as the
parabolas of :ref{#parabola-definition}.

:::definition[Ellipse as a curve]{#ellipse-definition}

An **ellipse** is the image (:ref{#isometries/image}) of the graph of

```math
\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1
```

under an isometry, for some numbers $$a > 0$$ and $$b > 0$$. For the graph itself:

- **Centre**: the origin $$O$$
- **Half-widths**: the numbers $$a$$ and $$b$$, also called the **semi-axes**
- **Axes**: the segment from $$(-a, 0)$$ to $$(a, 0)$$, of length $$2a$$, and the segment from
  $$(0, -b)$$ to $$(0, b)$$, of length $$2b$$. The longer one is the **major axis** and the shorter
  one the **minor axis**

For an ellipse moved by an isometry, the centre and axes are the images of these, and the
half-widths are the same two numbers $$a$$ and $$b$$.

:::

<EllipseParts caption="The ellipse x²/25 + y²/9 = 1. Its major axis runs 5 each way from O along the x-axis, and its minor axis 3 each way up and down the y-axis." />

An isometry keeps every distance (:ref{#isometries/isometry}), so a moved ellipse has axes of the
same lengths $$2a$$ and $$2b$$. A circle of radius $$r$$ is the ellipse with $$a = b = r$$. With
$$a = b = r$$ the equation becomes $$x^2 + y^2 = r^2$$, the circle with centre $$O$$ and radius
$$r$$ (:ref{#coordinates/circle-equation}), and every other circle of radius $$r$$ is a slide of
that one (:ref{#functions/slide-graph}).

When $$a < b$$ the ellipse is taller than it is wide. The flip $$S$$ in the line $$y = x$$ swaps the
letters $$x$$ and $$y$$ in an equation (:ref{#functions/flip-graph}), so it sends the graph of
$$\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$$ to the graph of $$\frac{y^2}{a^2} + \frac{x^2}{b^2} = 1$$,
which has the bigger number $$b^2$$ under $$x^2$$. For example,
$$\frac{x^2}{4} + \frac{y^2}{9} = 1$$ is the flip of $$\frac{x^2}{9} + \frac{y^2}{4} = 1$$.

A tall ellipse moved by an isometry $$G$$ is then the wide one moved by $$G \circ S$$, which is an
isometry too (:ref{#isometries/composite-isometry}). So every ellipse is a moved copy of one with
$$a \ge b$$. We call the graph of $$\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$$ with $$a \ge b > 0$$ an
ellipse in **standard position**.

## Sliding an ellipse

Moving an ellipse changes its equation in a way you can predict. By :ref{#functions/slide-graph},
the slide by $$(h, k)$$ replaces $$x$$ by $$x - h$$ and $$y$$ by $$y - k$$. So sliding
$$\frac{x^2}{9} + \frac{y^2}{4} = 1$$ by $$(2, -1)$$ gives

```math
\frac{(x - 2)^2}{9} + \frac{(y + 1)^2}{4} = 1
```

with centre $$(2, -1)$$. As a check, the point $$(5, -1)$$, three to the right of the new centre,
gives $$\frac{9}{9} + 0 = 1$$.

The signs are worth a second look. The bracket $$(x - 2)$$ means a slide 2 to the **right**, not the
left: the new curve does at $$x$$ what the old one did at $$x - 2$$, two steps earlier. In the same
way $$(y + 1)$$ is $$\big(y - (-1)\big)$$, a slide 1 down.

<SlidEllipse caption="The ellipse x²/9 + y²/4 = 1 (dashed) slid by (2, −1). Its centre moves from O to (2, −1), and it still reaches 3 across and 2 up from the centre." />

Multiplying out hides the centre. Multiply the slid equation by $$36$$ and expand, and it becomes
$$4x^2 + 9y^2 - 16x + 18y - 11 = 0$$. Given only that expanded form, completing the square in $$x$$
and in $$y$$ (:ref{#quadratics/complete-square}) brings the centre and half-widths back.

:::example[Recognising an ellipse]

Take $$4x^2 + 9y^2 - 16x + 18y - 11 = 0$$. Here $$x^2 - 4x = (x - 2)^2 - 4$$ and
$$y^2 + 2y = (y + 1)^2 - 1$$, so

```math
\begin{aligned}
4(x^2 - 4x) + 9(y^2 + 2y) &= 11 && \text{group the } x \text{ and } y \text{ terms, move } 11 \\
4\big((x - 2)^2 - 4\big) + 9\big((y + 1)^2 - 1\big) &= 11 && \text{complete each square} \\
4(x - 2)^2 + 9(y + 1)^2 &= 36 && \text{add } 16 + 9 \text{ to both sides} \\
\frac{(x - 2)^2}{9} + \frac{(y + 1)^2}{4} &= 1 && \text{divide both sides by } 36
\end{aligned}
```

So the curve is the ellipse with centre $$(2, -1)$$ and half-widths $$3$$ across and $$2$$ up. As a
check, the point $$(2, 1)$$ at the top gives $$16 + 9 - 32 + 18 - 11 = 0$$.

:::

The last step matters most. From $$4(x - 2)^2 + 9(y + 1)^2 = 36$$ it's tempting to read off
half-widths $$2$$ and $$3$$, from the $$4$$ and the $$9$$. Divide so that the right side is $$1$$:
then $$\frac{4}{36} = \frac{1}{9}$$ sits with $$(x - 2)^2$$ and gives the half-width $$3$$ across.
So the bigger number, $$9$$, goes with the narrower direction, up and down.

Two other things can happen when you complete the squares. If the right side comes out as $$0$$, as
in $$4(x - 2)^2 + 9(y + 1)^2 = 0$$, the only solution is the centre, since both squares must be
$$0$$. If it comes out negative, no point fits at all. The guide
[Recognising a curve from its equation](./general-equation.mdx) calls these degenerate cases
(:ref{#degenerate-conic}), and also shows how to deal with an $$xy$$ term, which appears when an
ellipse is turned (:ref{#remove-xy}).

## The two foci

Watch the gardener's string while the stick moves. The loop keeps its length, and the piece between
the two pegs keeps its length too. So the other two pieces, from the stick to each peg, always add
up to the same total. The bed is the set of points whose distances to two fixed points add up to a
fixed number, and the theorem below says that this set is an ellipse.

Here it is with numbers first. Take $$\frac{x^2}{25} + \frac{y^2}{9} = 1$$, so $$a = 5$$ and
$$b = 3$$, and put two pegs at $$(-4, 0)$$ and $$(4, 0)$$, where $$4 = \sqrt{25 - 9}$$:

- **The bottom point $$(0, -3)$$**: it is $$\sqrt{16 + 9} = 5$$ from each peg, and $$5 + 5 = 10$$
- **The end $$(5, 0)$$**: it is $$9$$ from $$(-4, 0)$$ and $$1$$ from $$(4, 0)$$, and $$9 + 1 = 10$$
- **The point $$(3, 2.4)$$**: it is on the curve, since
  $$\frac{9}{25} + \frac{5.76}{9} = 0.36 + 0.64 = 1$$. It is
  $$\sqrt{49 + 5.76} = \sqrt{54.76} = 7.4$$ from $$(-4, 0)$$ and
  $$\sqrt{1 + 5.76} = \sqrt{6.76} = 2.6$$ from $$(4, 0)$$, and $$7.4 + 2.6 = 10$$

Each time the total is $$10$$, which is $$2a$$, the length of the major axis.

<EllipseFoci caption="The ellipse x²/25 + y²/9 = 1 with its foci F₁ = (−4, 0) and F₂ = (4, 0). From P = (3, 2.4) the distances are 7.4 and 2.6, and from Q = (0, −3) they are 5 and 5. Both pairs add up to 10." />

:::theorem[The two foci]{#ellipse-foci}

Let $$a \ge b > 0$$, let $$c = \sqrt{a^2 - b^2}$$, and let $$F_1 = (-c, 0)$$ and $$F_2 = (c, 0)$$. A
point $$P$$ lies on the ellipse $$\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$$ exactly when

```math
d(P, F_1) + d(P, F_2) = 2a
```

The points $$F_1$$ and $$F_2$$ are the **foci** of the ellipse, and each one is a **focus**.

:::

The number $$c$$ exists because $$a \ge b$$ makes $$a^2 - b^2 \ge 0$$. Also $$c < a$$, since
$$c^2 = a^2 - b^2 < a^2$$ when $$b > 0$$, and square roots keep order. So the foci lie inside the
major axis, $$c$$ on each side of the centre.

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellipse#Standard_equation"}

Write $$P = (x, y)$$, and $$r_1 = d(P, F_1)$$ and $$r_2 = d(P, F_2)$$ for the two lengths of string.
By :ref{#coordinates/distance-formula},

```math
r_1^2 = (x + c)^2 + y^2 \qquad r_2^2 = (x - c)^2 + y^2
```

since $$x - (-c) = x + c$$. From $$c^2 = a^2 - b^2$$ we also have $$a^2 - b^2 = c^2$$ and
$$b^2 + c^2 = a^2$$, and we'll use both.

**Step 1: on the ellipse, find each distance.** Suppose $$P$$ is on the ellipse. Taking
$$\frac{x^2}{a^2}$$ from both sides of its equation and multiplying by $$b^2$$ gives
$$y^2 = b^2 - \frac{b^2x^2}{a^2}$$. Put that into $$r_2^2$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
r_2^2 &= x^2 - 2cx + c^2 + y^2 && \text{expand } (x - c)^2 \\
&= x^2 - 2cx + c^2 + b^2 - \frac{b^2x^2}{a^2} && \text{put in } y^2 \\
&= \frac{(a^2 - b^2)\,x^2}{a^2} - 2cx + (b^2 + c^2) && \text{collect the } x^2 \text{ terms} \\
&= \frac{c^2x^2}{a^2} - 2cx + a^2 && \text{since } a^2 - b^2 = c^2 \text{ and } b^2 + c^2 = a^2 \\
&= \left(a - \frac{cx}{a}\right)^2 && \text{a perfect square}
\end{aligned}
```

The last line checks by expanding:
$$\left(a - \frac{cx}{a}\right)^2 = a^2 - 2cx + \frac{c^2x^2}{a^2}$$. The same steps with $$x + c$$
in place of $$x - c$$ change only the sign of $$2cx$$, and give
$$r_1^2 = \left(a + \frac{cx}{a}\right)^2$$.

Now take square roots, which needs the signs. Since $$\frac{y^2}{b^2} \ge 0$$, the equation gives
$$\frac{x^2}{a^2} \le 1$$, so $$x^2 \le a^2$$. So $$|x| \le a$$: if $$|x|$$ were bigger than $$a$$,
then $$x^2 = |x|^2$$ would be bigger than $$a^2$$. Then

```math
\left|\frac{cx}{a}\right| = \frac{c\,|x|}{a} \le \frac{c \cdot a}{a} = c < a
```

So $$a - \frac{cx}{a}$$ and $$a + \frac{cx}{a}$$ are both positive. Two numbers that are not
negative and have equal squares are equal (:ref{#real-numbers/squaring-sides}), so

```math
r_1 = a + \frac{cx}{a} \qquad r_2 = a - \frac{cx}{a}
```

Adding, $$r_1 + r_2 = 2a$$.

**Step 2: a total of 2a puts P on the ellipse.** Suppose $$r_1 + r_2 = 2a$$. Take the two squared
distances away from each other:

```math
\begin{aligned}
r_1^2 - r_2^2 &= (x + c)^2 - (x - c)^2 && \text{the } y^2 \text{ terms cancel} \\
&= 4cx && \text{expand both squares}
\end{aligned}
```

The left side is $$(r_1 - r_2)(r_1 + r_2) = 2a(r_1 - r_2)$$, a difference of two squares. So
$$2a(r_1 - r_2) = 4cx$$, and dividing by $$2a$$, which is positive, gives
$$r_1 - r_2 = \frac{2cx}{a}$$. Now we know both the sum and the difference of $$r_1$$ and $$r_2$$.
Adding them gives $$2r_1 = 2a + \frac{2cx}{a}$$, so $$r_1 = a + \frac{cx}{a}$$. Square both sides
and use the distance formula for $$r_1^2$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
(x + c)^2 + y^2 &= \left(a + \frac{cx}{a}\right)^2 && \text{both sides are } r_1^2 \\
x^2 + 2cx + c^2 + y^2 &= a^2 + 2cx + \frac{c^2x^2}{a^2} && \text{expand both squares} \\
x^2 - \frac{c^2x^2}{a^2} + y^2 &= a^2 - c^2 && \text{take } 2cx + c^2 + \tfrac{c^2x^2}{a^2} \text{ from both sides} \\
\frac{(a^2 - c^2)\,x^2}{a^2} + y^2 &= a^2 - c^2 && \text{collect the } x^2 \text{ terms} \\
\frac{b^2x^2}{a^2} + y^2 &= b^2 && \text{since } a^2 - c^2 = b^2 \\
\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} &= 1 && \text{divide both sides by } b^2
\end{aligned}
```

So $$P$$ is on the ellipse.

:::

Step 2 squares only an equation whose two sides are both $$r_1^2$$, so it can't let in a false
solution, the danger that :ref{#real-numbers/squaring-sides} warns about. Step 1 also gives a
formula worth keeping: on the ellipse, the distance from $$P = (x, y)$$ to $$F_2$$ is
$$a - \frac{cx}{a}$$. For $$P = (3, 2.4)$$ on $$\frac{x^2}{25} + \frac{y^2}{9} = 1$$ that is
$$5 - \frac{4 \cdot 3}{5} = 2.6$$, as we found with the distance formula.

A circle fits the theorem too. With $$a = b$$, $$c = 0$$ and both foci sit at the centre $$O$$, so
the theorem says $$2\,d(P, O) = 2a$$: the points at distance $$a$$ from $$O$$.

For a moved ellipse, the foci move with it. If an isometry $$G$$ carries the ellipse $$C$$ in
standard position to $$G(C)$$, the **foci** of $$G(C)$$ are $$G(F_1)$$ and $$G(F_2)$$. They lie on
its major axis, $$c$$ on each side of its centre, because $$G$$ sends segments to segments and keeps
distances (:ref{#isometries/isometry-segments}).

:::corollary[The foci of a moved ellipse]{#ellipses-moved-foci}

Let $$C$$ be the ellipse $$\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$$ with $$a \ge b > 0$$ and foci
$$F_1$$ and $$F_2$$, and let $$G$$ be an isometry. A point $$Q$$ lies on $$G(C)$$ exactly when

```math
d\big(Q, G(F_1)\big) + d\big(Q, G(F_2)\big) = 2a
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellipse#Definition_as_locus_of_points"}

If $$Q$$ is on $$G(C)$$, then $$Q = G(P)$$ for a point $$P$$ of $$C$$ (:ref{#isometries/image}).
Since $$G$$ keeps distances, $$d(Q, G(F_1)) = d(P, F_1)$$ and $$d(Q, G(F_2)) = d(P, F_2)$$, and
these add up to $$2a$$ by :ref{#ellipse-foci}.

Conversely, suppose the two distances from $$Q$$ add up to $$2a$$. By
:ref{#isometries/isometry-inverse}, $$G$$ has an inverse $$G^{-1}$$, which is an isometry too. Let
$$P = G^{-1}(Q)$$.

Since $$G^{-1}$$ sends $$Q$$ to $$P$$ and $$G(F_1)$$ to $$F_1$$, keeping distances,
$$d(P, F_1) = d(Q, G(F_1))$$, and in the same way for $$F_2$$. So $$d(P, F_1) + d(P, F_2) = 2a$$,
and $$P$$ is on $$C$$ by :ref{#ellipse-foci}. Then $$Q = G(P)$$ is on $$G(C)$$.

:::

For example, the ellipse $$\frac{(x - 2)^2}{9} + \frac{(y + 1)^2}{4} = 1$$ is the slide by
$$(2, -1)$$ of one with $$a = 3$$, $$b = 2$$ and $$c = \sqrt{9 - 4} = \sqrt5 \approx 2.24$$. So its
foci are the slid points $$(2 - \sqrt5, -1)$$ and $$(2 + \sqrt5, -1)$$, and its points are those
whose distances to these two add up to $$6$$.

## Drawing an ellipse with string

The theorem explains the gardener's method. She puts the pegs at the foci, $$2c$$ apart, and uses a
loop of length $$2a + 2c$$. While the string is tight it makes a triangle with the pegs, and the
side between the pegs takes $$2c$$ of the loop. So the two sides from the stick to the pegs add up
to $$2a$$, and by :ref{#ellipse-foci} the stick is on the ellipse at every moment.

Turn that round to plan a bed $$5$$ m long and $$3$$ m wide. The half-widths are $$a = 2.5$$ and
$$b = 1.5$$, so $$c = \sqrt{6.25 - 2.25} = \sqrt4 = 2$$. The pegs go $$4$$ m apart, centred on the
long line through the middle of the bed, and the loop is $$5 + 4 = 9$$ m long.

As a check, at the far end of the bed the stick is $$4.5$$ m from one peg and $$0.5$$ m from the
other: with the $$4$$ m between the pegs, that uses all $$9$$ m. The bed has area
$$\pi \cdot 2.5 \cdot 1.5 \approx 11.78$$ square metres (:ref{#area-and-scaling/ellipse-area}).

## How round an ellipse is

Moving the pegs is how the gardener controls the shape. With the pegs close together the bed comes
out nearly round, and with the pegs near the ends of the bed it comes out long and thin. So the
distance of each focus from the centre, measured as a share of the half-width $$a$$, says how round
the ellipse is.

:::definition[Eccentricity of an ellipse]{#ellipse-eccentricity}

The **eccentricity** of an ellipse with half-widths $$a \ge b > 0$$ is

```math
e = \frac{c}{a} = \frac{\sqrt{a^2 - b^2}}{a}
```

Since $$0 \le c < a$$, the eccentricity satisfies $$0 \le e < 1$$.

:::

For $$\frac{x^2}{25} + \frac{y^2}{9} = 1$$, $$e = \frac{4}{5} = 0.8$$. The eccentricity is $$0$$
exactly when $$c = 0$$, that is when $$a = b$$, so the circles are the ellipses with $$e = 0$$. In
the other direction, $$e$$ fixes the ratio of the half-widths: dividing $$b^2 = a^2 - c^2$$ by
$$a^2$$ gives $$\frac{b^2}{a^2} = 1 - e^2$$, so $$\frac{b}{a} = \sqrt{1 - e^2}$$. For $$e = 0.8$$
that is $$\sqrt{0.36} = 0.6 = \frac{3}{5}$$, and as $$e$$ gets near $$1$$, $$\frac{b}{a}$$ gets near
$$0$$ and the ellipse gets long and thin.

Ellipses turn up in the sky. The planets move round the Sun on ellipses, with the Sun at one focus.
This is a fact of physics, which Kepler found from careful measurements of the planets and Newton
later explained with his law of gravity, and it's taken as given here. Two orbits show how different
the eccentricity can be:

- **The Earth**: $$e$$ is about $$0.0167$$, and $$a$$ is about $$149.6$$ million km. The Sun sits
  about $$ea \approx 2.5$$ million km from the centre of the orbit. But $$\frac{b}{a}$$ is about
  $$0.99986$$, so on a drawing with $$a = 10$$ cm, $$b$$ would be shorter by about $$0.01$$ mm,
  while the Sun would sit about $$1.7$$ mm from the centre
- **Halley's comet**: $$e$$ is about $$0.967$$, so $$\frac{b}{a}$$ is about $$0.25$$, and the orbit
  is about four times as long as it is wide. At its closest the comet comes to about $$0.59$$ times
  the Earth's distance from the Sun, and at its furthest it is about $$35$$ times as far

::see-also{src="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth%27s_orbit"}

The eccentricity comes back in the guide [Slicing a cone](./slicing-a-cone.mdx), where one rule with
a single number $$e$$ draws ellipses, parabolas and hyperbolas (:ref{#focus-directrix-e}).

## Where a line meets an ellipse

To find where a line meets an ellipse, use the method for a line and a circle
(:ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/line-circle}). Put the line's equation into the ellipse's, and you
get a quadratic in one letter. Its solutions are the meeting points, and its discriminant counts
them (:ref{#quadratics/root-count}).

:::example[Parallel lines and an ellipse]

Take the ellipse $$\frac{x^2}{4} + y^2 = 1$$, or $$x^2 + 4y^2 = 4$$ after multiplying by $$4$$, and
the lines $$x + 2y = k$$ for different numbers $$k$$.

**The line $$x + 2y = 2$$.** Here $$x = 2 - 2y$$, and putting that into the ellipse gives

```math
\begin{aligned}
(2 - 2y)^2 + 4y^2 &= 4 && \text{put in } x = 2 - 2y \\
4 - 8y + 4y^2 + 4y^2 &= 4 && \text{expand the square} \\
8y^2 - 8y &= 0 && \text{take } 4 \text{ from both sides} \\
8y(y - 1) &= 0 && \text{factor}
\end{aligned}
```

So $$y = 0$$ or $$y = 1$$, and the line meets the ellipse at $$(2, 0)$$ and $$(0, 1)$$.

**Every line $$x + 2y = k$$.** Now $$x = k - 2y$$, and the same steps give

```math
\begin{aligned}
(k - 2y)^2 + 4y^2 &= 4 && \text{put in } x = k - 2y \\
8y^2 - 4ky + (k^2 - 4) &= 0 && \text{expand and collect}
\end{aligned}
```

Its discriminant (:ref{#quadratics/discriminant}) is

```math
(-4k)^2 - 4 \cdot 8 \cdot (k^2 - 4) = 16k^2 - 32k^2 + 128 = 16(8 - k^2)
```

This is positive when $$k^2 < 8$$, zero when $$k = \pm 2\sqrt2 \approx \pm 2.83$$, and negative when
$$k^2 > 8$$. So the line crosses the ellipse twice when $$-2\sqrt2 < k < 2\sqrt2$$, meets it once at
$$k = \pm 2\sqrt2$$, and misses it otherwise. At $$k = 2\sqrt2$$ the one solution is
$$y = \frac{4k}{16} = \frac{\sqrt2}{2}$$, and then $$x = 2\sqrt2 - \sqrt2 = \sqrt2$$. As a check,
$$(\sqrt2)^2 + 4 \cdot \frac{1}{2} = 2 + 2 = 4$$.

:::

<EllipseLines caption="The ellipse x²/4 + y² = 1 and three of the lines x + 2y = k. The blue line (k = 2) crosses it twice, the green line (k = 2√2) touches it at (√2, √2/2), and the orange line (k = 3.5) misses it." />

As for a circle (:ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/tangent-line}), a line that meets an ellipse in
exactly one point is called a **tangent** to it. So $$x + 2y = 2\sqrt2$$ is the tangent to
$$\frac{x^2}{4} + y^2 = 1$$ at $$\left(\sqrt2, \frac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)$$.

## Ellipses come in different shapes

All parabolas have the same shape: each one is a zoom of $$y = x^2$$ moved into place
(:ref{#parabola-zoom}). Ellipses are different. A long thin oval and a round one can't be zoomed
into each other, because a zoom enlarges every direction by the same factor. What a zoom keeps is
the ratio $$\frac{b}{a}$$ of the half-widths.

:::proposition[Zooming an ellipse]{#ellipses-zoom}

Let $$a, b, r > 0$$. The zoom by $$r$$ about $$O$$ sends the graph of
$$\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$$ onto the graph of

```math
\frac{x^2}{(ra)^2} + \frac{y^2}{(rb)^2} = 1
```

So it keeps the ratio $$\frac{b}{a}$$ and the eccentricity. Two ellipses in standard position whose
half-widths have the same ratio are similar.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Similarity_(geometry)#Similar_curves"}

The zoom by $$r$$ about $$O$$ sends $$(x, y)$$ to $$(rx, ry)$$ (:ref{#coordinates/origin-zoom}),
which is the stretch along the axes $$D_{r,r}$$. By :ref{#functions/stretch-graph} it sends the
graph to the graph of

```math
\begin{aligned}
\frac{(x/r)^2}{a^2} + \frac{(y/r)^2}{b^2} &= 1 && \text{put } \tfrac{x}{r}, \tfrac{y}{r} \text{ in place of } x, y \\
\frac{x^2}{(ra)^2} + \frac{y^2}{(rb)^2} &= 1 && \text{since } r^2a^2 = (ra)^2 \text{ and } r^2b^2 = (rb)^2
\end{aligned}
```

The new ratio is $$\frac{rb}{ra} = \frac{b}{a}$$, and since $$e = \sqrt{1 - (b/a)^2}$$, the
eccentricity is kept too. Now take two ellipses in standard position with half-widths $$a, b$$ and
$$a', b'$$, where $$\frac{b'}{a'} = \frac{b}{a}$$. Put $$r = \frac{a'}{a}$$. Then $$ra = a'$$ and
$$rb = \frac{a'b}{a} = b'$$, so the zoom by $$r$$ sends the first ellipse exactly onto the second,
and they are similar (:ref{#area-and-scaling/similar}).

:::

For example, $$\frac{x^2}{25} + \frac{y^2}{9} = 1$$ and $$\frac{x^2}{100} + \frac{y^2}{36} = 1$$
both have $$\frac{b}{a} = 0.6$$, and the zoom by $$2$$ sends the first onto the second. But
$$\frac{x^2}{4} + y^2 = 1$$ has $$\frac{b}{a} = 0.5$$, so no zoom about $$O$$ sends it onto either
of them. The stronger statement, that ellipses with different ratios are not similar at all, is true
too, but we take it from the picture here.

## Reflecting off an ellipse

An ellipse also has a mirror property, like the parabola's (:ref{#parabola-reflection}). Picture a
room whose wall is an ellipse. A sound sent out from one focus bounces off the wall and passes
through the other focus, whichever way it starts. Such rooms are called whispering galleries: stand
at one focus and whisper, and a friend standing at the other focus hears you clearly.

:::remark[The reflecting property]{#ellipses-reflection}

A ray from one focus of an ellipse that reflects off the ellipse passes through the other focus. The
proof uses the tangent line at the point where the ray meets the ellipse, as in
:ref{#parabola-reflection}, and is not given here.

::see-also{src="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellipse#Focus-to-focus_reflection_property"}

:::

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on ellipses, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-ellipses-read-off}

The ellipse $$\frac{(x + 1)^2}{16} + \frac{(y - 2)^2}{49} = 1$$ is drawn on a grid. Find its centre,
its half-widths, and which way its major axis runs. Then find its foci, its eccentricity and its
area.

:::solution

By :ref{#functions/slide-graph}, $$x + 1 = x - (-1)$$ and $$y - 2$$ mean a slide by $$(-1, 2)$$: 1
to the left and 2 up. So the centre is $$(-1, 2)$$. The half-widths are $$\sqrt{16} = 4$$ across and
$$\sqrt{49} = 7$$ up, so the major axis is vertical, of length $$14$$, and the minor axis is
horizontal, of length $$8$$.

The curve is the slide by $$(-1, 2)$$ of $$\frac{x^2}{16} + \frac{y^2}{49} = 1$$, which is the flip
in $$y = x$$ of $$\frac{x^2}{49} + \frac{y^2}{16} = 1$$ (:ref{#functions/flip-graph}). That one is
in standard position with $$a = 7$$, $$b = 4$$ and $$c = \sqrt{49 - 16} = \sqrt{33} \approx 5.74$$,
with foci $$(\pm\sqrt{33}, 0)$$. The flip sends them to $$(0, \pm\sqrt{33})$$, and the slide to
$$(-1, 2 + \sqrt{33}) \approx (-1, 7.74)$$ and $$(-1, 2 - \sqrt{33}) \approx (-1, -3.74)$$, by
:ref{#ellipses-moved-foci}.

The eccentricity is $$\frac{\sqrt{33}}{7} \approx 0.82$$ (:ref{#ellipse-eccentricity}), and the area
is $$\pi \cdot 7 \cdot 4 = 28\pi \approx 87.96$$ (:ref{#area-and-scaling/ellipse-area}). As a check,
the point $$(3, 2)$$ at the right end of the minor axis is $$\sqrt{4^2 + 33} = \sqrt{49} = 7$$ from
each focus, and $$7 + 7 = 14 = 2a$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-ellipses-check-point}

Take the ellipse $$\frac{x^2}{169} + \frac{y^2}{25} = 1$$.

1. Find $$c$$, the foci and the eccentricity.
2. Check that $$P = \left(12, \frac{25}{13}\right)$$ is on the ellipse.
3. Find the distance from $$P$$ to each focus with the distance formula, and check that they add up
   to $$2a$$. Compare them with $$a + \frac{cx}{a}$$ and $$a - \frac{cx}{a}$$ from the proof of
   :ref{#ellipse-foci}.

:::solution

1. Here $$a = 13$$ and $$b = 5$$, so $$c = \sqrt{169 - 25} = \sqrt{144} = 12$$. The foci are
   $$(-12, 0)$$ and $$(12, 0)$$, and $$e = \frac{12}{13} \approx 0.92$$, a long thin ellipse.
2. $$\frac{144}{169} + \frac{625}{169} \cdot \frac{1}{25} = \frac{144}{169} + \frac{25}{169} = 1$$.
3. $$P$$ is straight above $$(12, 0)$$, so its distance to that focus is $$\frac{25}{13}$$. Its
   distance to $$(-12, 0)$$ is

   ```math
   \sqrt{24^2 + \left(\tfrac{25}{13}\right)^2} = \sqrt{\tfrac{97344 + 625}{169}} = \sqrt{\tfrac{97969}{169}} = \tfrac{313}{13}
   ```

   since $$313^2 = 97969$$. The total is $$\frac{25 + 313}{13} = \frac{338}{13} = 26 = 2a$$. The
   formulas agree: $$13 - \frac{12 \cdot 12}{13} = \frac{169 - 144}{13} = \frac{25}{13}$$ and
   $$13 + \frac{144}{13} = \frac{313}{13}$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-ellipses-complete-square}

Complete the squares to find what each equation draws.

1. $$9x^2 + 4y^2 + 54x - 8y + 49 = 0$$
2. $$x^2 + 4y^2 - 2x + 1 = 0$$

:::solution

1. Here $$x^2 + 6x = (x + 3)^2 - 9$$ and $$y^2 - 2y = (y - 1)^2 - 1$$
   (:ref{#quadratics/complete-square}), so

   ```math
   \begin{aligned}
   9(x^2 + 6x) + 4(y^2 - 2y) &= -49 && \text{group the terms, move } 49 \\
   9\big((x + 3)^2 - 9\big) + 4\big((y - 1)^2 - 1\big) &= -49 && \text{complete each square} \\
   9(x + 3)^2 + 4(y - 1)^2 &= 36 && \text{add } 81 + 4 \text{ to both sides} \\
   \frac{(x + 3)^2}{4} + \frac{(y - 1)^2}{9} &= 1 && \text{divide both sides by } 36
   \end{aligned}
   ```

   This is the ellipse with centre $$(-3, 1)$$, half-width $$2$$ across and $$3$$ up, so it is
   taller than it is wide. As a check, the top point $$(-3, 4)$$ gives
   $$81 + 64 - 162 - 32 + 49 = 0$$.

2. Here $$x^2 - 2x + 1 = (x - 1)^2$$ and $$4y^2 = (2y)^2$$, so the equation is
   $$(x - 1)^2 + (2y)^2 = 0$$. By part 2 of :ref{#real-numbers/squares-positive}, $$x - 1 = 0$$ and
   $$2y = 0$$. So the only point is $$(1, 0)$$: a degenerate case, not an ellipse.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-ellipses-garden}

1. A gardener wants an oval bed $$6$$ m long and $$4$$ m wide. How far apart should the pegs be, how
   long should the loop be, and what area will the bed have?
2. Another gardener has a loop $$16$$ m long and puts the pegs $$6$$ m apart. How long and how wide
   is the bed?
3. With the pegs $$6$$ m apart, why can't a loop $$10$$ m long draw anything?

:::solution

1. The half-widths are $$a = 3$$ and $$b = 2$$, so $$c = \sqrt{9 - 4} = \sqrt5$$. The pegs go
   $$2\sqrt5 \approx 4.47$$ m apart, and the loop is $$2a + 2c = 6 + 2\sqrt5 \approx 10.47$$ m. The
   area is $$\pi \cdot 3 \cdot 2 = 6\pi \approx 18.85$$ square metres
   (:ref{#area-and-scaling/ellipse-area}).
2. Now $$2c = 6$$, so $$c = 3$$, and $$2a = 16 - 6 = 10$$, so $$a = 5$$. Then
   $$b = \sqrt{25 - 9} = 4$$, and the bed is $$10$$ m long and $$8$$ m wide.
3. The loop would leave $$10 - 6 = 4$$ m for the two pieces from the stick to the pegs. But by the
   triangle inequality (:ref{#distance-and-angles/distance-rules}), those two pieces together are at
   least the $$6$$ m between the pegs. So the string can't go round the stick and both pegs at all.
   In the theorem this is the condition $$c < a$$, which fails here because $$a = 2$$ and $$c = 3$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-ellipses-slip}

A friend says that the foci of $$\frac{x^2}{9} + \frac{y^2}{25} = 1$$ are $$(4, 0)$$ and
$$(-4, 0)$$, because $$\sqrt{25 - 9} = 4$$. Test the claim with the points $$(0, 5)$$ and $$(3, 0)$$
of the ellipse, and find the correct foci.

:::solution

From $$(0, 5)$$ the distances to $$(\pm 4, 0)$$ are both $$\sqrt{16 + 25} = \sqrt{41}$$, a total of
$$2\sqrt{41} \approx 12.81$$. From $$(3, 0)$$ they are $$1$$ and $$7$$, a total of $$8$$. The totals
differ, so by :ref{#ellipses-moved-foci} these points can't be the foci.

The bigger number, $$25$$, is under $$y^2$$, so the major axis runs up the $$y$$-axis, with
$$a = 5$$. The ellipse is the flip in $$y = x$$ of $$\frac{x^2}{25} + \frac{y^2}{9} = 1$$
(:ref{#functions/flip-graph}), whose foci are $$(\pm 4, 0)$$. The flip swaps coordinates, so the
foci are $$(0, 4)$$ and $$(0, -4)$$.

As a check, from $$(0, 5)$$ the distances are $$1$$ and $$9$$, and from $$(3, 0)$$ they are both
$$\sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$$. Both totals are $$10 = 2a$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-ellipses-line}

For which numbers $$k$$ does the line $$y = x + k$$ cross the ellipse
$$\frac{x^2}{9} + \frac{y^2}{4} = 1$$ twice, touch it once, or miss it? Find the point where it
touches when $$k > 0$$.

:::solution

Multiplying by $$36$$, the ellipse is $$4x^2 + 9y^2 = 36$$. Put in $$y = x + k$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
4x^2 + 9(x + k)^2 &= 36 && \text{put in } y = x + k \\
13x^2 + 18kx + (9k^2 - 36) &= 0 && \text{expand and collect}
\end{aligned}
```

The discriminant is

```math
(18k)^2 - 4 \cdot 13 \cdot (9k^2 - 36) = 324k^2 - 468k^2 + 1872 = 144(13 - k^2)
```

By :ref{#quadratics/root-count}, the line crosses twice when $$k^2 < 13$$, that is
$$-\sqrt{13} < k < \sqrt{13}$$ (about $$\pm 3.61$$), touches once when $$k = \pm\sqrt{13}$$, and
misses when $$k^2 > 13$$.

At $$k = \sqrt{13}$$ the one solution is
$$x = -\frac{18k}{2 \cdot 13} = -\frac{9\sqrt{13}}{13} = -\frac{9}{\sqrt{13}} \approx -2.50$$, and
$$y = x + k = \frac{-9 + 13}{\sqrt{13}} = \frac{4}{\sqrt{13}} \approx 1.11$$. As a check,
$$4 \cdot \frac{81}{13} + 9 \cdot \frac{16}{13} = \frac{324 + 144}{13} = \frac{468}{13} = 36$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-ellipses-orbit}

Let $$P = (x, y)$$ move round the ellipse $$\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$$ with
$$a > b > 0$$, and let $$F_2 = (c, 0)$$.

1. Use the formula $$d(P, F_2) = a - \frac{cx}{a}$$ from the proof of :ref{#ellipse-foci} to show
   that $$P$$ is closest to $$F_2$$ at $$(a, 0)$$, at distance $$a - c$$, and furthest at
   $$(-a, 0)$$, at distance $$a + c$$.
2. The Earth's orbit has $$a$$ about $$149.6$$ million km and $$e$$ about $$0.0167$$, with the Sun
   at a focus. Find the Earth's closest and furthest distances from the Sun.

:::solution

1. Here $$c > 0$$ because $$a > b$$. Every point of the ellipse has $$-a \le x \le a$$, as the proof
   showed. Multiplying by $$\frac{c}{a} > 0$$ gives $$-c \le \frac{cx}{a} \le c$$, so

   ```math
   a - c \le a - \frac{cx}{a} \le a + c
   ```

   The distance is $$a - c$$ exactly when $$x = a$$, and the only point of the ellipse with
   $$x = a$$ is $$(a, 0)$$, since then $$\frac{y^2}{b^2} = 0$$. In the same way the distance is
   $$a + c$$ exactly at $$(-a, 0)$$.

2. Here $$c = ea \approx 0.0167 \times 149.6 \approx 2.50$$ million km. So the closest distance is
   about $$149.6 - 2.5 = 147.1$$ million km and the furthest about $$149.6 + 2.5 = 152.1$$ million
   km. The two differ by $$2c$$, about $$5$$ million km, even though the orbit looks like a circle.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-ellipses-longest}

Let $$P$$ and $$Q$$ be any two points of the ellipse $$\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$$ with
$$a \ge b > 0$$. Prove that $$d(P, Q) \le 2a$$, and find two points of the ellipse that are exactly
$$2a$$ apart.

:::solution

Let $$F_1$$ and $$F_2$$ be the foci. By the triangle inequality
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/distance-rules}), going from $$P$$ to $$Q$$ by way of a focus is never
shorter than going straight:

```math
d(P, Q) \le d(P, F_1) + d(F_1, Q) \qquad d(P, Q) \le d(P, F_2) + d(F_2, Q)
```

Adding the two and grouping the distances from each point,

```math
2\,d(P, Q) \le \big(d(P, F_1) + d(P, F_2)\big) + \big(d(Q, F_1) + d(Q, F_2)\big) = 2a + 2a
```

by :ref{#ellipse-foci}, used once for $$P$$ and once for $$Q$$. Dividing by $$2$$,
$$d(P, Q) \le 2a$$.

The ends of the major axis, $$(-a, 0)$$ and $$(a, 0)$$, are on the ellipse and exactly $$2a$$ apart.
So the major axis is the longest segment that fits between two points of the ellipse.

:::
