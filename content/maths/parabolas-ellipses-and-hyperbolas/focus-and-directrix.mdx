  A parabola as the points equally far from a point and a line, the focus of every parabola, and why
  a parabolic dish sends every straight-on ray to its focus.
---

Imagine a satellite dish on a roof, pointed at a satellite far out in space. Its signal arrives as a
bundle of parallel rays, and each ray strikes the bowl at a different place. Yet the small receiver
on its arm in front of the bowl picks up all of them. The bowl bounces every ray to that one spot.

<DishFocus caption="A dish seen from the side, pointed straight up. Rays of signal come straight down, hit the bowl at different places, and all bounce back up through the receiver." />

The cross-section of the bowl is a parabola, and the spot where the rays meet is its **focus**. The
focus comes with a partner line, the **directrix**, and together they describe the parabola without
any equation at all. In this guide we'll find the focus and directrix of every parabola, and then
prove that a parabola sends every straight-on ray to its focus.

## A point and a line

Pick a point $$F$$ and a line $$L$$ that misses it. Some points of the plane are closer to $$F$$
than to $$L$$, and some are closer to $$L$$. Between the two kinds lies a curve of points that are
equally far from both, and that curve turns out to be a parabola.

Here it is with numbers. Take $$F = (0, 1)$$ and let $$L$$ be the line $$y = -1$$. The distance from
a point to $$L$$ is the length of the perpendicular dropped onto $$L$$
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/point-line-distance}), and for a horizontal line that is how far above or
below it the point sits. So:

- $$(0, 0)$$ is $$1$$ from $$F$$ and $$1$$ above $$L$$
- $$(2, 1)$$ is $$2$$ from $$F$$, straight across, and $$2$$ above $$L$$
- $$(4, 4)$$ is $$\sqrt{4^2 + 3^2} = 5$$ from $$F$$ and $$5$$ above $$L$$

The point $$(-2, 1)$$ works in the same way as $$(2, 1)$$. All four points lie on the graph of
$$y = \frac{x^2}{4}$$: for example $$\frac{4^2}{4} = 4$$.

To find more such points, draw circles about $$F$$ and lines parallel to $$L$$. The circle of radius
$$r$$ about $$F$$ holds the points $$r$$ from $$F$$, and the line $$r$$ above $$L$$ holds the points
$$r$$ from $$L$$. So wherever the two meet, the point is $$r$$ from both.

<EqualDistances caption="F = (0, 1) and L is the line y = −1. Each circle about F meets the dashed line of its colour at points that are r from F and r from L. The dots all lie on the faint curve y = x²/4." />

:::definition[Focus and directrix]{#focus-directrix}

Let $$F$$ be a point and $$L$$ a line that does not pass through $$F$$. The **curve with focus**
$$F$$ **and directrix** $$L$$ is the set of points $$P$$ with

```math
d(P, F) = d(P, L)
```

where $$d(P, L)$$ is the distance from $$P$$ to the line $$L$$
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/point-line-distance}). The point $$F$$ is the **focus** and the line
$$L$$ is the **directrix**.

:::

The word "focus" is Latin for "fireplace": a parabolic mirror facing the sun gathers its heat at
that point. The circles and lines in the picture are one way to draw the curve point by point, and
the directrix is the line they are measured from.

## The focus of y = x²/(4p)

The four points in [A point and a line](#a-point-and-a-line) all fitted $$y = \frac{x^2}{4}$$. The
reason shows up when you write both distances with the distance formula and square them: the $$y^2$$
terms cancel and leave a parabola. The same happens with the focus at any height $$p$$ above the
origin and the directrix the same distance below it.

:::theorem[The focus of y = x²/(4p)]{#focus-parabola}

Let $$p \ne 0$$, let $$F = (0, p)$$, and let $$L$$ be the line $$y = -p$$. A point lies on the curve
with focus $$F$$ and directrix $$L$$ exactly when it lies on the graph of

```math
y = \frac{x^2}{4p}
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parabola#Axis_of_symmetry_parallel_to_the_y_axis"}

Let $$P = (x, y)$$. By :ref{#coordinates/distance-formula}, $$d(P, F) = \sqrt{x^2 + (y - p)^2}$$.
The line $$L$$ is $$0x + 1y = -p$$, so by :ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/distance-to-line},

```math
d(P, L) = \frac{|0x + 1y - (-p)|}{\sqrt{0^2 + 1^2}} = |y + p|
```

Both distances are at least $$0$$, so they are equal exactly when their squares are equal. Each line
below is equivalent to the one before:

```math
\begin{aligned}
x^2 + (y - p)^2 &= (y + p)^2 && \text{square both distances} \\
x^2 + y^2 - 2py + p^2 &= y^2 + 2py + p^2 && \text{multiply out the brackets} \\
x^2 - 2py &= 2py && \text{take } y^2 + p^2 \text{ from both sides} \\
x^2 &= 4py && \text{add } 2py \text{ to both sides} \\
\frac{x^2}{4p} &= y && \text{divide by } 4p \ne 0
\end{aligned}
```

The last line says that $$P$$ is on the graph of $$y = \frac{x^2}{4p}$$.

:::

With $$p = 1$$ this is the example we started with: focus $$(0, 1)$$, directrix $$y = -1$$, and the
curve $$y = \frac{x^2}{4}$$. Check one more point, $$(6, 9)$$, which is on the curve since
$$\frac{36}{4} = 9$$. It is $$\sqrt{6^2 + 8^2} = 10$$ from $$(0, 1)$$ and $$9 + 1 = 10$$ above the
line $$y = -1$$.

When $$p$$ is negative, the focus $$(0, p)$$ is below the origin and the directrix is above it, and
$$\frac{1}{4p}$$ is negative, so the parabola opens downwards. Either way the focus sits inside the
bowl of the parabola, a distance $$|p|$$ from the vertex, and the directrix runs outside it, the
same distance from the vertex on the other side.

## Every parabola has a focus

A parabola is the graph of $$y = ax^2$$ moved by an isometry (:ref{#parabola-definition}). The graph
of $$y = ax^2$$ is the graph of $$y = \frac{x^2}{4p}$$ with $$p = \frac{1}{4a}$$, so it has a focus
and a directrix. Moving the graph carries its focus and directrix along with it, because an isometry
keeps the distance between two points and also the distance from a point to a line.

:::lemma[Moves carry the focus and the directrix]{#focus-and-directrix-moves}

Let $$M$$ be an isometry, $$F$$ a point and $$L$$ a line that does not pass through $$F$$. Then:

1. $$M(L)$$ is a line, it does not pass through $$M(F)$$, and $$d(M(P), M(L)) = d(P, L)$$ for every
   point $$P$$
2. $$M$$ sends the curve with focus $$F$$ and directrix $$L$$ onto the curve with focus $$M(F)$$ and
   directrix $$M(L)$$

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isometry"}

**Part 1.** By :ref{#isometries/isometry-lines}, $$M(L)$$ is a line, and each of its points is
$$M(Y)$$ for a point $$Y$$ of $$L$$. Since $$M$$ keeps distances, $$d(M(P), M(Y)) = d(P, Y)$$. So
the distances from $$M(P)$$ to the points of $$M(L)$$ are exactly the distances from $$P$$ to the
points of $$L$$. The distance to a line is the smallest of these
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/shortest-distance}, :ref{#distance-and-angles/point-line-distance}), so
it is the same for both.

If $$M(F)$$ were on $$M(L)$$, it would be $$M(Y)$$ for some $$Y$$ on $$L$$, and then
$$d(F, Y) = d(M(F), M(Y)) = 0$$. That would put $$F = Y$$ on $$L$$, which it is not.

**Part 2.** Write $$C$$ for the curve with focus $$F$$ and directrix $$L$$, and $$C'$$ for the curve
with focus $$M(F)$$ and directrix $$M(L)$$.

- **Points of $$C$$ land on $$C'$$.** If $$P$$ is on $$C$$, then by part 1,
  $$d(M(P), M(F)) = d(P, F) = d(P, L) = d(M(P), M(L))$$, so $$M(P)$$ is on $$C'$$.
- **Every point of $$C'$$ is reached.** Let $$Q$$ be on $$C'$$. By
  :ref{#isometries/isometry-inverse}, $$M$$ can be undone, so $$Q = M(P)$$ with $$P = M^{-1}(Q)$$.
  Then $$d(P, F) = d(Q, M(F)) = d(Q, M(L)) = d(P, L)$$, using part 1 for the last step, so $$P$$ is
  on $$C$$.

:::

The other direction holds too: the curve for any point and any line not through it is a parabola. To
see this, we move the point and the line, by a slide and a turn, until the directrix is horizontal
with the focus straight above the origin. The figure shows the pieces we use.

```plane

```

:::corollary[Every parabola has a focus and a directrix]{#parabola-focus-exists}

1. For every $$a \ne 0$$, the graph of $$y = ax^2$$ is the curve with focus
   $$\left(0, \frac{1}{4a}\right)$$ and directrix $$y = -\frac{1}{4a}$$.
2. Every parabola is the curve with some focus $$F$$ and directrix $$L$$, where $$L$$ does not pass
   through $$F$$.
3. For every point $$F$$ and line $$L$$ not through $$F$$, the curve with focus $$F$$ and directrix
   $$L$$ is a parabola.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parabola#General_position"}

**Part 1.** Let $$p = \frac{1}{4a}$$, which is not $$0$$. Then $$\frac{1}{4p} = a$$, so
$$\frac{x^2}{4p} = ax^2$$, and :ref{#focus-parabola} with this $$p$$ is part 1.

**Part 2.** By :ref{#parabola-definition}, a parabola is $$M(C)$$ for an isometry $$M$$ and the
graph $$C$$ of some $$y = ax^2$$. By part 1, $$C$$ is the curve with a focus $$F_0$$ and a directrix
$$L_0$$. By :ref{#focus-and-directrix-moves}, $$M(C)$$ is the curve with focus $$M(F_0)$$ and
directrix $$M(L_0)$$, and $$M(L_0)$$ does not pass through $$M(F_0)$$.

**Part 3.** We build an isometry $$M$$ that sends $$F$$ to $$(0, p)$$ and $$L$$ to the line
$$y = -p$$, for a number $$p > 0$$.

**Step 1: the pieces.** Let $$Q$$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $$F$$ to $$L$$, so that
$$d(F, L) = d(F, Q)$$, which is positive since $$F$$ is not on $$L$$. Let
$$V = F + \frac{1}{2}(Q - F) = \frac{1}{2}(F + Q)$$, the point halfway along $$FQ$$, and let
$$p = \frac{1}{2}d(F, Q)$$. By :ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/fraction-along} with
$$t = \frac{1}{2}$$, $$d(F, V) = p$$. Also

```math
Q - V = \tfrac{1}{2}(Q - F) = -\big(F - V\big)
```

**Step 2: slide.** The slide $$T$$ by $$-V$$ sends each point $$X$$ to $$X - V$$
(:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/slide-formula}). Write $$F_1 = T(F) = F - V$$. By step 1,
$$T(Q) = Q - V = -F_1$$, and $$|F_1| = d(F, V) = p$$
(:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/distance-norm}).

**Step 3: turn.** By :ref{#trigonometry/polar-exist}, $$F_1 = (p\cos\theta, p\sin\theta)$$ for some
angle $$\theta$$. Let $$G$$ be the turn about $$O$$ by $$\frac{\pi}{2} - \theta$$. By
:ref{#trigonometry/polar-turn} and :ref{#trigonometry/quarter-values},

```math
G(F_1) = \left(p\cos\tfrac{\pi}{2},\ p\sin\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right) = (0, p)
```

In the formula for a turn (:ref{#trigonometry/rotation-formula}), changing the signs of $$x$$ and
$$y$$ changes the signs of both new coordinates, so $$G(-X) = -G(X)$$ for every point $$X$$. So
$$G(-F_1) = (0, -p)$$.

**Step 4: the moved focus and directrix.** Let $$M = G \circ T$$, which is an isometry by
:ref{#isometries/basic-isometries} and :ref{#isometries/composite-isometry}. By steps 2 and 3,
$$M(F) = (0, p)$$ and $$M(Q) = (0, -p)$$. The line $$M(L)$$ passes through $$M(Q) = (0, -p)$$. The
line $$L$$ is perpendicular to the line $$FQ$$, so $$M(L)$$ is perpendicular to the line through
$$(0, p)$$ and $$(0, -p)$$, which is the $$y$$-axis (:ref{#isometries/isometry-perpendicular}). The
line $$y = -p$$ also passes through $$(0, -p)$$ and is perpendicular to the $$y$$-axis, since its
direction $$(1, 0)$$ has dot product $$0$$ with $$(0, 1)$$
(:ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/perpendicular-formula}). There is only one such line, by rule 1 of
:ref{#distance-and-angles/perpendicular-rules}, so $$M(L)$$ is the line $$y = -p$$.

**Step 5: the curve.** Let $$C$$ be the curve with focus $$F$$ and directrix $$L$$. By
:ref{#focus-and-directrix-moves} and step 4, $$M$$ sends $$C$$ onto the curve with focus $$(0, p)$$
and directrix $$y = -p$$. By :ref{#focus-parabola}, that is the graph $$C_0$$ of
$$y = \frac{x^2}{4p}$$. Undoing $$M$$ (:ref{#isometries/isometry-inverse}) gives
$$C = M^{-1}(C_0)$$, the graph of $$y = ax^2$$ with $$a = \frac{1}{4p}$$ moved by the isometry
$$M^{-1}$$. So $$C$$ is a parabola by :ref{#parabola-definition}.

:::

For example, $$y = x^2$$ has $$a = 1$$, so its focus is $$\left(0, \frac{1}{4}\right)$$ and its
directrix is $$y = -\frac{1}{4}$$. Check the point $$(1, 1)$$: it is
$$\sqrt{1^2 + \left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^2} = \frac{5}{4}$$ from the focus and
$$1 + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{5}{4}$$ above the directrix. The bigger $$a$$ is, the narrower the bowl
and the closer the focus sits to the vertex.

:::example[The focus of a quadratic graph]

Take $$y = x^2 - 4x + 7$$. Completing the square gives $$x^2 - 4x + 7 = (x - 2)^2 + 3$$, so by
:ref{#quadratic-parabola} the graph is the graph of $$y = x^2$$ slid by $$(2, 3)$$. The slide moves
the focus $$\left(0, \frac{1}{4}\right)$$ to $$\left(2, \frac{13}{4}\right)$$, and it moves the line
$$y = -\frac{1}{4}$$ up by $$3$$ to the line $$y = \frac{11}{4}$$. By
:ref{#focus-and-directrix-moves}, these are the focus and directrix of the moved graph.

Check with the point $$(4, 7)$$, which is on the graph since $$16 - 16 + 7 = 7$$. Its distance to
the focus is

```math
\sqrt{(4 - 2)^2 + \left(7 - \tfrac{13}{4}\right)^2} = \sqrt{4 + \tfrac{225}{16}} = \sqrt{\tfrac{289}{16}} = \tfrac{17}{4}
```

and its distance to the directrix is $$7 - \frac{11}{4} = \frac{17}{4}$$.

:::

In general, $$y = ax^2 + bx + c$$ is $$y = ax^2$$ slid by $$(h, k)$$, with $$h = -\frac{b}{2a}$$ and
$$k = c - \frac{b^2}{4a}$$ (:ref{#quadratic-parabola}). So its focus is
$$\left(h, k + \frac{1}{4a}\right)$$ and its directrix is the line $$y = k - \frac{1}{4a}$$: the
vertex $$(h, k)$$ sits halfway between them.

## The reflecting property

Now back to the dish. A ray of signal comes straight down onto the bowl at a point $$P$$. Had the
bowl not been there, the ray would have carried straight on down to the directrix, meeting it at a
point we call $$N$$. The bowl bounces it instead, and the claim is that it bounces to the focus. The
proof turns on one picture: at $$P$$, the tangent to the parabola is the perpendicular bisector of
$$F$$ and $$N$$, so it acts as a mirror that swaps them.

Here is that picture with numbers first. On $$y = \frac{x^2}{4}$$ (so $$p = 1$$), take
$$P = \left(3, \frac{9}{4}\right)$$. Straight below it on the directrix $$y = -1$$ is
$$N = (3, -1)$$, and $$P$$ is $$\frac{13}{4}$$ from both $$F = (0, 1)$$ and $$N$$. The midpoint of
$$FN$$ is $$\left(\frac{3}{2}, 0\right)$$. The tangent at $$P$$ is
$$y = \frac{3}{2}x - \frac{9}{4}$$ (:ref{#parabola-tangent} with $$a = \frac{1}{4}$$ and the point
over $$x = 3$$), and putting in $$x = \frac{3}{2}$$ gives $$\frac{9}{4} - \frac{9}{4} = 0$$, so it
passes through that midpoint. Its slope is $$\frac{3}{2}$$ and the slope of $$FN$$ is
$$\frac{-1 - 1}{3 - 0} = -\frac{2}{3}$$, and $$\frac{3}{2} \cdot \left(-\frac{2}{3}\right) = -1$$,
so they are perpendicular (:ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/perpendicular-slopes}).

<ReflectionStep
  step={1}
  caption="Step 1. P is as far from F as from N, the point straight below it on the directrix. The tangent T at P passes through the midpoint M of FN and crosses FN square on: it is the perpendicular bisector of FN."
/>

:::lemma[The tangent is a perpendicular bisector]{#focus-and-directrix-tangent}

Let $$p \ne 0$$ and let $$P = \left(q, \frac{q^2}{4p}\right)$$ be a point of the graph of
$$y = \frac{x^2}{4p}$$. Let $$F = (0, p)$$ and $$N = (q, -p)$$, the point of the directrix straight
below or above $$P$$. Then $$F \ne N$$, and the tangent to the graph at $$P$$ is the perpendicular
bisector of $$FN$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parabola#Tangent_bisection_property"}

The points $$F$$ and $$N$$ are different, since their second coordinates $$p$$ and $$-p$$ differ
when $$p \ne 0$$.

**The tangent.** The graph is $$y = ax^2$$ with $$a = \frac{1}{4p}$$. By :ref{#parabola-tangent},
its tangent at the point over $$x = q$$ is $$y = 2aqx - aq^2$$, that is,

```math
y = \frac{q}{2p}\,x - \frac{q^2}{4p}
```

**The perpendicular bisector.** By :ref{#distance-and-angles/perpendicular-bisector-rule}, a point
$$X = (x, y)$$ is on the perpendicular bisector of $$FN$$ exactly when $$d(X, F) = d(X, N)$$.
Squaring both sides as in the proof of :ref{#focus-parabola}, each line below is equivalent to the
one before:

```math
\begin{aligned}
x^2 + (y - p)^2 &= (x - q)^2 + (y + p)^2 && \text{square both distances} \\
x^2 + y^2 - 2py + p^2 &= x^2 - 2qx + q^2 + y^2 + 2py + p^2 && \text{multiply out the brackets} \\
-2py &= -2qx + q^2 + 2py && \text{take } x^2 + y^2 + p^2 \text{ from both sides} \\
4py &= 2qx - q^2 && \text{add } 2qx + 2py \text{ to both sides} \\
y &= \frac{q}{2p}\,x - \frac{q^2}{4p} && \text{divide by } 4p \ne 0
\end{aligned}
```

This is the equation of the tangent, so the two lines are the same.

:::

The second ingredient is not mathematics at all. How light bounces off a mirror is a fact about the
physical world, found by experiment, and we take it as given. Picture the path the ray would have
taken with no mirror. The mirror folds that path over, as if the mirror line were a crease.

<ReflectionStep
  step={2}
  caption="Step 2, the law of reflection. A ray hits a curved mirror at P. With no mirror it would have carried on to X. The reflected ray leaves P through X′, the mirror image of X in the tangent line T at P."
/>

:::remark[Rules for reflection]{#reflection-law}

In a plane containing the ray of light:

1. **Flat mirrors**: a ray that hits a flat mirror at $$P$$ leaves along the mirror image of the
   path it would have taken with no mirror. If $$X \ne P$$ is a point of that straight-on path past
   $$P$$, the reflected ray is the ray from $$P$$ through $$R(X)$$, where $$R$$ is the reflection in
   the mirror's line (:ref{#isometries/reflection})
2. **Curved mirrors**: a ray that hits a curved mirror at $$P$$ reflects as it would off a flat
   mirror along the tangent line at $$P$$
3. **Paths run backwards**: a ray sent back along the reflected ray leaves along the path it came in
   by, the other way

These are taken as given.

::see-also{src="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specular_reflection#Law_of_reflection"}

:::

Physics books state rule 1 as "the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection". The two say
the same thing. The reflection $$R$$ keeps the point $$P$$ on the mirror and keeps every angle
(:ref{#isometries/isometry-angles}), so the incoming and outgoing rays make equal angles with the
mirror.

:::theorem[The reflecting property]{#parabola-reflection}

Let $$p > 0$$, and let $$P$$ be a point of the graph of $$y = \frac{x^2}{4p}$$, whose focus is
$$F = (0, p)$$. A ray of light coming straight down onto the inside of the curve at $$P$$ reflects
along the ray from $$P$$ through $$F$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parabola#Proof_of_the_reflective_property"}

Write $$P = \left(q, \frac{q^2}{4p}\right)$$ and $$N = (q, -p)$$. Let $$T$$ be the tangent at $$P$$
and $$R$$ the reflection in $$T$$.

**Step 1: the straight-on path.** The ray comes down the vertical line $$x = q$$. That line meets
the graph only at $$P$$, since the graph has one height for each $$x$$, so the ray reaches $$P$$
without touching the curve before. With no mirror, it would carry on down the same line to $$N$$,
which is below $$P$$ because $$\frac{q^2}{4p} \ge 0 > -p$$. So $$N$$ is a point of the straight-on
path past $$P$$.

**Step 2: the mirror.** By rule 2 of :ref{#reflection-law}, the ray reflects as it would off a flat
mirror along $$T$$. By :ref{#focus-and-directrix-tangent}, $$T$$ is the perpendicular bisector of
$$FN$$.

**Step 3: the flip.** The point $$N$$ is not on $$T$$: every point of $$T$$ is as far from $$F$$ as
from $$N$$ (:ref{#distance-and-angles/perpendicular-bisector-rule}), and $$d(N, N) = 0$$ while
$$d(N, F) > 0$$. So by :ref{#isometries/reflection}, $$R(N)$$ is the one point for which $$T$$ is
the perpendicular bisector of the segment from $$N$$ to it. Step 2 says $$F$$ is that point, so
$$R(N) = F$$.

**Step 4: the reflected ray.** By rule 1 of :ref{#reflection-law} with $$X = N$$, the reflected ray
is the ray from $$P$$ through $$R(N) = F$$.

:::

<ReflectionStep
  step={3}
  caption="Step 3. The ray comes straight down to P, and with no mirror it would carry on to N. The tangent T is the perpendicular bisector of FN, so the reflection in T sends N to F, and the reflected ray runs from P through F."
/>

When $$P$$ is the vertex, $$q = 0$$ and the ray passes down through $$F$$ before it reaches the
curve. The tangent there is the $$x$$-axis, $$N = (0, -p)$$ flips to $$F$$, and the ray bounces
straight back up through $$F$$. Slide $$P$$ along the curve below and watch the two distances stay
equal while the ray always lands on $$F$$.

## Every parabola sends rays to its focus

The theorem talks about $$y = \frac{x^2}{4p}$$ only, but a real dish can point any way. Tip the
dish, and the curve, the focus, the directrix and every tangent tip with it. The proof used one fact
about the curve, that the tangent at $$P$$ is the perpendicular bisector of $$F$$ and $$N$$. That
fact is about distances, and a move keeps distances, so it holds for every parabola.

On $$y = \frac{x^2}{4p}$$ the rays came straight down, square on to the directrix. For a tipped dish
they still travel square on to the directrix, so that is how we describe them.

:::lemma[Every tangent is a perpendicular bisector]{#focus-and-directrix-every-tangent}

Let $$C$$ be the curve with focus $$F$$ and directrix $$L$$, let $$P$$ be a point of $$C$$, and let
$$N$$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $$P$$ to $$L$$. Then $$C$$ has exactly one tangent at
$$P$$ (:ref{#parabola-tangent-def}), and it is the perpendicular bisector of $$FN$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parabola#Tangent_bisection_property"}

**The standard picture.** The proof of part 3 of :ref{#parabola-focus-exists} builds an isometry
$$M$$ and a number $$p > 0$$ with $$M(F) = (0, p)$$ and $$M(L)$$ the line $$y = -p$$. By
:ref{#focus-and-directrix-moves} and :ref{#focus-parabola}, $$M$$ sends $$C$$ onto the graph $$C_0$$
of $$y = \frac{x^2}{4p}$$. Write $$M(P) = \left(q, \frac{q^2}{4p}\right)$$.

**Where N goes.** The point $$P$$ is not on $$L$$, since otherwise $$d(P, F) = d(P, L) = 0$$ would
put $$F = P$$ on $$L$$. So the line $$PN$$ is the perpendicular from $$P$$ to $$L$$. The isometry
sends it to a line through $$M(P)$$ perpendicular to $$y = -p$$, meeting it at $$M(N)$$
(:ref{#isometries/isometry-lines}, :ref{#isometries/isometry-perpendicular}). The vertical line
$$x = q$$ is such a line (rule 3 of :ref{#coordinates/coordinate-rules}), and there is only one, by
rule 1 of :ref{#distance-and-angles/perpendicular-rules}. So $$M(N) = (q, -p)$$. Also $$N \ne F$$,
since $$N$$ is on $$L$$ and $$F$$ is not.

**The perpendicular bisector.** By :ref{#focus-and-directrix-tangent}, the tangent $$T_0$$ to
$$C_0$$ at $$M(P)$$ is the perpendicular bisector of $$(0, p)$$ and $$(q, -p)$$, which are $$M(F)$$
and $$M(N)$$. Let $$T$$ be the line that $$M^{-1}$$ sends $$T_0$$ onto
(:ref{#isometries/isometry-inverse}). A point $$X$$ is on $$T$$ exactly when $$M(X)$$ is on $$T_0$$,
that is, when $$d(M(X), M(F)) = d(M(X), M(N))$$. Since $$M$$ keeps distances, that says
$$d(X, F) = d(X, N)$$. So $$T$$ is the perpendicular bisector of $$FN$$
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/perpendicular-bisector-rule}).

**The only tangent.** Since $$M$$ is one-to-one and sends $$C$$ onto $$C_0$$ and lines onto lines, a
line $$K$$ meets $$C$$ only at $$P$$ exactly when $$M(K)$$ meets $$C_0$$ only at $$M(P)$$. The axis
of $$C$$ is the line that $$M^{-1}$$ sends the $$y$$-axis onto (:ref{#parabola-definition},
:ref{#parabolas-vertex-axis}). By :ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/line-intersection}, two lines are
not parallel exactly when they meet in exactly one point, so $$K$$ is not parallel to the axis of
$$C$$ exactly when $$M(K)$$ is not parallel to the $$y$$-axis. So $$K$$ is a tangent to $$C$$ at
$$P$$ exactly when $$M(K)$$ is a tangent to $$C_0$$ at $$M(P)$$. By :ref{#parabola-tangent} the only
one is $$T_0$$, so the only tangent to $$C$$ at $$P$$ is $$T$$.

:::

:::corollary[The reflecting property for every parabola]{#focus-and-directrix-every-reflection}

Let $$C$$ be the curve with focus $$F$$ and directrix $$L$$, and let $$P$$ be a point of $$C$$. A
ray of light travelling towards $$L$$, along the line through $$P$$ perpendicular to $$L$$, that
reaches the curve at $$P$$ reflects along the ray from $$P$$ through $$F$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parabola#Proof_of_the_reflective_property"}

Let $$N$$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $$P$$ to $$L$$. With no mirror, the ray would carry
on along the line $$PN$$ to $$N$$, so $$N$$ is a point of the straight-on path past $$P$$. Let $$T$$
be the tangent at $$P$$ and $$R$$ the reflection in $$T$$. By rule 2 of :ref{#reflection-law}, the
ray reflects as it would off a flat mirror along $$T$$.

By :ref{#focus-and-directrix-every-tangent}, $$T$$ is the perpendicular bisector of $$FN$$. Steps 3
and 4 of the proof of :ref{#parabola-reflection} now go through word for word: $$N$$ is not on
$$T$$, so $$R(N) = F$$, and by rule 1 of :ref{#reflection-law} the reflected ray is the ray from
$$P$$ through $$F$$.

:::

## Dishes, headlights and cookers

A real dish is a bowl whose every cross-section through the middle is the same parabola, so the
reflecting property works in each of those slices. To place the receiver, you need the focus. Say a
dish is $$60$$ cm across and $$10$$ cm deep. Put its lowest point at the origin, so its rim is at
$$(30, 10)$$ on a graph $$y = \frac{x^2}{4p}$$ measured in centimetres. Then

```math
10 = \frac{30^2}{4p} = \frac{900}{4p} \quad\text{so}\quad 4p = 90 \quad\text{and}\quad p = 22.5
```

So the receiver goes $$22.5$$ cm above the bottom of the bowl, well beyond the $$10$$ cm rim. That
is why it sits out in front of the dish on an arm, as in the picture at the start of this guide.

A car headlight uses the same curve the other way round. The bulb sits at the focus, and light from
it hits the mirror behind and leaves along the reverse of the path in :ref{#parabola-reflection}, by
rule 3 of :ref{#reflection-law}. So every ray leaves parallel to the axis, and the light goes out as
a beam instead of spreading in all directions.

A solar cooker is a dish that faces the sun. The sun is so far away that its rays arrive close to
parallel, so the bowl gathers them close to the focus, where the pot sits. That is the fireplace the
word "focus" is named after.

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on the focus of a parabola, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-focus-and-directrix-read-off}

Find the focus and directrix of the graph of $$y = \frac{x^2}{8}$$. Then check that the point
$$(4, 2)$$ on the graph is as far from the focus as from the directrix.

:::solution

Here $$\frac{x^2}{8} = \frac{x^2}{4p}$$ with $$4p = 8$$, so $$p = 2$$. By :ref{#focus-parabola} the
focus is $$(0, 2)$$ and the directrix is $$y = -2$$.

The point $$(4, 2)$$ is on the graph since $$\frac{16}{8} = 2$$. It is level with the focus, $$4$$
across from it, so its distance to the focus is $$4$$. It is $$2 - (-2) = 4$$ above the directrix.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-focus-and-directrix-steep}

Find the focus and directrix of the graph of $$y = 3x^2$$, and check your answer with the point
$$(1, 3)$$.

:::solution

By part 1 of :ref{#parabola-focus-exists} with $$a = 3$$, the focus is
$$\left(0, \frac{1}{12}\right)$$ and the directrix is $$y = -\frac{1}{12}$$.

The point $$(1, 3)$$ is on the graph. Its distance to the focus is

```math
\sqrt{1^2 + \left(3 - \tfrac{1}{12}\right)^2} = \sqrt{1 + \tfrac{1225}{144}} = \sqrt{\tfrac{1369}{144}} = \tfrac{37}{12}
```

and its distance to the directrix is $$3 + \frac{1}{12} = \frac{37}{12}$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-focus-and-directrix-downwards}

Find an equation for the curve with focus $$(0, -3)$$ and directrix $$y = 3$$. Which way does it
open? Check that $$(6, -3)$$ is on it.

:::solution

This is :ref{#focus-parabola} with $$p = -3$$, so the curve is the graph of

```math
y = \frac{x^2}{4 \cdot (-3)} = -\frac{x^2}{12}
```

The number in front of $$x^2$$ is negative, so it opens downwards, away from the directrix above it.
The point $$(6, -3)$$ fits, since $$-\frac{36}{12} = -3$$. It is level with the focus and $$6$$
across from it, and it is $$3 - (-3) = 6$$ below the directrix.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-focus-and-directrix-quadratic}

Find the focus and directrix of the graph of $$y = 2x^2 + 8x + 5$$. Check your answer with the point
$$(-1, -1)$$.

:::solution

Completing the square, $$2x^2 + 8x + 5 = 2(x^2 + 4x) + 5 = 2(x + 2)^2 - 8 + 5 = 2(x + 2)^2 - 3$$. So
by :ref{#quadratic-parabola} the graph is $$y = 2x^2$$ slid by $$(-2, -3)$$, with vertex
$$(-2, -3)$$. With $$a = 2$$, $$\frac{1}{4a} = \frac{1}{8}$$, so the focus is
$$\left(-2, -3 + \frac{1}{8}\right) = \left(-2, -\frac{23}{8}\right)$$ and the directrix is
$$y = -3 - \frac{1}{8} = -\frac{25}{8}$$.

The point $$(-1, -1)$$ is on the graph, since $$2 - 8 + 5 = -1$$. Its distance to the focus is

```math
\sqrt{1^2 + \left(-1 + \tfrac{23}{8}\right)^2} = \sqrt{1 + \tfrac{225}{64}} = \sqrt{\tfrac{289}{64}} = \tfrac{17}{8}
```

and its distance to the directrix is $$-1 + \frac{25}{8} = \frac{17}{8}$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-focus-and-directrix-sideways}

Find an equation for the curve with focus $$(2, 0)$$ and directrix $$x = -2$$, and check it with the
point $$(2, 4)$$. Which graph of the form $$y = \frac{x^2}{4p}$$ is it the flip of, and in which
line?

:::solution

The distance from $$(x, y)$$ to the line $$x = -2$$ is $$|x + 2|$$, by
:ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/distance-to-line}. Squaring both distances as in the proof of
:ref{#focus-parabola}:

```math
\begin{aligned}
(x - 2)^2 + y^2 &= (x + 2)^2 && \text{square both distances} \\
x^2 - 4x + 4 + y^2 &= x^2 + 4x + 4 && \text{multiply out the brackets} \\
y^2 &= 8x && \text{take } x^2 - 4x + 4 \text{ from both sides} \\
x &= \frac{y^2}{8} && \text{divide by } 8
\end{aligned}
```

The point $$(2, 4)$$ fits, since $$\frac{16}{8} = 2$$. It is $$4$$ straight above the focus, and
$$2 - (-2) = 4$$ to the right of the directrix.

Swapping the letters in $$y = \frac{x^2}{8}$$ gives $$x = \frac{y^2}{8}$$, so by :ref{#flip-graph}
the curve is the flip of $$y = \frac{x^2}{8}$$ in the line $$y = x$$. The flip swaps the coordinates
of every point, so it also sends the focus $$(0, 2)$$ of $$y = \frac{x^2}{8}$$ to $$(2, 0)$$ and the
directrix $$y = -2$$ to $$x = -2$$, as :ref{#focus-and-directrix-moves} says it must.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-focus-and-directrix-cooker}

A solar cooker is a parabolic dish $$1.2$$ m across, with its focus $$0.3$$ m above the bottom of
the bowl. How deep is the dish? Is the pot at the focus inside the bowl or above its rim?

:::solution

Put the bottom of the bowl at the origin, so its cross-section is $$y = \frac{x^2}{4p}$$ with
$$p = 0.3$$, by :ref{#focus-parabola}. The rim is $$0.6$$ m from the middle, so its height is

```math
\frac{0.6^2}{4 \times 0.3} = \frac{0.36}{1.2} = 0.3 \text{ m}
```

The dish is $$0.3$$ m deep, so the focus is level with the rim: the pot sits exactly in the opening
of the bowl.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-focus-and-directrix-chord}

Let $$p > 0$$. Show that the chord of $$y = \frac{x^2}{4p}$$ through the focus, parallel to the
directrix, has length $$4p$$. Do it in two ways: with the equation, and with the definition of
:ref{#focus-directrix} alone.

:::solution

**With the equation.** The chord lies on the line $$y = p$$, through the focus $$(0, p)$$. A point
$$(x, p)$$ is on the graph when $$\frac{x^2}{4p} = p$$, that is, when $$x^2 = 4p^2$$, so $$x = 2p$$
or $$x = -2p$$. The chord runs from $$(-2p, p)$$ to $$(2p, p)$$, which has length $$4p$$.

**With the definition.** Every point $$P$$ on the line $$y = p$$ is $$2p$$ above the directrix
$$y = -p$$. So $$P$$ is on the curve exactly when $$d(P, F) = 2p$$. The points of the line $$y = p$$
at distance $$2p$$ from $$F$$, which is on that line, are the two points $$2p$$ either side of it.
They are $$4p$$ apart.

For $$y = \frac{x^2}{4}$$ this chord runs from $$(-2, 1)$$ to $$(2, 1)$$, two of the points from
[A point and a line](#a-point-and-a-line).

:::

::::exercise{#ex-focus-and-directrix-isosceles}

Let $$p > 0$$ and $$q \ne 0$$, and let $$P = \left(q, \frac{q^2}{4p}\right)$$ on
$$y = \frac{x^2}{4p}$$, with focus $$F = (0, p)$$. The tangent at $$P$$ crosses the $$y$$-axis at a
point $$K$$. Show that $$d(F, K) = d(F, P)$$, and check it for $$p = 1$$, $$q = 3$$. Explain how
this gives another view of the reflecting property.

:::solution

By :ref{#parabola-tangent}, the tangent is $$y = \frac{q}{2p}x - \frac{q^2}{4p}$$, so putting
$$x = 0$$ gives $$K = \left(0, -\frac{q^2}{4p}\right)$$. Both $$F$$ and $$K$$ are on the $$y$$-axis,
with $$F$$ above $$K$$, so

```math
d(F, K) = p + \frac{q^2}{4p}
```

The point $$P$$ is on the curve, so $$d(F, P) = d(P, L)$$, where $$L$$ is the directrix $$y = -p$$.
That distance is $$\frac{q^2}{4p} + p$$, the same number. For $$p = 1$$ and $$q = 3$$,
$$K = \left(0, -\frac{9}{4}\right)$$ and both distances are $$1 + \frac{9}{4} = \frac{13}{4}$$.

So the triangle $$FPK$$ has two equal sides, and its angles at $$P$$ and $$K$$ are equal
(:ref{#isometries/isosceles}). The angle at $$K$$ is the angle between the tangent and the axis. The
incoming ray is parallel to the axis, so it meets the tangent at that same angle, as the picture in
step 3 shows. The equal angles at $$P$$ then say that the ray to $$F$$ leaves the tangent at the
angle the incoming ray arrived at, which is the "angle of incidence equals angle of reflection" form
of the law.

:::
