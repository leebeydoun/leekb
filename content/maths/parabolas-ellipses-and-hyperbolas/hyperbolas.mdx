Imagine ordering a £12 pizza and splitting the bill equally. Eat alone and you pay £12. Two of you
pay £6 each, three pay £4, and twelve pay £1. Twice the people means half each, so the number of
people times the share is always 12.

Now mark each pair as a dot, with the number of people across and the share up. You'd notice the
dots don't lie on a line. They fall steeply at first, then level out, creeping towards the bottom
axis without ever reaching it, since nobody's share is ever £0.

<PizzaShares caption="Each dot is (people, pounds each) for a £12 bill. Every dot has people × share = 12, so all of them lie on the graph of xy = 12." />

The curve through the dots is the graph of $$xy = 12$$, and it is a **hyperbola** seen from an
unusual angle: turned by an eighth of a turn, its equation becomes one of the form
$$\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$$. In this guide we'll get to know the graph of $$xy = c$$,
turn it to find the standard hyperbola, and then find the lines its branches approach and its two
foci.

## The graph of xy = c

The graph of $$xy = 12$$ is the set of points whose two coordinates multiply to $$12$$
(:ref{#linear-equations/graph}). Whole-number points are the place to start, since you can check
each one by multiplying:

| $$x$$ | $$1$$  | $$2$$ | $$3$$ | $$4$$ | $$6$$ | $$12$$ |
| ----- | ------ | ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- | ------ |
| $$y$$ | $$12$$ | $$6$$ | $$4$$ | $$3$$ | $$2$$ | $$1$$  |

The pizza only allowed a positive number of people, but the equation allows any numbers. A negative
times a negative is positive, so $$(-3, -4)$$ is on the graph as well, since $$(-3)(-4) = 12$$.
Putting a minus sign in front of every entry of the table gives six more points, in the bottom left
quarter of the plane. So the graph comes in two pieces, called **branches**, one in the top right
quarter and one in the bottom left.

<ProductBranches caption="The graph of xy = 12 has two branches. The half turn about O sends (3, 4) to (−3, −4), and the flip in y = x sends it to (4, 3): both images are on the graph too." />

It's tempting to draw only the top right branch, because that is the one the pizza drew. But the
bottom left branch belongs to the graph as much, and leaving it out is a slip worth watching for:
every hyperbola in this guide has two branches.

The two branches are mirror images of each other in two ways. The half turn about $$O$$ sends
$$(x, y)$$ to $$(-x, -y)$$, and $$(-x)(-y) = xy$$, so it sends every point of the graph to another
point of the graph. Swapping the coordinates, the flip $$S$$ in the line $$y = x$$
(:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/diagonal-reflection}), does the same, since $$yx = xy$$. By
:ref{#functions/flip-graph}, the flip $$S$$ sends the graph of $$xy = 12$$ onto the graph of
$$yx = 12$$, which is the same equation, so $$S$$ is a symmetry of the graph
(:ref{#isometries/symmetry}).

### Close to the axes, never on them

Go far to the right and the share gets tiny. At $$x = 1000$$ the point on the graph has
$$y = \frac{12}{1000} = 0.012$$, and at $$x = 1\,000\,000$$ it has $$y = 0.000012$$. To get within
any distance you name of the $$x$$-axis, go far enough right: to be within $$0.001$$, any $$x$$
bigger than $$12\,000$$ will do, since then $$y = \frac{12}{x}$$ is less than
$$\frac{12}{12\,000} = 0.001$$.

The curve never reaches the axis, though. A point of the $$x$$-axis has $$y = 0$$, so $$xy = 0$$,
which is not $$12$$. The same goes for the $$y$$-axis, which the branch approaches as $$x$$ gets
close to $$0$$. A line that a curve gets as close as you like to, far out, without meeting it, is
called an **asymptote** of the curve. So the two axes are the asymptotes of $$xy = 12$$. The graph
of the function $$\frac{1}{x}$$ in
[A gallery of graphs](../functions/graphs-of-functions.mdx#a-gallery-of-graphs) hugs the axes in the
same way, because it is the graph of $$xy = 1$$.

Everything here works for any number $$c \ne 0$$ in place of $$12$$. For $$c > 0$$ the branches sit
in the top right and bottom left quarters, as for $$xy = 12$$. For $$c < 0$$ one coordinate must be
negative and the other positive, so the branches sit in the top left and bottom right quarters.

## Sliding the graph of xy = c

Slide the graph of $$xy = 6$$ by $$(-2, 3)$$, two to the left and three up. By
:ref{#functions/slide-graph}, the new graph has the equation we get by putting $$x - (-2) = x + 2$$
in place of $$x$$ and $$y - 3$$ in place of $$y$$:

```math
(x + 2)(y - 3) = 6
```

The asymptotes slide with it, onto the lines $$x = -2$$ and $$y = 3$$, which cross at the **centre**
$$(-2, 3)$$. Notice the sign: $$x + 2$$ means a slide 2 to the **left**. The new curve at $$x$$ does
what the old one did at $$x + 2$$, two steps further right, so everything has moved left.

<MovedProduct caption="The graph of (x + 2)(y − 3) = 6, the graph of xy = 6 (faint) slid 2 to the left and 3 up. Its asymptotes are the dashed lines x = −2 and y = 3, and its centre is (−2, 3)." />

As a check, put $$x = 1$$ into $$(x + 2)(y - 3) = 6$$: then $$3(y - 3) = 6$$, so $$y - 3 = 2$$ and
$$y = 5$$. The point $$(1, 5)$$ is the point $$(3, 2)$$ of $$xy = 6$$ moved two left and three up,
and $$3 \cdot 2 = 6$$. Dividing by $$x + 2$$ gives the same curve as a formula for $$y$$:

```math
y = 3 + \frac{6}{x + 2} \qquad (x \ne -2)
```

### Recognising one from its equation

Multiply out $$(x + 2)(y - 3) = 6$$ and you get $$xy - 3x + 2y - 6 = 6$$, which is
$$xy - 3x + 2y - 12 = 0$$. Someone handing you that last equation has hidden the slide. To find it
again, factor the first three terms back into a product of two brackets.

:::proposition[Spotting a slid copy of xy = c]{#hyperbolas-moved-product}

For any numbers $$p$$, $$q$$ and $$r$$, the equation $$xy + px + qy + r = 0$$ has the same graph as

```math
(x + q)(y + p) = pq - r
```

When $$pq - r \ne 0$$, this is the graph of $$xy = pq - r$$ slid by $$(-q, -p)$$, with centre
$$(-q, -p)$$ and asymptotes $$x = -q$$ and $$y = -p$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbola#Hyperbola_with_equation_y_.3D_A.2Fx"}

Multiply out the brackets: $$(x + q)(y + p) = xy + px + qy + pq$$. So

```math
\begin{aligned}
xy + px + qy + r = 0 &\iff xy + px + qy + pq = pq - r && \text{add } pq - r \text{ to both sides} \\
&\iff (x + q)(y + p) = pq - r && \text{the brackets multiply out to the left side}
\end{aligned}
```

The two equations have the same solutions, so the same graph. Writing $$x + q$$ as $$x - (-q)$$ and
$$y + p$$ as $$y - (-p)$$, :ref{#functions/slide-graph} says this is the graph of $$xy = pq - r$$
slid by $$(-q, -p)$$. The slide carries the axes, the asymptotes of $$xy = pq - r$$, onto the lines
$$x = -q$$ and $$y = -p$$.

:::

For $$xy - 3x + 2y - 12 = 0$$ we have $$p = -3$$, $$q = 2$$ and $$r = -12$$, so
$$pq - r = -6 + 12 = 6$$, and the equation is $$(x + 2)(y - 3) = 6$$, as it should be. When
$$pq - r = 0$$ the equation says a product is $$0$$, so one bracket is $$0$$: the graph is the two
lines $$x = -q$$ and $$y = -p$$, and there is no curve at all.

## Turning xy = c by an eighth of a turn

The graph of $$xy = 2$$ is symmetric in the line $$y = x$$, and each branch comes nearest the origin
where it crosses that line, at $$(\sqrt2, \sqrt2)$$ and $$(-\sqrt2, -\sqrt2)$$. Picture turning the
whole graph anticlockwise by $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$ ($$45^\circ$$). The line $$y = x$$ swings up onto the
$$y$$-axis, so the branches end up opening upwards and downwards, symmetric in the $$y$$-axis. The
axes, the asymptotes, swing onto the diagonals $$y = x$$ and $$y = -x$$.

Try it with one point first. The point $$(1, 2)$$ is on $$xy = 2$$. The turn by $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$
has $$\cos\frac{\pi}{4} = \sin\frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}$$ (:ref{#trigonometry/sin-cos-45}),
so by :ref{#trigonometry/rotation-formula} it sends $$(1, 2)$$ to

```math
\left(\frac{1 - 2}{\sqrt2},\ \frac{1 + 2}{\sqrt2}\right) = \left(-\frac{1}{\sqrt2},\ \frac{3}{\sqrt2}\right)
```

The squares of those coordinates are $$\frac12$$ and $$\frac92$$, and $$\frac92 - \frac12 = 4$$. So
the turned point satisfies $$y^2 - x^2 = 4$$, and $$4$$ is twice the $$2$$ in $$xy = 2$$. The
theorem says this happens for every point.

:::theorem[Turning xy = c]{#hyperbola-turn}

Let $$c \ne 0$$, and let $$G_{\pi/4}$$ be the turn about $$O$$ by $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$.

1. $$G_{\pi/4}$$ sends the graph of $$xy = c$$ onto the graph of $$y^2 - x^2 = 2c$$
2. $$G_{\pi/4}$$ sends the graph of $$x^2 - y^2 = 2c$$ onto the graph of $$xy = c$$

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbola#Hyperbola_with_equation_y_.3D_A.2Fx"}

By :ref{#turn-graph}, the turn by $$\varphi$$ sends the graph of an equation in $$x$$ and $$y$$ onto
the graph of the equation we get by putting $$x\cos\varphi + y\sin\varphi$$ in place of $$x$$ and
$$-x\sin\varphi + y\cos\varphi$$ in place of $$y$$. That is the point turned back by $$\varphi$$.
For $$\varphi = \frac{\pi}{4}$$, both $$\cos\varphi$$ and $$\sin\varphi$$ are $$\frac{1}{\sqrt2}$$,
so we put

```math
\frac{x + y}{\sqrt2} \text{ in place of } x \qquad \frac{-x + y}{\sqrt2} \text{ in place of } y
```

**Part 1.** Making those replacements in $$xy = c$$ gives

```math
\begin{aligned}
\frac{x + y}{\sqrt2} \cdot \frac{y - x}{\sqrt2} &= c && \text{the replacements, with } -x + y \text{ written } y - x \\
\frac{(y + x)(y - x)}{2} &= c && \text{since } \sqrt2 \cdot \sqrt2 = 2 \\
\frac{y^2 - x^2}{2} &= c && \text{multiplying out the brackets} \\
y^2 - x^2 &= 2c && \text{doubling both sides}
\end{aligned}
```

**Part 2.** Making the same replacements in $$x^2 - y^2 = 2c$$ gives

```math
\begin{aligned}
\frac{(x + y)^2}{2} - \frac{(y - x)^2}{2} &= 2c && \text{the replacements, squared} \\
\frac{(x^2 + 2xy + y^2) - (y^2 - 2xy + x^2)}{2} &= 2c && \text{multiplying out both squares} \\
\frac{4xy}{2} &= 2c && \text{the } x^2 \text{ and } y^2 \text{ terms cancel} \\
xy &= c && \text{dividing both sides by } 2
\end{aligned}
```

:::

The signs are where things go wrong, so it's worth checking part 1 against the point from before.
The turned point $$\left(-\frac{1}{\sqrt2}, \frac{3}{\sqrt2}\right)$$ satisfies $$y^2 - x^2 = 4$$,
as part 1 says for $$c = 2$$. Notice that :ref{#turn-graph} puts in the point turned **back**, which
is why its signs are the other way round from the formula for the turn itself.

Drag the slider to turn the graph of $$xy = 2$$ from $$0$$ to $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$. The blue curve is
the turned graph and the faint dashed curve is where it started. The orange dashed lines are the
turned axes, and the purple dot is the turned point $$(\sqrt2, \sqrt2)$$. At $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$ the
$$xy$$ term vanishes from the equation, and at $$\frac{\pi}{2}$$ the graph has become $$xy = -2$$.

## The hyperbola

Part 2 of :ref{#hyperbola-turn} says that $$xy = c$$ is the turned copy of a curve whose equation
has a square of $$x$$ minus a square of $$y$$. Curves of that kind, moved by any isometry, are the
hyperbolas.

:::definition[Hyperbola]{#hyperbola-definition}

A **hyperbola** is the image, under an isometry, of the graph of

```math
\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1
```

for some numbers $$a > 0$$ and $$b > 0$$. This graph itself is the **standard hyperbola** with those
numbers. Its **centre** is $$O$$ and its **vertices** are $$(a, 0)$$ and $$(-a, 0)$$, and for a
moved hyperbola they are the images of these points.

:::

Here is the standard hyperbola with $$a = 3$$ and $$b = 4$$, the graph of
$$\frac{x^2}{9} - \frac{y^2}{16} = 1$$. As a check, put in the point $$(3, 0)$$:
$$\frac{9}{9} - 0 = 1$$. For a point off the axis, put in $$\left(5, \frac{16}{3}\right)$$:
$$\frac{25}{9} - \frac{256}{9 \cdot 16} = \frac{25}{9} - \frac{16}{9} = 1$$.

<StandardHyperbola caption="The standard hyperbola x²/9 − y²/16 = 1, with vertices (±3, 0) and foci F₁ and F₂ at (±5, 0). The dashed lines through the corners of the box from (−3, −4) to (3, 4) are y = ±4x/3, the lines the branches approach." />

The standard hyperbola has two branches for the same reason as $$xy = 12$$. Rearranged, its equation
says $$\frac{x^2}{a^2} = 1 + \frac{y^2}{b^2}$$, which is at least $$1$$. So $$x^2 \ge a^2$$, and
every point has $$x \ge a$$ or $$x \le -a$$. No point of the graph lies in the strip between
$$x = -a$$ and $$x = a$$, so the graph comes in two pieces: the **right branch**, with $$x \ge a$$,
and the **left branch**, with $$x \le -a$$.

### The graph of xy = c is a hyperbola

For $$c > 0$$, divide both sides of $$x^2 - y^2 = 2c$$ by $$2c$$:

```math
\frac{x^2}{2c} - \frac{y^2}{2c} = 1
```

This is the standard hyperbola with $$a^2 = b^2 = 2c$$, so $$a = b = \sqrt{2c}$$. By part 2 of
:ref{#hyperbola-turn}, the turn $$G_{\pi/4}$$ sends it onto the graph of $$xy = c$$, and turns keep
distances (:ref{#isometries/basic-isometries}). So the graph of $$xy = c$$ is a hyperbola. Its
vertices are where the turn sends $$(\pm\sqrt{2c}, 0)$$, and by :ref{#trigonometry/rotation-formula}
the turn sends $$(\sqrt{2c}, 0)$$ to

```math
\left(\frac{\sqrt{2c}}{\sqrt2},\ \frac{\sqrt{2c}}{\sqrt2}\right) = (\sqrt{c}, \sqrt{c})
```

That fits: $$\sqrt c \cdot \sqrt c = c$$. For $$xy = 12$$, $$a = b = \sqrt{24} = 2\sqrt6$$, and the
vertices are $$(2\sqrt3, 2\sqrt3) \approx (3.46, 3.46)$$ and $$(-2\sqrt3, -2\sqrt3)$$. For
$$c < 0$$, the number $$-c$$ is positive, so the graph of $$xy = -c$$ is a hyperbola. The flip in
the $$y$$-axis sends it onto the graph of $$(-x)y = -c$$ by :ref{#functions/flip-graph}, which is
$$xy = c$$. A turn followed by a flip is an isometry (:ref{#isometries/composite-isometry}), so
$$xy = c$$ is a hyperbola for every $$c \ne 0$$.

One slip is worth naming here. The equation $$x^2 - y^2 = 4$$ is a hyperbola, but to read off $$a$$
and $$b$$ you first divide by $$4$$ to get $$1$$ on the right:
$$\frac{x^2}{4} - \frac{y^2}{4} = 1$$, so $$a = b = 2$$, not $$1$$.

### Hyperbolas that open up and down

The graph of $$y^2 - x^2 = 4$$ from part 1 of :ref{#hyperbola-turn} opens up and down, not left and
right. It is a hyperbola too. By :ref{#functions/flip-graph}, swapping the letters $$x$$ and $$y$$
is the flip in $$y = x$$, and swapping them in $$\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$$ gives
$$\frac{y^2}{a^2} - \frac{x^2}{b^2} = 1$$. So every equation of that second shape is a flipped
standard hyperbola, with vertices $$(0, \pm a)$$ on the $$y$$-axis.

Dividing $$y^2 - x^2 = 4$$ by $$4$$ gives $$\frac{y^2}{4} - \frac{x^2}{4} = 1$$, so $$a = b = 2$$
and the vertices are $$(0, 2)$$ and $$(0, -2)$$. As a check, $$(0, 2)$$ gives $$2^2 - 0^2 = 4$$.

## The lines the branches approach

Far out along the standard hyperbola, $$x$$ and $$y$$ are large, and the $$1$$ on the right of
$$\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$$ is tiny next to them. Without it, the equation would say
$$\frac{y^2}{b^2} = \frac{x^2}{a^2}$$, which is $$y = \pm\frac{b}{a}x$$. So far out, the curve
should look almost like those two lines. They are the dashed lines through the corners of the box in
the picture under [The hyperbola](#the-hyperbola).

With numbers, take $$a = 3$$ and $$b = 4$$, and go out to $$x = 30$$. The right branch has
$$y = \frac43\sqrt{30^2 - 9} = \frac43\sqrt{891} \approx 39.80$$ there, and the line
$$y = \frac43 x$$ has $$y = 40$$. The gap between them is about $$0.20$$, small next to $$40$$.

:::proposition[The asymptotes]{#hyperbola-asymptotes}

Let $$a, b > 0$$, and write $$C$$ for the graph of $$\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$$.

1. $$C$$ never meets either of the lines $$y = \frac{b}{a}x$$ and $$y = -\frac{b}{a}x$$
2. For $$x \ge a$$, the point of $$C$$ above $$x$$ with $$y \ge 0$$ lies below the line
   $$y = \frac{b}{a}x$$, by a vertical gap of

   ```math
   \frac{b}{a}\left(x - \sqrt{x^2 - a^2}\right) = \frac{ab}{x + \sqrt{x^2 - a^2}}
   ```

3. That gap is at most $$\frac{ab}{x}$$. So for any positive number $$g$$, at every $$x$$ bigger
   than both $$a$$ and $$\frac{ab}{g}$$ the gap is less than $$g$$

These two lines are the asymptotes of $$C$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbola#Asymptotes"}

**Part 1.** Put a point $$\left(x, \pm\frac{b}{a}x\right)$$ of either line into the left side of the
equation:

```math
\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{1}{b^2} \cdot \frac{b^2x^2}{a^2} = \frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{x^2}{a^2} = 0
```

That is $$0$$, not $$1$$, so no point of either line is on $$C$$.

**Part 2.** For a point $$(x, y)$$ of $$C$$ with $$y \ge 0$$, solve the equation for $$y$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
\frac{y^2}{b^2} &= \frac{x^2}{a^2} - 1 && \text{moving the terms across} \\
y^2 &= \frac{b^2}{a^2}\left(x^2 - a^2\right) && \text{multiplying by } b^2 \text{ and taking out } \tfrac{1}{a^2} \\
y &= \frac{b}{a}\sqrt{x^2 - a^2} && \text{the square root, since } y \ge 0
\end{aligned}
```

The line is at height $$\frac{b}{a}x$$, so the gap is $$\frac{b}{a}x - y$$. Multiply and divide by
$$x + \sqrt{x^2 - a^2}$$, which is positive since $$x \ge a > 0$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
\frac{b}{a}x - y &= \frac{b}{a}\left(x - \sqrt{x^2 - a^2}\right) && \text{taking out } \tfrac{b}{a} \\
&= \frac{b}{a} \cdot \frac{\left(x - \sqrt{x^2 - a^2}\right)\left(x + \sqrt{x^2 - a^2}\right)}{x + \sqrt{x^2 - a^2}} && \text{multiplying and dividing by the same number} \\
&= \frac{b}{a} \cdot \frac{x^2 - (x^2 - a^2)}{x + \sqrt{x^2 - a^2}} && (u - v)(u + v) = u^2 - v^2 \\
&= \frac{b}{a} \cdot \frac{a^2}{x + \sqrt{x^2 - a^2}} = \frac{ab}{x + \sqrt{x^2 - a^2}} && \text{cancelling one } a
\end{aligned}
```

This gap is positive, so the point of $$C$$ lies below the line.

**Part 3.** The square root is at least $$0$$, so the bottom of the fraction is at least $$x$$, and
dividing by a bigger number gives a smaller answer. So the gap is at most $$\frac{ab}{x}$$. If also
$$x > \frac{ab}{g}$$, then $$\frac{ab}{x} < g$$, and the gap is less than $$g$$.

:::

Here are the gaps for $$a = 3$$ and $$b = 4$$, where $$ab = 12$$:

| $$x$$                                 | $$5$$    | $$30$$   | $$300$$   | $$3000$$   |
| ------------------------------------- | -------- | -------- | --------- | ---------- |
| gap $$\frac{12}{x + \sqrt{x^2 - 9}}$$ | $$1.33$$ | $$0.20$$ | $$0.020$$ | $$0.0020$$ |
| bound $$\frac{12}{x}$$                | $$2.4$$  | $$0.4$$  | $$0.04$$  | $$0.004$$  |

Each time $$x$$ is ten times bigger, the gap is about ten times smaller. To be within $$0.001$$ of
the line, part 3 says any $$x$$ bigger than $$\frac{12}{0.001} = 12\,000$$ will do. That is what "as
close as you like" means: name any gap, however small, and far enough out the curve is closer than
that.

<AsymptoteGap caption="The upper part of the right branch of x²/9 − y²/16 = 1 and the asymptote y = 4x/3. The gap between them shrinks from 1.81 at x = 4 to 0.78 at x = 8 and 0.51 at x = 12, and it is never 0." />

The other three quarters behave the same way. Swapping $$x$$ for $$-x$$, or $$y$$ for $$-y$$, leaves
the equation unchanged, so by :ref{#functions/flip-graph} the flips in the two axes are symmetries
of $$C$$. Each flip swaps the two asymptotes, so the same gaps appear in every quarter. For
$$xy = c$$ the asymptotes are the axes: the turn by $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$ carries the lines
$$y = \pm x$$, the asymptotes of $$x^2 - y^2 = 2c$$ (where $$\frac{b}{a} = 1$$), onto the two axes,
as the orange lines in the turning diagram show.

## The two foci

An ellipse has two foci, and the distances from them to a point of the ellipse add up to the same
number everywhere (:ref{#ellipse-foci}). A hyperbola has two foci as well, but now the
**difference** of the two distances stays the same. Points near one focus are far from the other,
and moving out along a branch makes both distances grow at almost the same rate.

Take $$a = 3$$ and $$b = 4$$ again. The foci will be $$F_1 = (-5, 0)$$ and $$F_2 = (5, 0)$$, where
$$5 = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2}$$. The vertex $$(3, 0)$$ is $$8$$ from $$F_1$$ and $$2$$ from $$F_2$$, a
difference of $$6$$. The point $$P = \left(5, \frac{16}{3}\right)$$ sits straight above $$F_2$$, so
it is $$\frac{16}{3}$$ from $$F_2$$, and by :ref{#coordinates/distance-formula} it is
$$\sqrt{10^2 + \left(\frac{16}{3}\right)^2} = \sqrt{\frac{1156}{9}} = \frac{34}{3}$$ from $$F_1$$.
The difference is $$\frac{34}{3} - \frac{16}{3} = 6$$ again, and $$6 = 2a$$.

<FocalDistances caption="The point P = (5, 16/3) on x²/9 − y²/16 = 1 is 34/3 from F₁ = (−5, 0) and 16/3 from F₂ = (5, 0). The difference is 6, twice the distance from the centre to a vertex." />

:::theorem[The two foci of a hyperbola]{#hyperbola-foci}

Let $$a, b > 0$$ and $$c = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$$, and let $$F_1 = (-c, 0)$$ and $$F_2 = (c, 0)$$. A
point $$P$$ lies on the graph of $$\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$$ exactly when

```math
\big|\,d(P, F_1) - d(P, F_2)\,\big| = 2a
```

The points $$F_1$$ and $$F_2$$ are the **foci** of the standard hyperbola. On the right branch
$$d(P, F_1) - d(P, F_2) = 2a$$, and on the left branch $$d(P, F_2) - d(P, F_1) = 2a$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbola#Equation"}

Write $$P = (x, y)$$, $$d_1 = d(P, F_1)$$ and $$d_2 = d(P, F_2)$$. By
:ref{#coordinates/distance-formula},

```math
d_1^2 = (x + c)^2 + y^2 \qquad d_2^2 = (x - c)^2 + y^2
```

We use one fact about $$c$$ throughout: $$c^2 = a^2 + b^2$$, so $$c^2 - b^2 = a^2$$ and
$$c^2 - a^2 = b^2$$.

**On the curve, the distances.** Suppose $$P$$ is on the graph. Then
$$y^2 = \frac{b^2x^2}{a^2} - b^2$$, from the equation multiplied by $$b^2$$. Put that into
$$d_2^2$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
d_2^2 &= x^2 - 2cx + c^2 + \frac{b^2x^2}{a^2} - b^2 && \text{multiplying out, and the value of } y^2 \\
&= x^2\left(1 + \frac{b^2}{a^2}\right) - 2cx + a^2 && \text{since } c^2 - b^2 = a^2 \\
&= \frac{c^2x^2}{a^2} - 2cx + a^2 && \text{since } 1 + \frac{b^2}{a^2} = \frac{a^2 + b^2}{a^2} = \frac{c^2}{a^2} \\
&= \left(\frac{cx}{a} - a\right)^2 && \text{a perfect square: } (u - v)^2 = u^2 - 2uv + v^2
\end{aligned}
```

So $$d_2 = \left|\frac{cx}{a} - a\right|$$, and the same steps with $$+c$$ in place of $$-c$$ give
$$d_1 = \left|\frac{cx}{a} + a\right|$$.

**On the curve, the difference.** Every point of the graph has $$x \ge a$$ or $$x \le -a$$, as we
saw under [The hyperbola](#the-hyperbola). Also $$c > a$$, since $$c^2 = a^2 + b^2 > a^2$$.

- **Right branch**: $$x \ge a$$ gives $$\frac{cx}{a} \ge c > a$$, so both $$\frac{cx}{a} + a$$ and
  $$\frac{cx}{a} - a$$ are positive. Then
  $$d_1 - d_2 = \left(\frac{cx}{a} + a\right) - \left(\frac{cx}{a} - a\right) = 2a$$
- **Left branch**: $$x \le -a$$ gives $$\frac{cx}{a} \le -c < -a$$, so both are negative. Then
  $$d_1 = -\frac{cx}{a} - a$$ and $$d_2 = -\frac{cx}{a} + a$$, so $$d_2 - d_1 = 2a$$

Either way $$|d_1 - d_2| = 2a$$.

**The other way round.** Now suppose $$|d_1 - d_2| = 2a$$. Then $$d_1 - d_2 = 2a\varepsilon$$, where
$$\varepsilon$$ (read "epsilon") is $$1$$ or $$-1$$. Subtracting the two squares above, the $$x^2$$
and $$y^2$$ and $$c^2$$ cancel:

```math
d_1^2 - d_2^2 = (x + c)^2 - (x - c)^2 = 4cx
```

Since $$d_1^2 - d_2^2 = (d_1 - d_2)(d_1 + d_2)$$, and $$d_1 - d_2 = 2a\varepsilon$$ is not $$0$$, we
can divide. Using $$\frac{1}{\varepsilon} = \varepsilon$$:

```math
\begin{aligned}
d_1 + d_2 &= \frac{4cx}{2a\varepsilon} = \frac{2cx\varepsilon}{a} && \text{dividing } d_1^2 - d_2^2 \text{ by } d_1 - d_2 \\
2d_1 &= 2a\varepsilon + \frac{2cx\varepsilon}{a} && \text{adding } d_1 - d_2 = 2a\varepsilon \\
d_1 &= \varepsilon\left(a + \frac{cx}{a}\right) && \text{halving, and taking out } \varepsilon
\end{aligned}
```

Squaring both sides, with $$\varepsilon^2 = 1$$, gives $$d_1^2 = \left(a + \frac{cx}{a}\right)^2$$.
Now write $$d_1^2$$ out and rearrange:

```math
\begin{aligned}
x^2 + 2cx + c^2 + y^2 &= a^2 + 2cx + \frac{c^2x^2}{a^2} && \text{both sides multiplied out} \\
x^2 - \frac{c^2x^2}{a^2} + y^2 &= a^2 - c^2 && \text{taking } 2cx + c^2 + \tfrac{c^2x^2}{a^2} \text{ from both sides} \\
-\frac{b^2x^2}{a^2} + y^2 &= -b^2 && \text{since } 1 - \frac{c^2}{a^2} = -\frac{b^2}{a^2} \text{ and } a^2 - c^2 = -b^2 \\
\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} &= 1 && \text{dividing both sides by } -b^2
\end{aligned}
```

So $$P$$ is on the graph.

:::

For the point $$P = \left(5, \frac{16}{3}\right)$$, the formula from the proof gives
$$d_2 = \frac{5 \cdot 5}{3} - 3 = \frac{16}{3}$$ and $$d_1 = \frac{25}{3} + 3 = \frac{34}{3}$$, the
two distances we measured. An isometry keeps distances, so a moved hyperbola has foci too: the
images of $$F_1$$ and $$F_2$$, with the same difference $$2a$$.

### How wide the branches open

For an ellipse the foci are inside, at distance $$c < a$$ from the centre. For a hyperbola they sit
beyond the vertices, at $$c > a$$. The ratio of the two says how widely the branches open.

:::definition[Eccentricity of a hyperbola]{#hyperbola-eccentricity}

The **eccentricity** of the standard hyperbola $$\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$$, and of
every hyperbola moved from it, is

```math
e = \frac{c}{a} = \frac{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}}{a}
```

Since $$c > a$$, every hyperbola has $$e > 1$$.

:::

For $$a = 3$$ and $$b = 4$$ the eccentricity is $$\frac{5}{3} \approx 1.67$$. For $$xy = c$$, with
$$a = b$$, it is $$\frac{\sqrt{2a^2}}{a} = \sqrt2 \approx 1.41$$, whatever $$c$$ is. Squaring
$$e = \frac{c}{a}$$ gives $$e^2 = 1 + \frac{b^2}{a^2}$$, so the asymptotes have slopes
$$\pm\frac{b}{a} = \pm\sqrt{e^2 - 1}$$. An eccentricity close to $$1$$ means flat asymptotes and
narrow branches, and a large eccentricity means steep asymptotes and branches opened wide. The guide
[Slicing a cone](./slicing-a-cone.mdx) shows how eccentricity sorts ellipses, parabolas and
hyperbolas into one family (:ref{#focus-directrix-e}).

## Finding a ship from two radio signals

Before satellite positioning, ships found their place at sea with radio stations on the coast, in a
system called LORAN. In its plainest form, two stations $$A$$ and $$B$$, $$100$$ km apart, send out
a pulse at the same instant. A radio signal covers about $$300\,000$$ km a second, which is $$0.3$$
km each millionth of a second. A ship hears $$B$$'s pulse $$200$$ millionths of a second before
$$A$$'s, so it is $$200 \times 0.3 = 60$$ km nearer to $$B$$ than to $$A$$.

Put the coastline on the $$x$$-axis with $$A = (-50, 0)$$ and $$B = (50, 0)$$, measuring in km. The
ship's distances to $$A$$ and $$B$$ differ by $$60$$, so by :ref{#hyperbola-foci} it lies on the
hyperbola with foci $$A$$ and $$B$$ and $$2a = 60$$. That gives $$a = 30$$ and $$c = 50$$, so
$$b = \sqrt{50^2 - 30^2} = 40$$, and the ship is on the right branch, the one nearer $$B$$, of

```math
\frac{x^2}{900} - \frac{y^2}{1600} = 1
```

<RadioStations caption="Stations A and B are 100 km apart. Every point of the blue branch is 60 km nearer B than A; the ship at (34, 21.3) is 86.7 km from A and 26.7 km from B." />

One such point is $$\left(34, \frac{64}{3}\right) \approx (34, 21.3)$$, which is
$$\sqrt{84^2 + \left(\frac{64}{3}\right)^2} = \frac{260}{3} \approx 86.7$$ km from $$A$$ and
$$\sqrt{16^2 + \left(\frac{64}{3}\right)^2} = \frac{80}{3} \approx 26.7$$ km from $$B$$, a
difference of $$\frac{180}{3} = 60$$. One pair of stations puts the ship somewhere on this branch,
but not where on it. A second pair gives a second hyperbola, and the ship is where the two cross.

::::exercise{#ex-hyperbolas-product-table}

1. Find all the points with whole-number coordinates on the top right branch of $$xy = 20$$.
2. Is $$(-4, -5)$$ on the graph? Is $$(-4, 5)$$?
3. What are the asymptotes of $$xy = 20$$, and how far right must you go before the curve is within
   $$0.01$$ of the $$x$$-axis?

:::solution

1. The whole numbers that multiply to $$20$$ in pairs are $$1 \cdot 20$$, $$2 \cdot 10$$ and
   $$4 \cdot 5$$, in either order. So the points are $$(1, 20)$$, $$(2, 10)$$, $$(4, 5)$$,
   $$(5, 4)$$, $$(10, 2)$$ and $$(20, 1)$$.
2. $$(-4)(-5) = 20$$, so $$(-4, -5)$$ is on the graph, on the bottom left branch. But
   $$(-4) \cdot 5 = -20$$, so $$(-4, 5)$$ is not on it. It is on the graph of $$xy = -20$$.
3. The asymptotes are the two axes. The height of the curve at $$x > 0$$ is $$y = \frac{20}{x}$$,
   which is less than $$0.01$$ exactly when $$x > \frac{20}{0.01} = 2000$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-hyperbolas-moved}

Show that the graph of $$xy + 4x - y - 10 = 0$$ is a slid copy of the graph of $$xy = c$$ for some
$$c$$. Find $$c$$, the centre and the asymptotes, write $$y$$ as a formula in $$x$$, and check your
answer at $$x = 4$$.

:::solution

Here $$p = 4$$, $$q = -1$$ and $$r = -10$$, so by :ref{#hyperbolas-moved-product} the equation is

```math
(x - 1)(y + 4) = pq - r = -4 + 10 = 6
```

Multiplying out checks it: $$(x - 1)(y + 4) = xy + 4x - y - 4$$, and $$xy + 4x - y - 4 = 6$$ is the
original equation. So $$c = 6$$, and the graph is $$xy = 6$$ slid by $$(1, -4)$$: centre
$$(1, -4)$$, asymptotes $$x = 1$$ and $$y = -4$$. Dividing by $$x - 1$$ gives
$$y = -4 + \frac{6}{x - 1}$$ for $$x \ne 1$$.

At $$x = 4$$ the formula gives $$y = -4 + 2 = -2$$. In the original equation,
$$4 \cdot (-2) + 4 \cdot 4 - (-2) - 10 = -8 + 16 + 2 - 10 = 0$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-hyperbolas-read-off}

For the hyperbola $$\frac{x^2}{16} - \frac{y^2}{9} = 1$$, find the vertices, the foci, the
asymptotes and the eccentricity. Then check :ref{#hyperbola-foci} at the point
$$\left(5, \frac94\right)$$.

:::solution

Here $$a = 4$$ and $$b = 3$$, so $$c = \sqrt{16 + 9} = 5$$. The vertices are $$(\pm 4, 0)$$, the
foci are $$(\pm 5, 0)$$, the asymptotes are $$y = \pm\frac34 x$$, and the eccentricity is
$$e = \frac54$$.

The point is on the curve: $$\frac{25}{16} - \frac{81/16}{9} = \frac{25}{16} - \frac{9}{16} = 1$$.
It sits straight above $$(5, 0)$$, so it is $$\frac94$$ from that focus. From $$(-5, 0)$$ it is
$$\sqrt{10^2 + \left(\frac94\right)^2} = \sqrt{\frac{1681}{16}} = \frac{41}{4}$$. The difference is
$$\frac{41}{4} - \frac{9}{4} = 8 = 2a$$, as the theorem says.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-hyperbolas-turn-negative}

1. Use :ref{#hyperbola-turn} to find the equation of the graph of $$xy = -3$$ turned by
   $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$. Check your answer with the point $$(1, -3)$$.
2. Find $$a$$ and $$b$$ for the turned curve, and use them to find the vertices of $$xy = -3$$.

:::solution

1. By part 1 of :ref{#hyperbola-turn} with $$c = -3$$, the turned graph is $$y^2 - x^2 = -6$$, which
   is $$x^2 - y^2 = 6$$. The turn sends $$(1, -3)$$ to
   $$\left(\frac{1 + 3}{\sqrt2}, \frac{1 - 3}{\sqrt2}\right) = (2\sqrt2, -\sqrt2)$$ by
   :ref{#trigonometry/rotation-formula}, and $$(2\sqrt2)^2 - (-\sqrt2)^2 = 8 - 2 = 6$$.
2. Dividing by $$6$$ gives $$\frac{x^2}{6} - \frac{y^2}{6} = 1$$, so $$a = b = \sqrt6$$ and the
   turned curve has vertices $$(\pm\sqrt6, 0)$$. To get back to $$xy = -3$$, turn by
   $$-\frac{\pi}{4}$$, which undoes the turn (:ref{#trigonometry/radian-turns}). With
   $$\cos\left(-\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}$$ and
   $$\sin\left(-\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = -\frac{1}{\sqrt2}$$, it sends $$(\sqrt6, 0)$$ to
   $$\left(\frac{\sqrt6}{\sqrt2}, -\frac{\sqrt6}{\sqrt2}\right) = (\sqrt3, -\sqrt3)$$. So the
   vertices of $$xy = -3$$ are $$(\sqrt3, -\sqrt3)$$ and $$(-\sqrt3, \sqrt3)$$. Check:
   $$\sqrt3 \cdot (-\sqrt3) = -3$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-hyperbolas-gap}

For the hyperbola $$x^2 - y^2 = 1$$, work out the gap between the upper right branch and the line
$$y = x$$ at $$x = 2$$, both directly and from the formula in :ref{#hyperbola-asymptotes}. How far
right must you go to be sure the gap is less than $$0.001$$?

:::solution

Here $$a = b = 1$$. At $$x = 2$$ the curve has $$y = \sqrt{4 - 1} = \sqrt3 \approx 1.732$$, and the
line has $$y = 2$$, so the gap is $$2 - \sqrt3 \approx 0.268$$. The formula gives
$$\frac{1}{2 + \sqrt3} \approx 0.268$$ too, and in fact
$$\frac{1}{2 + \sqrt3} = \frac{2 - \sqrt3}{(2 + \sqrt3)(2 - \sqrt3)} = \frac{2 - \sqrt3}{4 - 3} = 2 - \sqrt3$$.

By part 3 of the proposition the gap is at most $$\frac{ab}{x} = \frac1x$$, which is less than
$$0.001$$ once $$x > 1000$$. At $$x = 1000$$ itself the gap is
$$\frac{1}{1000 + \sqrt{999\,999}} \approx 0.0005$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-hyperbolas-lines-through-centre}

Let $$m$$ be a number. Show that the line $$y = mx$$ meets the hyperbola
$$\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$$ exactly when $$m^2 < \frac{b^2}{a^2}$$, and that it then
meets it in two points. Describe the answer in a picture.

:::solution

A point $$(x, mx)$$ of the line is on the hyperbola exactly when

```math
\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{m^2x^2}{b^2} = 1, \quad \text{which is} \quad x^2\left(\frac{1}{a^2} - \frac{m^2}{b^2}\right) = 1
```

If the bracket is $$0$$ or negative, the left side is $$0$$ or negative for every $$x$$, so there is
no solution. If the bracket is positive, there are two solutions,
$$x = \pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{1/a^2 - m^2/b^2}}$$. The bracket is positive exactly when
$$\frac{m^2}{b^2} < \frac{1}{a^2}$$, which is $$m^2 < \frac{b^2}{a^2}$$.

In the picture: the lines through the centre that are less steep than the asymptotes cross both
branches, once each. The asymptotes themselves, and every line through the centre steeper than them,
miss the hyperbola altogether.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-hyperbolas-perpendicular-asymptotes}

Show that the asymptotes of $$\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$$ are perpendicular exactly when
$$a = b$$. Then explain why the asymptotes of every graph $$xy = c$$ are perpendicular, in two ways.

:::solution

The asymptotes have slopes $$\frac{b}{a}$$ and $$-\frac{b}{a}$$. By
:ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/perpendicular-slopes}, they are perpendicular exactly when
$$\frac{b}{a} \cdot \left(-\frac{b}{a}\right) = -1$$, which is $$b^2 = a^2$$. Both are positive, so
this is $$a = b$$.

For $$xy = c$$, the asymptotes are the two axes, which are perpendicular. The second way: the graph
of $$xy = c$$ is a turned or flipped copy of a standard hyperbola with $$a = b = \sqrt{2|c|}$$, as
under [The graph of xy = c is a hyperbola](#the-graph-of-xy--c-is-a-hyperbola), so its asymptotes
are the images of two perpendicular lines. An isometry keeps angles
(:ref{#isometries/isometry-angles}), so they are perpendicular.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-hyperbolas-radio}

Two radio stations are $$100$$ km apart and send a pulse at the same instant. A ship hears one pulse
$$\frac{80}{0.3} \approx 267$$ millionths of a second before the other, so it is $$80$$ km nearer to
one station. With the stations at $$(\pm 50, 0)$$, find the equation of the hyperbola the ship is
on. If the ship is due north of the nearer station, how far is it from each station?

:::solution

By :ref{#hyperbola-foci}, $$2a = 80$$, so $$a = 40$$, and $$c = 50$$, so
$$b = \sqrt{2500 - 1600} = 30$$. The hyperbola is $$\frac{x^2}{1600} - \frac{y^2}{900} = 1$$.

Suppose the nearer station is $$(50, 0)$$, so the ship is at $$(50, y)$$ with $$y > 0$$. Then
$$\frac{2500}{1600} - \frac{y^2}{900} = 1$$, so $$y^2 = 900 \cdot \frac{900}{1600} = 506.25$$ and
$$y = 22.5$$. The ship is $$22.5$$ km from the nearer station, and
$$\sqrt{100^2 + 22.5^2} = \sqrt{10\,506.25} = 102.5$$ km from the other. The difference is
$$102.5 - 22.5 = 80$$, as it should be.

:::
