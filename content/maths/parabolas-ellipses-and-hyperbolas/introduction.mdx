Imagine shining a torch at a wall in a dark room. Pointed straight at the wall, the patch of light
is a circle. Tilt the torch and the circle stretches into an oval, and tilt it further still and the
patch runs off along the wall without end, its edge opening out like the arms of a U. The edge of
that patch traces the three curves of this chapter: the **ellipse**, the **parabola** and the
**hyperbola**. In this chapter we'll find their equations, prove what makes each one special, and
see why the torch beam produces all three.

One idea carries the chapter: changing an equation moves its graph in a way you can predict. Replace
$$x$$ by $$x - 3$$ and the graph slides $$3$$ to the right. Replace $$x$$ by $$\frac{x}{2}$$ and it
stretches to twice its width. So we only need to understand three standard equations well. Every
other parabola, ellipse or hyperbola is one of them slid, turned or flipped into place, and its
equation tells you how.

## What this chapter builds on

The curves live in the coordinate plane of [Coordinates](../coordinates/introduction.mdx), and the
moves come from [Adding and scaling points](../adding-and-scaling-points/introduction.mdx) and the
formula for a turn in [Trigonometry](../trigonometry/rotations.mdx). The only new fact taken as
given is a piece of physics, the law of reflection for mirrors (:ref{#reflection-law}), which the
chapter uses to explain satellite dishes. Everything else is proved.

Along the way the chapter keeps two promises from earlier chapters:

- **The graph of a quadratic**: [Quadratics](../quadratics/parabolas.mdx) took from the picture that
  the graph of $$y = ax^2 + bx + c$$ is a U-shaped curve with an axis of symmetry and a lowest or
  highest point. Here it is proved (:ref{#quadratic-parabola}, :ref{#parabolas-remark-proved}).
- **The ellipse**: [Stretching](../area-and-scaling/stretching.mdx) built the ellipse by stretching
  a disc and found its area. Here we find the equation of its edge (:ref{#ellipse-equation}) and
  prove the gardener's two-peg description (:ref{#ellipse-foci}).

**Moving graphs**

| Result                                    | Statement                                                                                      |
| ----------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| :ref{#slide-graph}                        | The slide by $$(h, k)$$ sends the graph of $$E(x, y)$$ to the graph of $$E(x - h, y - k)$$     |
| :ref{#sliding-and-stretching-point-slope} | The line through $$(h, k)$$ with slope $$m$$ is $$y - k = m(x - h)$$                           |
| :ref{#axis-stretch-two}                   | $$D_{a,b}$$ is a stretch away from the $$y$$-axis then one away from the $$x$$-axis            |
| :ref{#stretch-graph}                      | $$D_{a,b}$$ sends the graph of $$E(x, y)$$ to the graph of $$E(\frac{x}{a}, \frac{y}{b})$$     |
| :ref{#flip-graph}                         | The flips give $$E(-x, y)$$, $$E(x, -y)$$ and $$E(y, x)$$                                      |
| :ref{#turn-graph}                         | The turn by $$\varphi$$ gives $$E(x\cos\varphi + y\sin\varphi, -x\sin\varphi + y\cos\varphi)$$ |

**Parabolas**

| Result                                      | Statement                                                                                      |
| ------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| :ref{#quadratic-parabola}                   | $$y = ax^2 + bx + c$$ is $$y = ax^2$$ slid by $$(-\frac{b}{2a}, c - \frac{b^2}{4a})$$          |
| :ref{#parabolas-remark-proved}              | It is symmetric in $$x = h$$, with lowest point when $$a > 0$$ and highest when $$a < 0$$      |
| :ref{#parabola-zoom}                        | Every parabola is similar to $$y = x^2$$                                                       |
| :ref{#line-parabola}                        | A line meets a parabola in $$2$$, $$1$$ or $$0$$ points, by a discriminant                     |
| :ref{#parabola-tangent}                     | The tangent to $$y = ax^2$$ at $$(p, ap^2)$$ is $$y = 2apx - ap^2$$                            |
| :ref{#parabolas-vertex-axis}                | However a parabola is placed, its vertex and axis come out the same                            |
| :ref{#focus-parabola}                       | The points equally far from $$(0, p)$$ and $$y = -p$$ form $$y = \frac{x^2}{4p}$$              |
| :ref{#focus-and-directrix-moves}            | An isometry carries a focus and directrix along with the curve                                 |
| :ref{#parabola-focus-exists}                | Every parabola has a focus and a directrix, and every such curve is a parabola                 |
| :ref{#focus-and-directrix-tangent}          | The tangent at $$P$$ is the perpendicular bisector of $$F$$ and the foot $$N$$                 |
| :ref{#parabola-reflection}                  | Light arriving parallel to the axis reflects through the focus                                 |
| :ref{#focus-and-directrix-every-tangent}    | On any parabola the tangent at $$P$$ is the perpendicular bisector of $$F$$ and the foot $$N$$ |
| :ref{#focus-and-directrix-every-reflection} | On any parabola, light arriving parallel to the axis reflects through the focus                |

**Ellipses**

| Result                          | Statement                                                                       |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| :ref{#ellipse-equation}         | $$D_{a,b}$$ sends the unit circle to $$\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$$  |
| :ref{#ellipses-parametrisation} | The points of the ellipse are $$(a\cos t, b\sin t)$$                            |
| :ref{#ellipse-foci}             | $$d(P, F_1) + d(P, F_2) = 2a$$ exactly on the ellipse, with foci $$(\pm c, 0)$$ |
| :ref{#ellipses-moved-foci}      | A moved ellipse keeps the sum property with its moved foci                      |
| :ref{#ellipses-zoom}            | A zoom keeps $$\frac{b}{a}$$, and the same ratio means similar                  |
| :ref{#ellipses-reflection}      | A ray from one focus reflects through the other (not proved here)               |

**Hyperbolas**

| Result                          | Statement                                                                    |
| ------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| :ref{#hyperbolas-moved-product} | $$xy + px + qy + r = 0$$ is $$(x + q)(y + p) = pq - r$$                      |
| :ref{#hyperbola-turn}           | The turn by $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$ sends $$xy = c$$ to $$y^2 - x^2 = 2c$$         |
| :ref{#hyperbola-asymptotes}     | The curve never meets $$y = \pm\frac{b}{a}x$$ and comes as close as you like |
| :ref{#hyperbola-foci}           | $$\lvert d(P, F_1) - d(P, F_2) \rvert = 2a$$ exactly on the hyperbola        |

**The cone and the general equation**

| Result                                 | Statement                                                                                                                       |
| -------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| :ref{#slicing-a-cone-flat-coordinates} | $$u = x\sqrt{1 + m^2}$$, $$v = y$$ are distance-keeping coordinates on $$z = mx + 1$$                                           |
| :ref{#cone-slices}                     | The plane $$z = mx + 1$$ cuts a circle, an ellipse, a parabola or a hyperbola as $$m = 0$$, $$0 < m < 1$$, $$m = 1$$, $$m > 1$$ |
| :ref{#slicing-a-cone-move-into-place}  | An isometry moves a point to $$O$$ and a line not through it to $$x = -d$$, keeping distances to the line                       |
| :ref{#focus-directrix-e}               | $$d(P, F) = e \cdot d(P, L)$$ gives an ellipse, a parabola or a hyperbola as $$e < 1$$, $$e = 1$$, $$e > 1$$                    |
| :ref{#no-xy-term}                      | Without an $$xy$$ term, the signs of $$A$$ and $$C$$ name the family                                                            |
| :ref{#remove-xy}                       | Turning by $$\theta$$ with $$\tan 2\theta = \frac{B}{A - C}$$ removes the $$xy$$ term                                           |
| :ref{#conic-discriminant}              | $$B^2 - 4AC$$ is unchanged by turns: negative ellipse, zero parabola, positive hyperbola, or a degenerate case                  |
