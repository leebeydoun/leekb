Imagine climbing a tall ladder in fog so thick that you can't see the top or count the rungs. You
can still be sure of reaching any rung you like, as long as two things hold. You can get onto the
bottom rung, and from whichever rung you're standing on, you can reach the one above. Nobody has to
tell you how tall the ladder is, because those two facts carry you past rung 10, rung 1000 and every
rung after.

<FoggyLadder caption="A ladder whose top is lost in fog. The bottom rung is the base case, and the arrows are the step: from any rung you can reach the next, so every rung gets reached." />

That way of reaching every rung is **induction**. The guide
[Proof by induction](../basic-proofs/induction.mdx) set it out as :ref{#basic-proofs/induction},
with the bottom rung as the base case and the climb from one rung to the next as the inductive step.
It proved the formula for $$1 + 2 + \cdots + n$$, a fact about divisibility, and an inequality that
only starts at $$n = 5$$. In this guide we'll use induction on harder statements, including ones
where a step needs more than the rung right below it, and learn to find the broken step in a proof
that only looks right.

## Leaning on every rung below

Think about how you'd show that every whole number from $$2$$ on can be built by multiplying primes,
the numbers like $$2$$, $$3$$, $$5$$ and $$7$$ that don't split. To build $$360$$, you split it as
$$8 \cdot 45$$ and then build $$8$$ and $$45$$. Knowing about $$359$$, the number right below, is no
help at all. What you need is every number below $$360$$, since the pieces could be any of them.

On the ladder this changes nothing. By the time you're on rung $$k$$, you've stood on every rung
below it too, so you may lean on any of them. The theorem below says the same for statements, and
its proof is ordinary induction applied to the whole climb so far.

:::theorem[Strong induction]{#strong-induction}

Suppose that for each positive integer $$n$$ there is a statement $$P(n)$$, and that:

1. **Base case:** $$P(1)$$ is true.
2. **Strong inductive step:** for every positive integer $$k$$, if $$P(1)$$, $$P(2)$$, …, $$P(k)$$
   are all true, then $$P(k + 1)$$ is true.

Then $$P(n)$$ is true for every positive integer $$n$$. The same holds with any whole number $$n_0$$
in place of $$1$$: if $$P(n_0)$$ is true, and for every $$k \ge n_0$$ the truth of $$P(n_0)$$, …,
$$P(k)$$ gives $$P(k + 1)$$, then $$P(n)$$ is true for every $$n \ge n_0$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_induction#Complete_(strong)_induction"}

Let $$Q(n)$$ be the statement "$$P(1)$$, $$P(2)$$, …, $$P(n)$$ are all true". We prove $$Q(n)$$ for
every positive integer $$n$$ by ordinary induction (:ref{#basic-proofs/induction}).

**Base case.** $$Q(1)$$ says that $$P(1)$$ is true, which is part 1.

**Inductive step.** Suppose $$Q(k)$$ is true for some positive integer $$k$$.

1. Then $$P(1)$$, …, $$P(k)$$ are all true, because that is what $$Q(k)$$ says.
2. So $$P(k + 1)$$ is true, by part 2.
3. So $$P(1)$$, …, $$P(k + 1)$$ are all true, which is $$Q(k + 1)$$.

**Conclusion.** By induction, $$Q(n)$$ is true for every $$n$$. Each $$Q(n)$$ includes $$P(n)$$, so
$$P(n)$$ is true for every $$n$$.

For a start at $$n_0$$, let $$Q(n)$$ say "$$P(n_0)$$, …, $$P(n)$$ are all true" and run the same
argument as an induction that starts at $$n_0$$, as in
[Starting somewhere else](../basic-proofs/induction.mdx#starting-somewhere-else).

:::

So strong induction proves nothing that ordinary induction can't. It lets you write the step the way
the problem wants it: to reach $$P(k + 1)$$, use whichever earlier cases help.

## Primes, the pieces every number is built from

Try splitting the numbers from $$2$$ to $$12$$ into smaller factors. Some split, like
$$6 = 2 \cdot 3$$ and $$12 = 3 \cdot 4$$. Others, like $$7$$ and $$11$$, don't split at all: the
only way to write $$7$$ as a product of two positive whole numbers is $$1 \cdot 7$$. Numbers that
don't split are the building blocks.

:::definition[Prime]{#prime}

A **prime** is a whole number $$p \ge 2$$ whose only positive divisors (:ref{#numbers/divides}) are
$$1$$ and $$p$$. A whole number $$n \ge 2$$ that is not prime is called **composite**.

:::

The primes up to $$30$$ are $$2$$, $$3$$, $$5$$, $$7$$, $$11$$, $$13$$, $$17$$, $$19$$, $$23$$ and
$$29$$. The number $$1$$ is left out on purpose. Leaving it out keeps the factor lists below from
filling up with $$1$$s, which would change nothing in a product.

<FactorTree caption="A factor tree for 360. Each number that isn't prime splits into two smaller factors, and the branches stop at primes (green). Reading off the ends of the branches, 360 = 2 · 2 · 2 · 3 · 3 · 5." />

The factor tree for $$360$$ is strong induction at work. To build $$360$$ we split it as
$$8 \cdot 45$$ and built each piece, and to build $$8$$ we split it as $$2 \cdot 4$$. Every split
leans on numbers far below the one being split. So the proof that every number has a tree like this
needs every rung below, not only the last one.

:::theorem[Every number is a product of primes]{#prime-factorisation}

Every whole number $$n \ge 2$$ is a prime or a product of primes.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_induction#Example:_prime_factorization"}

Let $$P(n)$$ be the statement "$$n$$ is a prime or a product of primes". We use strong induction
(:ref{#strong-induction}) starting at $$n_0 = 2$$.

**Base case.** The only positive divisors of $$2$$ are $$1$$ and $$2$$, so $$2$$ is prime, and
$$P(2)$$ is true.

**Strong inductive step.** Let $$k \ge 2$$, and suppose $$P(2)$$, …, $$P(k)$$ are all true. If
$$k + 1$$ is prime, then $$P(k + 1)$$ is true. Otherwise $$k + 1$$ has a positive divisor $$d$$
other than $$1$$ and $$k + 1$$, so $$k + 1 = de$$ for a whole number $$e$$.

1. $$d \ge 2$$, since $$d$$ is positive and not $$1$$.
2. $$e \ge 2$$. Indeed $$e$$ is positive, since $$d$$ and $$de$$ are. And $$e = 1$$ would make
   $$d = k + 1$$, which we ruled out.
3. $$d \le k$$ and $$e \le k$$. From $$e \ge 2$$, $$k + 1 = de \ge 2d > d$$, and in the same way
   $$k + 1 \ge 2e > e$$.

So $$d$$ and $$e$$ both lie between $$2$$ and $$k$$, and $$P(d)$$ and $$P(e)$$ are among the cases
we may assume. Each of $$d$$ and $$e$$ is a prime or a product of primes. Writing the primes for
$$d$$ next to the primes for $$e$$ gives $$k + 1 = de$$ as a product of primes, which is
$$P(k + 1)$$.

**Conclusion.** By strong induction, $$P(n)$$ is true for every $$n \ge 2$$.

:::

Grouping the primes for $$360$$ with powers gives $$360 = 2^3 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 5$$. You can check it:
$$8 \cdot 9 = 72$$ and $$72 \cdot 5 = 360$$. Splitting $$360$$ another way, as $$10 \cdot 36$$, ends
with the same primes, $$2 \cdot 5 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 3$$. That the primes always come out
the same, apart from their order, is true, but its proof needs another fact about primes, and we
don't prove it here.

## Making amounts from stamps

Imagine a post office machine that sells only 4p and 5p stamps, and a parcel that needs a given
amount exactly. Some amounts are out of reach. You can make $$8$$, $$9$$ and $$10$$ (as $$4 + 4$$,
$$4 + 5$$ and $$5 + 5$$), but there is no way to make $$11$$. Writing out the amounts from $$1$$ to
$$16$$ shows a pattern:

| Amount              | Made from 4p and 5p | Amount | Made from 4p and 5p |
| ------------------- | ------------------- | ------ | ------------------- |
| $$1$$, $$2$$, $$3$$ | no                  | $$10$$ | $$5 + 5$$           |
| $$4$$               | $$4$$               | $$11$$ | no                  |
| $$5$$               | $$5$$               | $$12$$ | $$4 + 4 + 4$$       |
| $$6$$, $$7$$        | no                  | $$13$$ | $$4 + 4 + 5$$       |
| $$8$$               | $$4 + 4$$           | $$14$$ | $$4 + 5 + 5$$       |
| $$9$$               | $$4 + 5$$           | $$15$$ | $$5 + 5 + 5$$       |

From $$12$$ on, four amounts in a row can be made: $$12$$, $$13$$, $$14$$ and $$15$$. Adding one 4p
stamp to each gives the next four, $$16$$ to $$19$$, and adding another gives $$20$$ to $$23$$. So
the gaps stop at $$11$$, and each new amount leans on the amount four below it, not the one right
below. That is a job for strong induction.

:::proposition[Stamps of 4 and 5]{#stamps}

Every whole number $$n \ge 12$$ can be written as $$n = 4a + 5b$$ for whole numbers $$a \ge 0$$ and
$$b \ge 0$$. So every amount from 12p up can be made exactly from 4p and 5p stamps.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_induction#Example:_forming_dollar_amounts_by_coins"}

Let $$P(n)$$ be the statement "$$n = 4a + 5b$$ for some whole numbers $$a, b \ge 0$$". We use strong
induction (:ref{#strong-induction}) starting at $$n_0 = 12$$.

**Base case.** $$12 = 4 \cdot 3 + 5 \cdot 0$$, so $$P(12)$$ is true.

**Strong inductive step.** Let $$k \ge 12$$, and suppose $$P(12)$$, …, $$P(k)$$ are all true. We
show $$P(k + 1)$$, in two cases.

- **If $$k + 1$$ is $$13$$, $$14$$ or $$15$$**, it is in the table: $$13 = 4 \cdot 2 + 5 \cdot 1$$,
  $$14 = 4 \cdot 1 + 5 \cdot 2$$ and $$15 = 4 \cdot 0 + 5 \cdot 3$$.
- **If $$k + 1 \ge 16$$**, then $$k + 1 - 4 = k - 3$$ is at least $$12$$ and at most $$k$$, so
  $$P(k - 3)$$ is one of the cases we may assume. It gives $$k - 3 = 4a + 5b$$ with $$a, b \ge 0$$.
  Adding $$4$$ to both sides, $$k + 1 = 4(a + 1) + 5b$$, which is $$P(k + 1)$$.

**Conclusion.** By strong induction, $$P(n)$$ is true for every $$n \ge 12$$.

:::

The amounts $$12$$ to $$15$$ act as four base cases. The step reaches back four places, so the first
amount it can handle is $$16$$, and every amount below that has to be checked by hand. Pick an
amount in the stamp maker below, and watch the chain of steps run back to one of those four.

## Bernoulli's inequality

Picture savings that grow by $$1\%$$ every year. Each year the amount is multiplied by $$1.01$$, so
after $$n$$ years the starting amount has been multiplied by $$1.01^n$$. Each year also adds $$1\%$$
of the current amount, which is at least $$1\%$$ of the starting amount. So after $$n$$ years the
savings have grown by at least $$n\%$$, and $$1.01^n \ge 1 + 0.01n$$.

The same holds for any growth rate $$h$$, and even for a loss, as long as you never lose more than
everything. In the inequality below, $$h$$ is the fraction gained each year, and a negative $$h$$ is
a fraction lost. The guide [Geometric series](./geometric-series.mdx) uses it to show that the
powers of a number between $$0$$ and $$1$$ shrink to $$0$$.

:::proposition[Bernoulli's inequality]{#bernoulli}

For every number $$h \ge -1$$ and every whole number $$n \ge 0$$,

```math
(1 + h)^n \ge 1 + nh
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernoulli%27s_inequality"}

Fix $$h \ge -1$$, and use induction on $$n$$, starting at $$n = 0$$.

**Base case.** For $$n = 0$$ both sides are $$1$$, since $$(1 + h)^0 = 1$$ and
$$1 + 0 \cdot h = 1$$.

**Inductive step.** Let $$k \ge 0$$ and suppose $$(1 + h)^k \ge 1 + kh$$.

1. The number $$1 + h$$ is at least $$0$$, because $$h \ge -1$$.
2. So multiplying both sides by $$1 + h$$ keeps the direction:
   $$(1 + h)^{k + 1} \ge (1 + kh)(1 + h)$$. When $$1 + h > 0$$ this is part 4 of
   :ref{#real-numbers/inequality-rules}, and when $$1 + h = 0$$ both sides are $$0$$.
3. Multiplying out, $$(1 + kh)(1 + h) = 1 + (k + 1)h + kh^2$$.
4. The last term $$kh^2$$ is at least $$0$$, so the right side is at least $$1 + (k + 1)h$$.

Putting steps 2 to 4 together, $$(1 + h)^{k + 1} \ge 1 + (k + 1)h$$.

**Conclusion.** By induction, $$(1 + h)^n \ge 1 + nh$$ for every $$n \ge 0$$.

:::

With $$h = 0.01$$ and $$n = 100$$, the inequality says $$1.01^{100} \ge 1 + 100 \cdot 0.01 = 2$$. So
savings growing by $$1\%$$ a year at least double in a century. In fact
$$1.01^{100} \approx 2.705$$, so the inequality is far from tight here, but it needs no calculator.
The case $$h \ge 0$$ was an exercise in the induction guide
(:ref{#basic-proofs/ex-induction-bernoulli}), and the proof above also covers losses.

The condition $$h \ge -1$$ is needed for step 1. With $$h = -4$$ and $$n = 3$$, the left side is
$$(-3)^3 = -27$$ and the right side is $$1 - 12 = -11$$, and $$-27$$ is smaller.

## Functions that turn adding into adding

Imagine a shop that sells rice by weight, where the price of $$x$$ kilos and $$y$$ kilos bought
together is the price of $$x$$ kilos plus the price of $$y$$ kilos. Then 3 kilos cost the same as 1
kilo three times over: $$f(3) = f(1 + 1 + 1) = f(1) + f(1) + f(1)$$. Induction turns that into a
statement for every whole number.

:::proposition[Additive functions on whole numbers]{#additive-integers}

Let $$f$$ be a function from the numbers to the numbers (:ref{#functions/function}) with
$$f(x + y) = f(x) + f(y)$$ for all numbers $$x$$ and $$y$$. Then $$f(0) = 0$$, and $$f(n) = n f(1)$$
for every whole number $$n \ge 1$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy%27s_functional_equation#Solutions_over_the_rational_numbers"}

**The value at 0.** Put $$x = y = 0$$: $$f(0) = f(0 + 0) = f(0) + f(0)$$. Subtracting $$f(0)$$ from
both sides gives $$0 = f(0)$$.

**Whole numbers.** Let $$P(n)$$ be the statement "$$f(n) = n f(1)$$", and use induction on $$n$$.

- **Base case.** $$P(1)$$ says $$f(1) = 1 \cdot f(1)$$, which is true.
- **Inductive step.** Suppose $$f(k) = k f(1)$$. Then

  ```math
  \begin{aligned}
  f(k + 1) &= f(k) + f(1) &&\qquad \text{the rule, with } x = k \text{ and } y = 1 \\
  &= k f(1) + f(1) &&\qquad \text{the assumption } P(k) \\
  &= (k + 1) f(1) &&\qquad \text{taking out the factor } f(1)
  \end{aligned}
  ```

  which is $$P(k + 1)$$.

**Conclusion.** By induction, $$f(n) = n f(1)$$ for every whole number $$n \ge 1$$.

:::

So if 1 kilo of rice costs £2, the rule forces 7 kilos to cost seven times £2, which is £14. One of
the exercises below carries this on to negative whole numbers.

## Counting mappings

Imagine painting a row of three doors, each one blue or orange. A painting gives each door one
colour, so it is a mapping from the set of doors to the set of colours
(:ref{#mappings-and-permutations/mapping}). Door 1 has 2 choices, door 2 has 2, and door 3 has 2, so
there are $$2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 = 8$$ paintings. Sorting them by the colour of the last door shows
where the doubling comes from.

<PaintedDoors caption="The eight ways to paint three doors blue or orange. Each group fixes the colour of door 3, and within it doors 1 and 2 run through their four paintings. So each new door doubles the count." />

Each group holds a full copy of the four ways to paint doors 1 and 2. Adding one more door with
$$m$$ colours multiplies the count by $$m$$, and that step from $$k$$ doors to $$k + 1$$ doors is
exactly an inductive step. Counting here means listing each mapping exactly once, as in
:ref{#mappings-and-permutations/one-to-one-and-onto-count}.

:::theorem[Counting mappings]{#counting-mappings}

Let $$T$$ be a set with $$m \ge 1$$ elements. For every $$n \ge 1$$, if $$S$$ is a set with $$n$$
elements, there are exactly $$m^n$$ mappings from $$S$$ to $$T$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Cardinality_of_Set_of_All_Mappings"}

Let $$P(n)$$ be the statement "every set with $$n$$ elements has exactly $$m^n$$ mappings to $$T$$",
and use induction on $$n$$.

**Base case.** Let $$S = \{x_1\}$$. A mapping from $$S$$ to $$T$$ is fixed by the one value
$$f(x_1)$$, which can be any element of $$T$$. So there is one mapping for each element of $$T$$,
and that is $$m = m^1$$ mappings.

**Inductive step.** Suppose $$P(k)$$ is true, and let $$S$$ have $$k + 1$$ elements, listed as
$$x_1, \ldots, x_{k + 1}$$. Let $$S'$$ be the set $$\{x_1, \ldots, x_k\}$$, which has $$k$$
elements.

1. A mapping $$f: S \to T$$ is fixed by two things: what it does on $$S'$$, which is a mapping
   $$g: S' \to T$$, and the value $$f(x_{k + 1})$$, which is some $$t$$ in $$T$$.
2. In the other direction, any mapping $$g: S' \to T$$ and any $$t$$ in $$T$$ give exactly one
   mapping $$f$$: send each $$x_i$$ in $$S'$$ to $$g(x_i)$$, and send $$x_{k + 1}$$ to $$t$$.
3. So for each $$t$$ in $$T$$, the mappings $$f$$ with $$f(x_{k + 1}) = t$$ match the mappings
   $$g: S' \to T$$ one for one. By $$P(k)$$ there are $$m^k$$ of those.
4. Every mapping $$f$$ has exactly one value at $$x_{k + 1}$$, so listing the $$m$$ groups one after
   another lists every mapping exactly once. That makes $$m \cdot m^k = m^{k + 1}$$ mappings.

So $$P(k + 1)$$ is true.

**Conclusion.** By induction, $$P(n)$$ is true for every $$n \ge 1$$.

:::

A quiz with 4 questions, each answered A, B, C or D, has an answer sheet that is a mapping from the
4 questions to the 4 letters. So there are $$4^4 = 256$$ possible answer sheets. The mappings from
$$\{1, 2, 3\}$$ to itself number $$3^3 = 27$$, and only $$3! = 6$$ of those are permutations
(:ref{#mappings-and-permutations/permutation-count}). The other 21 send two numbers to the same
place.

## A proof that goes wrong

Every induction proof has the same two parts, so a false one has to hide its mistake in one of them.
Here is a "proof" of something plainly false. Read it once and see whether it convinces you, before
we find the broken step.

> **Claim.** In every box of $$n$$ crayons, all the crayons are the same colour.
>
> **Base case.** A box of $$1$$ crayon has only one colour in it.
>
> **Inductive step.** Suppose every box of $$k$$ crayons is all one colour, and take a box of
> $$k + 1$$. Lift out one crayon, A. The other $$k$$ are one colour, by the assumption. Put A back
> and lift out a different crayon, B. Again the other $$k$$ are one colour. The crayons other than A
> and B were in both groups, so the two groups share a colour, and all $$k + 1$$ crayons match.

The step sounds right for big boxes, and it is right for them. The trouble is the sentence "the
crayons other than A and B were in both groups". Count them: there are $$k - 1$$, and when $$k = 1$$
there are none.

<CrayonBoxes caption="In a box of 4 crayons, leaving out B and leaving out A gives two groups of 3 that share the two middle crayons, so the groups must match. In a box of 2, the groups are A alone and B alone, nothing joins them, and A and B can differ." />

So the step from $$1$$ crayon to $$2$$ fails. The base case holds, and every later step works from
$$k = 2$$ on, but the ladder is missing its second rung, so nothing above the first rung is ever
reached. A box with one blue and one orange crayon breaks the claim, and the step from $$1$$ to
$$2$$ is exactly where.

The crayon proof and the slips named in the induction guide suggest what to check in any induction
proof of your own:

1. **Say exactly what $$P(n)$$ is**, and the number it starts from.
2. **Check every base case the step needs.** A step that reaches back four places, as with the
   stamps, needs four base cases.
3. **Test the step at its smallest $$k$$ with real numbers.** The crayon step looks fine in general
   and fails at $$k = 1$$.
4. **Assume $$P(k)$$ and prove $$P(k + 1)$$.** In the step you may assume $$P(k)$$, or for strong
   induction every case up to $$k$$, but never $$P(k + 1)$$ itself, which is what you are trying to
   reach.
5. **Don't stop at examples.** Checking the first few cases, however many, tells you what to prove.
   It isn't a proof.

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on induction in practice, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-induction-in-practice-factor}

Write $$84$$ and $$1001$$ as products of primes, showing each split you make. Then write each answer
with powers.

:::solution

Split off a small factor each time, as in the factor tree for $$360$$:

- $$84 = 2 \cdot 42 = 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 21 = 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 7$$, so
  $$84 = 2^2 \cdot 3 \cdot 7$$. Check: $$4 \cdot 3 \cdot 7 = 84$$.
- $$1001$$ has no factor $$2$$, $$3$$ or $$5$$, but trying $$7$$ works: $$1001 = 7 \cdot 143$$, and
  $$143 = 11 \cdot 13$$. So $$1001 = 7 \cdot 11 \cdot 13$$, with every prime appearing once. Check:
  $$7 \cdot 11 = 77$$ and $$77 \cdot 13 = 1001$$.

Each of $$2$$, $$3$$, $$7$$, $$11$$ and $$13$$ is prime (:ref{#prime}), so the splitting stops
there.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-induction-in-practice-two-ways}

Make $$29$$p from 4p and 5p stamps in two different ways. Then show that $$11$$p can't be made at
all.

:::solution

- $$29 = 4 \cdot 6 + 5 = 24 + 5$$: six 4p stamps and one 5p stamp.
- $$29 = 4 + 5 \cdot 5 = 4 + 25$$: one 4p stamp and five 5p stamps.

For $$11$$p, the number of 5p stamps can only be $$0$$, $$1$$ or $$2$$, since three would already
make $$15$$p. That leaves $$11$$, $$6$$ or $$1$$ pence to make from 4p stamps, and none of these is
a multiple of $$4$$. So $$11$$p can't be made, which is why :ref{#stamps} starts at $$12$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-induction-in-practice-bernoulli-numbers}

Use :ref{#bernoulli} to show that $$1.05^{20} \ge 2$$ and that $$0.98^{10} \ge 0.8$$. Then find both
powers on a calculator and compare.

:::solution

1. With $$h = 0.05$$ and $$n = 20$$: $$1.05^{20} \ge 1 + 20 \cdot 0.05 = 2$$. A calculator gives
   $$1.05^{20} \approx 2.653$$.
2. With $$h = -0.02$$, which is at least $$-1$$, and $$n = 10$$:
   $$0.98^{10} \ge 1 + 10 \cdot (-0.02) = 0.8$$. A calculator gives $$0.98^{10} \approx 0.817$$.

In both cases the true power is a little above the bound. In money terms, losing $$2\%$$ a year for
ten years leaves at least $$80\%$$ of what you started with.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-induction-in-practice-three-seven}

Prove that every amount of at least 12p can be made from 3p and 7p stamps. Say how many base cases
you need, and why.

:::solution

Let $$P(n)$$ say "$$n = 3a + 7b$$ for whole numbers $$a, b \ge 0$$". The step will add one 3p stamp
to the amount three below, so it reaches back three places and needs three base cases:

- $$12 = 3 \cdot 4$$
- $$13 = 3 \cdot 2 + 7$$
- $$14 = 7 \cdot 2$$

**Strong inductive step.** Let $$k \ge 14$$ and suppose $$P(12)$$, …, $$P(k)$$ are true. Then
$$k + 1 \ge 15$$, so $$k - 2$$ is between $$12$$ and $$k$$, and $$P(k - 2)$$ gives
$$k - 2 = 3a + 7b$$. Adding $$3$$, $$k + 1 = 3(a + 1) + 7b$$.

**Conclusion.** By :ref{#strong-induction} with the three base cases, $$P(n)$$ holds for every
$$n \ge 12$$. The amount $$11$$ is out of reach: with no 7p stamp, $$11$$ pence are left for 3p
stamps, and with one, $$4$$ pence are left, and neither is a multiple of $$3$$. So $$12$$ is the
right place to start.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-induction-in-practice-negative}

Let $$f$$ be a function with $$f(x + y) = f(x) + f(y)$$ for all numbers $$x$$ and $$y$$. Using
:ref{#additive-integers}, show that $$f(-n) = -n f(1)$$ for every whole number $$n \ge 1$$.

:::solution

Put $$x = n$$ and $$y = -n$$ into the rule:

```math
f(n) + f(-n) = f(n + (-n)) = f(0) = 0
```

using $$f(0) = 0$$ from :ref{#additive-integers}. So $$f(-n) = -f(n)$$, and since $$f(n) = n f(1)$$,
this gives $$f(-n) = -n f(1)$$. So $$f(n) = n f(1)$$ holds for every whole number $$n$$, positive,
zero or negative.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-induction-in-practice-mappings}

1. A bike lock has 4 dials, each showing a digit from 0 to 9. Count its settings.
2. Count the mappings from $$\{1, 2, 3\}$$ to itself, and count how many of them are not one to one.

:::solution

1. A setting gives each of the 4 dials one of 10 digits, so it is a mapping from a set of 4 dials to
   a set of 10 digits. By :ref{#counting-mappings} there are $$10^4 = 10000$$ settings.
2. There are $$3^3 = 27$$ mappings, by :ref{#counting-mappings}. The one to one mappings from
   $$\{1, 2, 3\}$$ to itself are the permutations, and there are $$3! = 6$$ of them
   (:ref{#mappings-and-permutations/permutation-count}). So $$27 - 6 = 21$$ mappings are not one to
   one.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-induction-in-practice-find-the-flaw}

Here is a "proof" that every amount from 4p up can be made from 4p and 5p stamps. Find the amount
where the claim fails, and the exact place where the proof goes wrong.

> **Base case.** $$4 = 4$$. **Strong step.** Suppose every amount from $$4$$ to $$k$$ can be made.
> Then $$k + 1 = (k - 3) + 4$$, and $$k - 3$$ can be made by the assumption, so adding a 4p stamp
> makes $$k + 1$$.

:::solution

The claim fails at $$6$$p: one 5p stamp leaves $$1$$p, and no 5p stamps leaves $$6$$p, and neither
is a multiple of $$4$$.

The proof uses $$P(k - 3)$$, but the assumption only covers amounts from $$4$$ to $$k$$. For
$$k + 1 = 5$$, $$6$$ or $$7$$, the amount $$k - 3$$ is $$1$$, $$2$$ or $$3$$, which is below $$4$$,
so the assumption says nothing about it. The step reaches back four places, so it needs four base
cases in a row, and that is why :ref{#stamps} starts at $$12$$ with the base cases $$12$$ to $$15$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-induction-in-practice-fibonacci}

The numbers $$F_1 = 1$$, $$F_2 = 1$$ and $$F_{n + 1} = F_n + F_{n - 1}$$ for $$n \ge 2$$ begin
$$1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13$$. Prove by strong induction that $$F_n \le 2^n$$ for every $$n \ge 1$$.

:::solution

Let $$P(n)$$ say "$$F_n \le 2^n$$". The step uses the two cases before it, so check two base cases:
$$F_1 = 1 \le 2$$ and $$F_2 = 1 \le 4$$.

**Strong inductive step.** Let $$k \ge 2$$ and suppose $$P(1)$$, …, $$P(k)$$ are true. Then

```math
\begin{aligned}
F_{k + 1} &= F_k + F_{k - 1} &&\qquad \text{the rule for the numbers} \\
&\le 2^k + 2^{k - 1} &&\qquad P(k) \text{ and } P(k - 1) \\
&\le 2^k + 2^k = 2^{k + 1} &&\qquad 2^{k - 1} \le 2^k
\end{aligned}
```

which is $$P(k + 1)$$. Here $$k - 1 \ge 1$$, so $$P(k - 1)$$ is one of the cases assumed.

**Conclusion.** By :ref{#strong-induction}, $$F_n \le 2^n$$ for every $$n \ge 1$$. For example,
$$F_7 = 13 \le 128$$.

:::
