These exercises use the whole chapter, and most need ideas from more than one guide. They start
gently and get harder towards the end. Try each one before you open its solution, and name the rule
or result behind each step, as the guides do.

One habit carries most of them: work out the first few cases by hand before reaching for a proof. A
small table shows you the pattern, tells you what the inductive step has to do, and gives you a
check at the end.

## Induction

::::exercise{#ex-induction-and-sums-ch-nine}

Prove that $$9$$ divides $$4^n + 15n - 1$$ for every whole number $$n \ge 1$$.

:::solution

Check first: $$n = 1, 2, 3$$ give $$18$$, $$45$$ and $$108$$, all multiples of $$9$$.

**Base case.** For $$n = 1$$, $$4 + 15 - 1 = 18 = 9 \cdot 2$$.

**Inductive step.** Suppose $$9$$ divides $$4^k + 15k - 1$$. Going from $$4^k$$ to $$4^{k + 1}$$
multiplies by $$4$$, so bring in four copies of the number we know about:

```math
\begin{aligned}
4^{k + 1} + 15(k + 1) - 1 &= 4 \cdot 4^k + 15k + 14 && \text{expand} \\
&= 4(4^k + 15k - 1) - 60k + 4 + 15k + 14 && \text{add and take away } 4(15k - 1) \\
&= 4(4^k + 15k - 1) - 45k + 18 && \text{collect terms}
\end{aligned}
```

The first term is $$4$$ times a multiple of $$9$$ by assumption, and $$45k = 9 \cdot 5k$$ and
$$18 = 9 \cdot 2$$ are multiples of $$9$$, so the whole is a multiple of $$9$$
(:ref{#numbers/multiples}). By :ref{#basic-proofs/induction}, the claim holds for every $$n \ge 1$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-induction-and-sums-ch-factorial}

Prove that $$n! > 2^n$$ for every whole number $$n \ge 4$$.

:::solution

Check first: $$4! = 24 > 16$$, $$5! = 120 > 32$$.

**Base case.** $$n = 4$$: $$24 > 16$$.

**Inductive step.** Let $$k \ge 4$$ and suppose $$k! > 2^k$$. Then

```math
\begin{aligned}
(k + 1)! &= (k + 1) \cdot k! && \text{definition of the factorial} \\
&> (k + 1) \cdot 2^k && \text{the assumption, times } k + 1 > 0 \\
&> 2 \cdot 2^k = 2^{k + 1} && \text{since } k + 1 \ge 5 > 2
\end{aligned}
```

The first step is :ref{#numbers/factorial}, and multiplying an inequality by a positive number keeps
it (:ref{#real-numbers/inequality-rules}). Induction from $$4$$, as in
[Induction](../basic-proofs/induction.mdx#starting-somewhere-else), proves the claim.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-induction-and-sums-ch-binary}

Prove that every whole number $$n \ge 1$$ is a sum of different powers of $$2$$, for example
$$13 = 8 + 4 + 1$$.

:::solution

Use strong induction (:ref{#strong-induction}). For $$n = 1$$, $$1 = 2^0$$.

Suppose every number from $$1$$ to $$k$$ is a sum of different powers of $$2$$, and look at
$$k + 1$$. Let $$2^m$$ be the largest power of $$2$$ with $$2^m \le k + 1$$, so
$$k + 1 < 2^{m + 1}$$. If $$k + 1 = 2^m$$ we are done. Otherwise $$r = k + 1 - 2^m$$ is between
$$1$$ and $$k$$, so by assumption $$r$$ is a sum of different powers of $$2$$. Each of them is less
than $$2^m$$, because $$r < 2^{m + 1} - 2^m = 2^m$$. So $$k + 1 = 2^m + r$$ is a sum of different
powers of $$2$$, with $$2^m$$ not repeated.

Check with $$13$$: the largest power of $$2$$ up to $$13$$ is $$8$$, leaving $$5$$; the largest up
to $$5$$ is $$4$$, leaving $$1$$. So $$13 = 8 + 4 + 1$$.

:::

## Sums

::::exercise{#ex-induction-and-sums-ch-triple-products}

Show that $$k(k + 1)(k + 2)(k + 3) - (k - 1)k(k + 1)(k + 2) = 4k(k + 1)(k + 2)$$, and use it to find
a formula for $$1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 + 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 4 + \cdots + n(n + 1)(n + 2)$$. Check it at
$$n = 3$$.

:::solution

Both products on the left share the factor $$k(k + 1)(k + 2)$$. Taking it out leaves
$$(k + 3) - (k - 1) = 4$$, which gives the first claim.

So with $$a_k = \frac{k(k + 1)(k + 2)(k + 3)}{4}$$, each term $$k(k + 1)(k + 2)$$ is
$$a_k - a_{k - 1}$$. Here $$a_0 = 0$$, so by :ref{#telescoping},

```math
1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 + 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 4 + \cdots + n(n + 1)(n + 2) = a_n - a_0 = \frac{n(n + 1)(n + 2)(n + 3)}{4}
```

Check at $$n = 3$$: $$6 + 24 + 60 = 90$$, and $$\frac{3 \cdot 4 \cdot 5 \cdot 6}{4} = 90$$. The same
trick with two factors gives $$1 \cdot 2 + \cdots + n(n + 1) = \frac{n(n + 1)(n + 2)}{3}$$, with no
sum of squares needed.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-induction-and-sums-ch-roots}

Find $$\displaystyle \sum_{k=1}^{99} \frac{1}{\sqrt{k} + \sqrt{k + 1}}$$ exactly.

:::solution

Multiply the top and bottom of each term by $$\sqrt{k + 1} - \sqrt{k}$$:

```math
\frac{1}{\sqrt{k} + \sqrt{k + 1}} = \frac{\sqrt{k + 1} - \sqrt{k}}{(k + 1) - k} = \sqrt{k + 1} - \sqrt{k}
```

So the sum telescopes (:ref{#telescoping}) with $$a_k = \sqrt{k + 1}$$, and equals
$$\sqrt{100} - \sqrt{1} = 9$$. Check the first term: $$\frac{1}{1 + \sqrt2} \approx 0.414$$ and
$$\sqrt2 - 1 \approx 0.414$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-induction-and-sums-ch-weighted}

Prove that $$1 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot 2^2 + 3 \cdot 2^3 + \cdots + n \cdot 2^n = (n - 1)2^{n + 1} + 2$$
for every $$n \ge 1$$.

:::solution

Check: for $$n = 3$$, $$2 + 8 + 24 = 34$$ and $$2 \cdot 16 + 2 = 34$$.

**Base case.** $$n = 1$$: $$2 = 0 + 2$$.

**Inductive step.** Suppose the formula holds for $$k$$. Adding the next term:

```math
\begin{aligned}
(k - 1)2^{k + 1} + 2 + (k + 1)2^{k + 1} &= 2k \cdot 2^{k + 1} + 2 && \text{collect the powers} \\
&= k \cdot 2^{k + 2} + 2
\end{aligned}
```

which is the formula for $$k + 1$$, since $$((k + 1) - 1)2^{(k + 1) + 1} = k \cdot 2^{k + 2}$$. By
:ref{#basic-proofs/induction}, it holds for every $$n \ge 1$$.

:::

## Areas, volumes and endless sums

::::exercise{#ex-induction-and-sums-ch-band}

Find the area of the region under $$y = x^2$$ between $$x = 1$$ and $$x = 2$$.

:::solution

The region under the curve from $$0$$ to $$2$$ is made of the region from $$0$$ to $$1$$ and the
region from $$1$$ to $$2$$, which don't overlap except along the line $$x = 1$$. So, by
:ref{#areas-and-volumes-parabola-to-b} and the rule that areas of pieces add
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/area-rules}),

```math
\text{area} = \frac{2^3}{3} - \frac{1^3}{3} = \frac{7}{3}
```

Check: the region lies between the rectangles of height $$1$$ and $$4$$ on a base of width $$1$$,
and $$1 < \frac73 \approx 2.33 < 4$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-induction-and-sums-ch-skin}

An orange is a ball of radius $$4$$ cm, and its peel is the outer $$0.5$$ cm. Find the fraction of
the orange's volume that is peel, and the fraction of a ball of radius $$10$$ that lies within $$1$$
of its surface.

:::solution

By :ref{#ball-volume}, the whole orange has volume $$\frac43\pi \cdot 4^3$$ and the part inside the
peel, a ball of radius $$3.5$$, has volume $$\frac43\pi \cdot 3.5^3$$. The $$\frac43\pi$$ cancels in
the fraction:

```math
\frac{4^3 - 3.5^3}{4^3} = \frac{64 - 42.875}{64} = \frac{21.125}{64} \approx 0.33
```

So about a third of the orange is peel, even though the peel is only an eighth of the radius. For
the ball of radius $$10$$, the fraction is $$\frac{1000 - 729}{1000} = 0.271$$: more than a quarter
of a ball lies within a tenth of its radius from the surface.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-induction-and-sums-ch-snowflake}

Start with an equilateral triangle of area $$A$$ and sides of length $$s$$. At each step, stick a
new equilateral triangle on the middle third of every edge, pointing outwards, with side a third of
that edge. After step $$n$$ the shape has $$3 \cdot 4^n$$ edges, each of length $$\frac{s}{3^n}$$.

1. Show that the perimeter grows past every number.
2. Show that the area settles towards $$\frac{8}{5}A$$.

:::solution

1. The perimeter after step $$n$$ is $$3 \cdot 4^n \cdot \frac{s}{3^n} = 3s\left(\frac43\right)^n$$.
   By :ref{#bernoulli} with $$h = \frac13$$, $$\left(\frac43\right)^n \ge 1 + \frac{n}{3}$$, so the
   perimeter is at least $$3s\left(1 + \frac{n}{3}\right) = 3s + ns$$, which passes any number once
   $$n$$ is large enough.
2. A triangle with side a third as long has $$\frac19$$ of the area
   (:ref{#area-and-scaling/area-scaling}). Step $$k$$ adds one triangle on each of the
   $$3 \cdot 4^{k - 1}$$ edges there were before it, each of area $$\frac{A}{9^k}$$. So after step
   $$n$$ the area is

   ```math
   A + \sum_{k=1}^{n} 3 \cdot 4^{k - 1} \cdot \frac{A}{9^k} = A + \frac{A}{3}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\frac49\right)^{k - 1}
   ```

   The sum is a geometric sum with $$c = \frac49$$, which settles towards
   $$\frac{1}{1 - 4/9} = \frac95$$ (:ref{#geometric-series}). So the area settles towards
   $$A + \frac{A}{3} \cdot \frac95 = \frac85 A$$. By :ref{#geometric-sum}, the gap after step $$n$$
   is exactly $$\frac{3A}{5}\left(\frac49\right)^n$$. So the shape keeps a finite area while its
   edge grows past every length.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-induction-and-sums-ch-halves}

1. Show that $$\frac{1}{n + 1} + \frac{1}{n + 2} + \cdots + \frac{1}{2n} \ge \frac12$$ for every
   $$n \ge 1$$.
2. Use part 1 and the definition of settling (:ref{#settles-towards}) to give a second proof that
   the harmonic series doesn't settle, without grouping it into blocks.

:::solution

1. There are $$n$$ terms, and each is at least the smallest, $$\frac{1}{2n}$$. So the sum is at
   least $$n \cdot \frac{1}{2n} = \frac12$$.
2. Write $$H_n = 1 + \frac12 + \cdots + \frac1n$$, so part 1 says $$H_{2n} - H_n \ge \frac12$$.
   Suppose the partial sums settled towards some $$L$$. With the margin $$\frac14$$, there is an
   $$N$$ with $$|H_n - L| < \frac14$$ for every $$n \ge N$$. Both $$N$$ and $$2N$$ are at least
   $$N$$, so by :ref{#real-numbers/distance-triangle},

   ```math
   |H_{2N} - H_N| \le |H_{2N} - L| + |L - H_N| < \tfrac14 + \tfrac14 = \tfrac12
   ```

   But part 1 with $$n = N$$ says $$H_{2N} - H_N \ge \frac12$$. These can't both hold, so the
   harmonic series doesn't settle, as :ref{#harmonic-unbounded} showed by grouping.

:::
