Pick a whole number, square it, then add the number you started with. Starting from $$3$$ gives
$$12$$, from $$4$$ gives $$20$$ and from $$7$$ gives $$56$$, and all three are even. You could keep
checking for an hour and still only know about the numbers you tried. A **proof** settles it for
every number at once, by showing why the answer has to be even.

Proofs can look intimidating at first, but almost all of them follow a handful of patterns. Once you
can spot the pattern, you know what each step is aiming for. In this guide we'll meet the four
patterns used most often:

- arguing directly
- disproving with a single example
- arguing by contradiction
- splitting into cases

The next two guides cover ["if, then" statements](./implications.mdx) and
[induction](./induction.mdx).

The examples use even and odd numbers and remainders. These are defined in the
[Numbers](../numbers/divisibility.mdx) chapter, but you only need the familiar meanings: an even
number is $$2$$ times a whole number, and an odd number is one more than an even number.

## What a proof is

Think of a proof as an explanation good enough to convince a careful sceptic. Saying "I tried
$$2 + 4$$, $$6 + 8$$ and $$10 + 12$$, and they all came out even" won't do, because the sceptic can
always ask about the next pair. What convinces them is a reason that works for every pair at once. A
proof is that kind of reason, written so that each step can be checked.

A **proof** is a chain of steps that starts from things you already accept and ends at the statement
you want. Every step has to be justified by one of three things:

- a **definition**, which says exactly what a word means
- a **basic rule** taken as given, such as $$a + b = b + a$$
- a **result proved earlier**

Nothing else is allowed: not "it looks true", not "it works for the examples I tried". In return, a
proof settles a question **for every case at once**, including the infinitely many cases nobody will
ever check by hand. That is what makes it so much stronger than checking examples.

In these notes a proof sits under the statement it proves, in a box marked _Proof_, and ends with
the symbol ∎ to show that the argument is finished. Other books and lecture notes mark the end of a
proof in different ways, so you may meet any of these:

- **∎ or □**: a filled or hollow square, called a tombstone or Halmos box after the mathematician
  who made it popular
- **Q.E.D.**: short for the Latin _quod erat demonstrandum_, "which was to be shown"
- **A closing sentence**: words such as "as required" or "this completes the proof"

They all mean the same thing: the argument is complete.

### Letters stand for every number at once

Statements in mathematics usually talk about letters, as in "for any integers $$a$$ and $$b$$,
$$a + b = b + a$$". The letter doesn't stand for one particular number that has been forgotten: it
stands for any integer at all. A proof about $$a$$ and $$b$$ may only use facts that are true of
**every** integer, and that is exactly why its conclusion holds for every integer.

The words around a letter say whether it means every number or only some: "for every" or "there is"
(:ref{#quantifiers}). Definitions often hide a "there is". For example, "$$n$$ is even" means
$$n = 2k$$ for **some** integer $$k$$ (:ref{#numbers/even-odd}). Spotting these hidden phrases is
the first step in most proofs.

### Saying the opposite

Several proof methods below start by supposing that a statement is **false**, so they rely on saying
"not" correctly. The rules are on the Statements page (:ref{#statement-negation}): to deny that all
swans are white, you only need one swan that isn't; and the opposite of "both are even" is "at least
one is odd", not "both are odd".

## Direct proofs

The most common pattern starts from what you are told and works forward, step by step, to what you
want. A reliable way to find a direct proof is:

1. **Write down what you are given**, and unpack every definition in it into a formula.
2. **Write down what you want**, also unpacked into a formula. This is your target.
3. **Bridge the gap**: rearrange what you have until it matches the target.

Step 2 is the one people skip, and it is often the most useful. Once you can see the exact form the
answer must take, the algebra usually tells you what to do.

:::example[A sum of even numbers]

**Claim.** If $$a$$ and $$b$$ are even, then $$a + b$$ is even.

**Given.** $$a$$ and $$b$$ are even, so by :ref{#numbers/even-odd} there are integers $$k$$ and
$$l$$ with $$a = 2k$$ and $$b = 2l$$. (The proof uses two different letters, because $$a$$ and $$b$$
needn't be equal.)

**Want.** $$a + b = 2 \times (\text{some integer})$$.

**Bridge.** Substitute and take out the common factor:

```math
a + b = 2k + 2l = 2(k + l)
```

Since $$k + l$$ is an integer, $$a + b$$ is $$2$$ times an integer, so it is even.

:::

:::example[Multiples of 6 are multiples of 3]

**Claim.** If $$n$$ is a multiple of $$6$$, then $$n$$ is a multiple of $$3$$.

**Given.** $$n = 6k$$ for some integer $$k$$.

**Want.** $$n = 3 \times (\text{some integer})$$.

**Bridge.** $$n = 6k = 3(2k)$$, and $$2k$$ is an integer.

:::

It is fine, and often wise, to work **backwards** on scrap paper, starting from the target and
asking "what would I need for this to be true?". But the written proof should run forwards, from
what is given to what is wanted, so that every step follows from the ones before it.

## Examples can't prove, but one example can disprove

Checking examples is an excellent way to guess what is true. It can never prove a "for every"
statement, though, because however many cases you check, there are always infinitely many left. On
the other hand, a single case where a statement fails is enough to show that it is false.

:::definition[Counterexample]{#counterexample}

A **counterexample** to a statement about every number is one particular number for which the
statement is false.

:::

This is the rule for opposites (:ref{#statement-negation}) at work: "not every number has property
$$P$$" means "some number fails to have it", and a counterexample is that number.

:::example[Patterns can break]

Take the claim that $$n^2 + n + 11$$ is never a multiple of $$11$$, for positive integers $$n$$.
Checking the first few values gives

```math
13,\ 17,\ 23,\ 31,\ 41,\ 53,\ 67,\ 83,\ 101
```

for $$n = 1, \dots, 9$$, and none of them is a multiple of $$11$$. But $$n = 10$$ gives
$$100 + 10 + 11 = 121 = 11 \cdot 11$$. Nine cases in a row looked convincing, and the tenth is a
counterexample.

:::

Counterexamples also catch tempting mistakes, such as treating $$(a + b)^2$$ as $$a^2 + b^2$$.
Taking $$a = b = 1$$ gives $$4$$ for the first and $$2$$ for the second, so the two are different.

When hunting for a counterexample, try the awkward cases first: $$0$$, $$1$$, negative numbers, two
equal numbers, and small numbers you can work out in your head. A false statement usually fails at
one of these.

## Proof by contradiction

People argue this way all the time. "If it had rained in the night, the pavement would be wet. The
pavement is dry, so it didn't rain." You take the idea you want to rule out, follow it to its
consequences, and find one that can't be right. Then the idea itself has to go.

In a proof, the idea you want to rule out is that the statement is false. So to prove that something
is true, you suppose that it is **false**, and show that this leads to something impossible.

:::remark[Proof by contradiction]{#contradiction}

To prove a statement $$S$$:

1. Suppose $$S$$ is false.
2. Using that supposition, together with definitions, rules and earlier results, reach something
   impossible: a statement that contradicts itself, or contradicts something already known.
3. Conclude that the supposition was wrong, so $$S$$ is true.

:::

This works because correct steps never lead from true statements to a false one. So if correct steps
have led to something impossible, one of the starting points must have been false. The only starting
point in doubt was the supposition that $$S$$ is false.

:::example[There is no largest integer]

**Claim.** There is no largest integer.

**Suppose not:** suppose there is a largest integer, and call it $$N$$. Then $$N + 1$$ is also an
integer, and $$N + 1 > N$$. So $$N$$ is not the largest after all, which contradicts the choice of
$$N$$. So there is no largest integer.

:::

:::example[An equation with no solutions]

**Claim.** There are no integers $$m$$ and $$n$$ with $$2m + 4n = 7$$.

**Suppose not:** suppose $$2m + 4n = 7$$ for some integers $$m$$ and $$n$$. The left side is
$$2(m + 2n)$$, which is even. The right side, $$7 = 2 \cdot 3 + 1$$, is odd. So $$7$$ would be both
even and odd, which is impossible (:ref{#numbers/even-or-odd}). So there are no such integers.

:::

Proof by contradiction is especially useful for statements that say something **doesn't** exist,
like these two, because "it does exist" gives you something concrete to work with. The most famous
example, in the [Numbers](../numbers/irrational-numbers.mdx) chapter, shows that no fraction squares
to $$2$$. It supposes that one does, writes it in lowest form, and shows that its top and bottom
would then both have to be even, which lowest form rules out.

A related method, proving the contrapositive, is explained on the [next page](./implications.mdx).
The two are so close that the same argument can often be written either way. Take the claim "if
$$n^2$$ is even, then $$n$$ is even".

- **By contradiction**: suppose $$n^2$$ is even but $$n$$ is odd. An odd number squared is odd, so
  $$n^2$$ is odd. That clashes with $$n^2$$ being even, so $$n$$ must be even.
- **By the contrapositive**: suppose $$n$$ is odd. An odd number squared is odd, so $$n^2$$ is odd.
  So whenever $$n^2$$ is even, $$n$$ can't be odd.

The key step, that an odd number squared is odd, is the same in both. The second version is shorter,
because it doesn't need to set up a clash at the end.

## Splitting into cases

When every number falls into one of a few kinds, you can prove a statement by checking each kind
separately. The important thing is that the cases must **cover every possibility**, or the proof has
a hole.

:::example[Two cases]

**Claim.** For every integer $$n$$, the number $$n^2 + n$$ is even.

Every integer is either even or odd (:ref{#numbers/even-or-odd}), so two cases cover everything.

**Case 1: $$n$$ is even.** Write $$n = 2k$$. Then $$n^2 + n = 4k^2 + 2k = 2(2k^2 + k)$$, which is
even.

**Case 2: $$n$$ is odd.** Write $$n = 2k + 1$$. Then

```math
n^2 + n = (4k^2 + 4k + 1) + (2k + 1) = 4k^2 + 6k + 2 = 2(2k^2 + 3k + 1)
```

which is even too.

:::

There is a neater proof here as well: $$n^2 + n = n(n + 1)$$ is a product of two consecutive
integers, one of which is even. Looking for a slicker argument after finding one that works is a
good habit, but a proof by cases that works is a perfectly good proof.

Cases can be split more than two ways. Dividing by $$3$$ leaves remainder $$0$$, $$1$$ or $$2$$
(:ref{#numbers/division-remainder}), so every integer can be written as $$3k$$, $$3k + 1$$ or
$$3k + 2$$.

:::example[Three cases]

**Claim.** For every integer $$n$$, the number $$n(n + 1)(2n + 1)$$ is a multiple of $$3$$.

Each case finds one factor that is a multiple of $$3$$, and that makes the whole product a multiple
of $$3$$.

- **$$n = 3k$$:** the factor $$n$$ is a multiple of $$3$$.
- **$$n = 3k + 1$$:** the factor $$2n + 1 = 6k + 3 = 3(2k + 1)$$ is a multiple of $$3$$.
- **$$n = 3k + 2$$:** the factor $$n + 1 = 3k + 3 = 3(k + 1)$$ is a multiple of $$3$$.

:::

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on proofs, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-proofs-even-times-any}

Prove that if $$a$$ is an even integer and $$b$$ is any integer, then $$ab$$ is even.

:::solution

**Given.** $$a$$ is even, so $$a = 2k$$ for some integer $$k$$ (:ref{#numbers/even-odd}). Nothing is
known about $$b$$ except that it is an integer.

**Want.** $$ab = 2 \times (\text{some integer})$$.

**Bridge.** $$ab = 2k \cdot b = 2(kb)$$, and $$kb$$ is an integer, so $$ab$$ is even.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-proofs-three-consecutive}

Prove that the sum of any three consecutive integers is a multiple of $$3$$.

:::solution

Three consecutive integers can be written $$n$$, $$n + 1$$ and $$n + 2$$, where $$n$$ is the
smallest. Their sum is

```math
n + (n + 1) + (n + 2) = 3n + 3 = 3(n + 1),
```

and $$n + 1$$ is an integer, so the sum is a multiple of $$3$$.

Choosing one letter for all three numbers is the key step. Writing them as $$a$$, $$b$$ and $$c$$
would lose the fact that they are consecutive.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-proofs-not-a-proof}

Here is an argument. "**Claim:** for every positive integer $$n$$, the number $$n^2 - n + 41$$ is
prime. **Proof:** for $$n = 1, 2, 3$$ it gives $$41$$, $$43$$ and $$47$$, which are all prime." Why
is this not a proof, and is the claim true?

:::solution

Checking cases can't prove a "for every" statement, however many cases you check, because infinitely
many are left. The claim is in fact false. Take $$n = 41$$:

```math
41^2 - 41 + 41 = 41^2 = 1681,
```

which is $$41 \cdot 41$$ and so not prime. This is a counterexample (:ref{#counterexample}). The
formula gives a prime for every $$n$$ from $$1$$ to $$40$$, which is why the pattern is so
convincing.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-proofs-counterexample-hunt}

Each statement is false. Find a counterexample to each.

1. For all positive numbers $$a$$ and $$b$$, $$\frac{1}{a + b} = \frac{1}{a} + \frac{1}{b}$$.
2. For every positive integer $$n$$, $$n^2 + 1$$ is odd.
3. For every integer $$n$$, $$2n > n$$.
4. Every positive integer whose last digit is $$3$$ is prime.

:::solution

One case where the statement fails is enough (:ref{#counterexample}).

1. $$a = b = 1$$: the left side is $$\frac{1}{2}$$ and the right side is $$2$$.
2. $$n = 1$$: $$1^2 + 1 = 2$$, which is even. (Every odd $$n$$ is a counterexample.)
3. $$n = 0$$: $$0$$ is not bigger than $$0$$. (Every negative $$n$$ fails too.)
4. $$33 = 3 \cdot 11$$, which is not prime.

Each was found by trying the awkward cases first: equal numbers, $$1$$, $$0$$ and negative numbers.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-proofs-no-solution}

Prove by contradiction that there are no integers $$m$$ and $$n$$ with $$6m + 9n = 100$$.

:::solution

**Suppose not:** suppose $$6m + 9n = 100$$ for some integers $$m$$ and $$n$$. The left side is
$$3(2m + 3n)$$, a multiple of $$3$$, so it leaves remainder $$0$$ when divided by $$3$$. But
$$100 = 3 \cdot 33 + 1$$ leaves remainder $$1$$. A number leaves only one remainder
(:ref{#numbers/division-remainder}), so this is impossible (:ref{#contradiction}). So there are no
such integers.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-proofs-odd-square-eight}

Prove that the square of every odd integer is one more than a multiple of $$8$$. You may use the
fact, proved in the "Two cases" example under [Splitting into cases](#splitting-into-cases), that
$$k^2 + k$$ is even for every integer $$k$$.

:::solution

Let $$n$$ be odd, so $$n = 2k + 1$$ for some integer $$k$$. Then

```math
n^2 = 4k^2 + 4k + 1 = 4(k^2 + k) + 1.
```

Now $$k^2 + k$$ is even, so $$k^2 + k = 2m$$ for some integer $$m$$. So
$$n^2 = 4 \cdot 2m + 1 = 8m + 1$$, which is one more than a multiple of $$8$$.

For example, $$3^2 = 9 = 8 + 1$$, $$5^2 = 25 = 24 + 1$$ and $$7^2 = 49 = 48 + 1$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-proofs-missing-case-true}

Here is an argument. "**Claim:** for every integer $$n$$, $$n(n + 1) \ge 0$$. **Proof:** if
$$n \ge 0$$, both factors are at least $$0$$, so the product is at least $$0$$. If $$n \le -2$$,
both factors are negative, so the product is positive." Is the proof complete? Is the claim true?

:::solution

The proof is not complete: the cases miss $$n = -1$$, and a proof by cases has to cover every
possibility. Here the claim happens to hold in the missing case, since $$(-1) \cdot 0 = 0 \ge 0$$.
Adding that third case finishes the proof, and the claim is true.

A missing case doesn't always hide a false claim, but you can't know until you check it. Until then
the argument is not a proof.

:::
