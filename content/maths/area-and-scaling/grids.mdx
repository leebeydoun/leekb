---
title: Measuring area with a grid
description:
  Measuring curved shapes by counting squares, and why a finer grid closes in on the area.
---

Suppose you're buying a liner for a kidney-shaped garden pond. Its edge is curved, so the area rules
for rectangles and triangles don't apply. What you'd probably do is draw the pond on graph paper and
count the squares. In this guide we'll turn that counting into an exact method, and see why a finer
grid always gets closer to the true area.

## Counting squares

Lay a grid of equal squares over a shape. Some squares sit wholly inside the shape, and their total
area is too small: it misses the bits of the shape near the edge. Other squares are only partly
covered, because the edge runs through them. Adding those in gives a total that is too big, since it
includes bits outside the shape. The true area lies somewhere in between.

Two facts about area make this work. The first is the plain one that a piece of a shape can't have
more area than the whole. The second, from the [Isometries](../isometries/isometries.mdx) chapter,
is that moving a shape doesn't change its area. Together with :ref{#distance-and-angles/area-rules},
which gives the area of a rectangle and says that areas of pieces add, these are the rules this
chapter uses.

:::remark[Rules for comparing areas]{#area-compare}

1. **Inside means no more**: if a region $$S$$ lies inside a region $$T$$, then the area of $$S$$ is
   at most the area of $$T$$
2. **Moving keeps area**: an isometry sends a region to a region with the same area
   (:ref{#isometries/isometry-area})

These are taken as given.

::see-also{src="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Area#Formal_definition"}

:::

Now the two counts can be named. In what follows, a grid square **overlaps** a shape when the two
have some area in common. A square that meets the shape only along part of its edge, or at a single
corner, doesn't count, since a line or a point covers no area.

:::definition[Inner and outer counts]{#grid-counts}

Lay a grid of squares with sides of length $$c$$ over a region $$S$$. Each square has area $$c^2$$.

- The **inner count** is the total area of the squares that lie wholly inside $$S$$.
- The **outer count** is the total area of the squares that overlap $$S$$, whether wholly inside it
  or only partly.

:::

:::proposition[The area lies between the counts]{#grid-squeeze}

For any grid and any region $$S$$,

```math
\text{inner count} \le \text{area of } S \le \text{outer count}
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan_measure"}

**The inner count.** The squares wholly inside $$S$$ meet each other only along edges, which cover
no area, so they don't overlap. By :ref{#distance-and-angles/area-rules}, the region they make
together has area equal to the inner count. That region lies inside $$S$$, so by rule 1 of
:ref{#area-compare} its area is at most the area of $$S$$.

**The outer count.** Every point of $$S$$ lies in one of the squares that $$S$$ overlaps. This is
taken from the picture: a region has no hairs or spikes so thin that they cover no area. So $$S$$
lies inside the region made by the overlapping squares. Those squares don't overlap each other, so
that region has area equal to the outer count, and rule 1 of :ref{#area-compare} gives the second
inequality.

:::

The best way to trust the method is to try it on a shape whose area you already know. The right
triangle below has legs $$6$$ and $$4$$, so its area is $$\frac{1}{2} \cdot 6 \cdot 4 = 12$$
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/triangle-area}). Each picture uses squares half the size of the one
before. The blue squares are wholly inside, and the orange ones are only partly covered.

<GridTriangle caption="The same triangle, area 12, on three grids. The blue squares give the inner count; blue and orange together give the outer count. The area 12 is always between them." />

:::example[Closing in on 12]

Counting the squares in each picture:

| Side of each square | Inner count | Outer count | Gap   |
| ------------------- | ----------- | ----------- | ----- |
| $$1$$               | $$8$$       | $$16$$      | $$8$$ |
| $$\frac{1}{2}$$     | $$10$$      | $$14$$      | $$4$$ |
| $$\frac{1}{4}$$     | $$11$$      | $$13$$      | $$2$$ |
| $$\frac{1}{8}$$     | $$11.5$$    | $$12.5$$    | $$1$$ |

Every row has $$12$$ between the two counts, as :ref{#grid-squeeze} promises. Better still, each
time the squares get half as wide the gap halves. With squares of side $$\frac{1}{8}$$, the counts
already pin the area down to within $$1$$, and smaller squares would do even better.

:::

## Why the gap shrinks

The gap between the counts is the total area of the orange squares: the ones the edge passes
through. So the question is how much area those squares can take up. Every orange square touches the
edge, and a small square touching the edge can't reach far from it. So all the orange squares crowd
into a thin band that hugs the edge, and as the squares shrink, so does the band.

Try it in the diagram below, with a lake. Pick smaller squares and watch the orange ring get
thinner, and the gap between the counts shrink. Then show the band: every orange square sits inside
it.

<GridCount />

:::proposition[The edge band]{#edge-band}

Use a grid of squares of side $$c$$. Every square that overlaps a region $$S$$ but is not wholly
inside it lies within distance $$c\sqrt{2}$$ of the edge of $$S$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan_measure"}

Take such a square. It overlaps $$S$$, so it contains a point $$A$$ inside $$S$$. It is not wholly
inside $$S$$, so it contains a point $$B$$ outside $$S$$. The segment $$AB$$ lies in the square, and
on the way from inside $$S$$ to outside it crosses the edge of $$S$$ at some point $$E$$. That
crossing is taken from the picture.

The longest distance between two points of a square is its diagonal. By
:ref{#distance-and-angles/pythagoras} the diagonal has length $$\sqrt{c^2 + c^2} = c\sqrt{2}$$. So
every point of the square is within $$c\sqrt{2}$$ of the point $$E$$, which lies on the edge.

:::

Now picture the band of all points within $$c\sqrt{2}$$ of the edge, on either side. It is like a
ribbon of width $$2c\sqrt{2}$$ laid along the edge. If the edge has length $$\ell$$ (as
[Zooming and length](./scaling-lengths.mdx#the-length-of-a-curve) defines it), the ribbon has area
about $$2c\sqrt{2} \cdot \ell$$, exactly as a long thin rectangle would. That estimate is taken from
the picture. The orange squares don't overlap each other and all lie in the band, so their total
area, the gap between the counts, is at most about

```math
2\sqrt{2} \cdot c \cdot \ell
```

The edge length $$\ell$$ stays the same, while $$c$$ can be made as small as you like. Halve the
squares and the bound halves; make them ten times smaller and it is ten times smaller.

:::example[A lake on a map]

A lake has a shoreline $$10$$ km long. On a grid of squares $$100$$ m wide, which is $$0.1$$ km, the
gap between the counts is at most about

```math
2\sqrt{2} \cdot 0.1 \cdot 10 \approx 2.83 \text{ km}^2
```

With squares $$10$$ m wide the bound is ten times smaller, about $$0.28$$ km$$^2$$. The bound can be
generous. In the triangle example, only the slanted side made orange squares, and the actual gaps
were well under the bound.

:::

## Grids pin down area

Put the two results together. The area of a region is always caught between the two counts, and for
a region whose edge has a length, the gap between the counts can be made as small as you like by
using small enough squares. So the counts close in on one number, and that number is the area.

:::remark[Grids pin down area]{#grid-rule}

Let $$S$$ be a region whose edge is a curve with a length. As the grid squares get smaller, the
inner and outer counts get as close as you like to each other, and so to the area of $$S$$. In
particular, two regions whose inner and outer counts agree for every grid have the same area.

This is taken as given. Calculus makes it precise.

::see-also{src="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan_measure"}

:::

The condition on the edge rules out strange regions whose edge wiggles so wildly that it has no
length, like an idealised coastline that shows new bays and headlands at every scale. Every shape in
this chapter, from rectangles to discs to ellipses, has an edge with a length, so the rule applies.

The grid rule is the tool the rest of the chapter uses. To see how a zoom changes area
([Zooming and area](./scaling-areas.mdx)), or how a stretch does
([Stretching one way](./stretching.mdx)), you only need to see what it does to the little squares.
And it is how the area of a disc, $$\pi r^2$$, is pinned down in
[The area of a disc](./disc-area.mdx).

:::example[A leaf on graph paper]

You trace a leaf onto paper ruled in $$1$$ cm squares. It covers $$37$$ squares completely and
$$18$$ more partly. So its area is between $$37$$ and $$37 + 18 = 55$$ cm$$^2$$.

That range is wide, and a practical rule narrows it: count each partly covered square as half a
square. The edge cuts through those squares, so on average about half of each is inside. That gives
$$37 + \frac{18}{2} = 46$$ cm$$^2$$. The rule is an estimate, not a proof. On the triangle with
squares of side $$1$$, it gives $$8 + \frac{8}{2} = 12$$, the exact area. For a sure answer, use
finer paper.

:::

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on measuring area with a grid, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-grids-rectangle}

A rectangle $$2.5$$ wide and $$1.5$$ tall has one corner at a corner of the grid, with its sides
along grid lines. Find the inner and outer counts for squares of side $$1$$, and then for squares of
side $$\frac{1}{2}$$. What do you notice?

:::solution

**Side 1.** Along the width, the squares from $$0$$ to $$1$$ and $$1$$ to $$2$$ fit inside, and the
one from $$2$$ to $$3$$ sticks out. Up the height, the square from $$0$$ to $$1$$ fits and the one
from $$1$$ to $$2$$ sticks out. So $$2 \cdot 1 = 2$$ squares lie wholly inside, and
$$3 \cdot 2 = 6$$ overlap the rectangle. The counts are $$2$$ and $$6$$, and the area
$$2.5 \cdot 1.5 = 3.75$$ (:ref{#distance-and-angles/area-rules}) lies between them.

**Side ½.** Now $$2.5$$ is $$5$$ half-squares and $$1.5$$ is $$3$$, so $$5 \cdot 3 = 15$$ squares
fill the rectangle exactly and none sticks out. Both counts are $$15 \cdot \frac{1}{4} = 3.75$$.

When every edge of the shape lies along grid lines, no square is only partly covered, so the counts
agree and give the area exactly.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-grids-diagonal}

Show that two points of a square of side $$c$$ that are at opposite corners are $$c\sqrt{2}$$ apart.
How far apart are opposite corners of a square of side $$\frac{1}{2}$$? Of side $$0.1$$?

:::solution

Two neighbouring sides of the square meet at a right angle (:ref{#distance-and-angles/rectangle}),
so the diagonal is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs $$c$$ and $$c$$. By
:ref{#distance-and-angles/pythagoras}, its length $$d$$ has $$d^2 = c^2 + c^2 = 2c^2$$, so
$$d = c\sqrt{2}$$.

For side $$\frac{1}{2}$$, $$d = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \approx 0.707$$. For side $$0.1$$,
$$d = 0.1\sqrt{2} \approx 0.141$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-grids-orange-squares}

For the triangle with legs $$6$$ and $$4$$, squares of side $$\frac{1}{8}$$ give an inner count of
$$11.5$$ and an outer count of $$12.5$$.

1. How many squares are only partly covered?
2. Only the slanted side passes through squares; the other two sides lie along grid lines. That side
   has length $$\sqrt{52} \approx 7.21$$. What does the band bound $$2\sqrt{2} \cdot c \cdot \ell$$
   give, using that side for $$\ell$$? Is the actual gap within it?

:::solution

1. The gap is $$12.5 - 11.5 = 1$$, and each square has area $$\frac{1}{64}$$. So there are
   $$1 \div \frac{1}{64} = 64$$ partly covered squares.
2. $$2\sqrt{2} \cdot \frac{1}{8} \cdot 7.21 \approx 2.55$$. The actual gap, $$1$$, is well within
   the bound, which is only ever an upper limit (:ref{#edge-band}).

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-grids-shifted}

Slide the grid under the same triangle by half a square, so that no side of the triangle lies along
a grid line. With squares of side $$1$$, the counts become $$4$$ and $$23$$, much further apart than
$$8$$ and $$16$$. Explain why, and check that the gap is within the band bound for the whole
perimeter.

:::solution

Before, the two legs lay along grid lines, so no square was cut by them: only the slanted side made
partly covered squares. After the shift, each leg runs through the middle of a row or column of
squares, so those squares are partly covered too. More of the edge cuts through squares, so the gap
grows from $$8$$ to $$23 - 4 = 19$$.

The perimeter is $$6 + 4 + \sqrt{52} \approx 17.21$$. The band bound (:ref{#edge-band}) gives
$$2\sqrt{2} \cdot 1 \cdot 17.21 \approx 48.7$$, and $$19$$ is within it. Moving a region or a grid
never changes the area itself (:ref{#area-compare}); it only changes how well a coarse grid measures
it.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-grids-pond}

A pond has a shoreline $$40$$ m long.

1. Using squares $$0.5$$ m wide, about how large could the gap between the counts be?
2. How small should the squares be to make sure the gap is at most about $$5$$ m$$^2$$?

:::solution

1. The band bound is $$2\sqrt{2} \cdot 0.5 \cdot 40 \approx 56.6$$ m$$^2$$.
2. You need $$2\sqrt{2} \cdot c \cdot 40 \le 5$$, so $$c \le \frac{5}{80\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.044$$ m.
   Squares about $$4.4$$ cm wide, or smaller, will do.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-grids-why-inside}

Without using :ref{#grid-squeeze}, explain why the inner count is never bigger than the outer count
for the same grid.

:::solution

A square wholly inside $$S$$ has all of its area covered by $$S$$, so it overlaps $$S$$
(:ref{#grid-counts}). So every square counted in the inner count is also counted in the outer count,
which may count some partly covered squares as well. Adding up the same squares and possibly more
can't give a smaller total.

:::
