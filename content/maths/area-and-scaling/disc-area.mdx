---
title: The area of a disc
description:
  Where the formula pi r squared comes from, how big pi is, and why sectors keep their share.
---

A garden sprinkler wets a disc of lawn. A goat tied to a stake can graze a disc of grass. A pizza is
a disc of dough. The [Distance and angles](../distance-and-angles/introduction.mdx) chapter used the
formula $$\pi r^2$$ for the area of a disc, and took it as given
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/disc-area}). This guide explains where it comes from.

The whole explanation rests on one observation: every disc is a zoom of the disc of radius $$1$$.
Once you know the area of that one disc, [Zooming and area](./scaling-areas.mdx) gives the area of
every other. The number $$\pi$$ is then the name for that one area. The rest of this page works out
roughly how big it is, and uses it to prove the rules about discs that the earlier chapter assumed.

## One number for every disc

Picture a disc of radius $$1$$ printed on a sheet, and a photocopier set to zoom by $$3$$ about its
centre. The copy is a disc of radius $$3$$, and its area is $$3^2 = 9$$ times the area of the
original (:ref{#area-scaling}). The same works for any radius $$r$$: the zoom by $$r$$ turns the
disc of radius $$1$$ into the disc of radius $$r$$, with $$r^2$$ times the area.

So every disc's area is $$r^2$$ times one fixed number: the area of the disc of radius $$1$$. That
number turns up so often that it has its own name.

:::definition[The number pi]{#pi}

The number $$\pi$$ (the Greek letter pi) is the area of a disc of radius $$1$$.

:::

The disc of radius $$1$$ is called the **unit disc**. The number $$\pi$$ doesn't depend on which
unit disc you take. A slide that takes one centre to the other carries one disc of radius $$1$$ onto
the other, as in the proof of :ref{#isometries/circles-congruent}, and isometries keep area
(:ref{#isometries/isometry-area}).

You may have met $$\pi$$ as the circumference of a circle divided by its diameter. That is a
different question, about a length rather than an area, and it is a surprise that the answer is the
same number. [Around the circle](./circumference.mdx) shows why.

## How big is pi?

The definition says what $$\pi$$ is, but not how big it is. Two squares give a first answer. A
square of side $$2$$ fits around the unit disc, touching it at four points, and a smaller square
fits inside it with its corners on the circle. The disc is bigger than the inner square and smaller
than the outer one.

```plane
point O 0 0 sw
point A 2.5 0 hidden
point B 0 2.5 hidden
point C -2.5 0 hidden
point D 0 -2.5 hidden
point E 2.5 2.5 hidden
point F -2.5 2.5 hidden
point G -2.5 -2.5 hidden
point H 2.5 -2.5 hidden
polygon E F G H color=muted
circle O 2.5 fill
polygon A B C D color=orange
segment O A label="1"
caption "The unit disc between two squares: the inner square has area 2 and the outer one area 4"
```

:::proposition[Pi is between 2 and 4]{#pi-bounds}

The number $$\pi$$ lies between $$2$$ and $$4$$:

```math
2 < \pi < 4
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pi#Polygon_approximation_era"}

Let $$O$$ be the centre of the unit disc.

**The outer square.** Take the two lines through $$O$$ that meet at right angles, and the square of
side $$2$$ centred at $$O$$ with its sides parallel to them. This square is made of the points that
are within $$1$$ of both lines, as the picture shows. A point $$P$$ of the disc has
$$d(O, P) \le 1$$. Its distance to each line is at most $$d(O, P)$$, since the shortest distance
from a point to a line is along the perpendicular (:ref{#distance-and-angles/shortest-distance}). So
$$P$$ is within $$1$$ of both lines, and lies in the square. The disc is inside a square of area
$$2 \cdot 2 = 4$$ (:ref{#distance-and-angles/area-rules}).

**The inner square.** Mark the four points where the two lines cross the circle. Joining them gives
a square made of four right triangles, each with legs of length $$1$$ meeting at $$O$$. Each
triangle has area $$\frac{1 \cdot 1}{2} = \frac{1}{2}$$
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/right-triangle-area}), so the square has area
$$4 \cdot \frac{1}{2} = 2$$. Every point of this square is within $$1$$ of $$O$$, since its farthest
points from $$O$$ are its corners, as the picture shows. So it lies inside the disc.

A shape inside another has no more area (:ref{#area-compare}), so $$2 \le \pi \le 4$$. Neither is an
equality: the disc leaves the corners of the outer square empty, and it spills over each side of the
inner square, and those pieces have area. So $$2 < \pi < 4$$.

:::

A finer grid of squares pins $$\pi$$ down much more closely. Lay a grid of squares of side $$c$$
over the unit disc, with a corner of the grid at the centre. The inner count adds up the squares
wholly inside the disc, and the outer count adds up every square that overlaps the disc in some area
(:ref{#grid-counts}). The area $$\pi$$ lies between them (:ref{#grid-squeeze}). Counting by computer
gives these results:

| Side of each square | Squares wholly inside | Inner count | Squares overlapping | Outer count | Gap       |
| ------------------- | --------------------- | ----------- | ------------------- | ----------- | --------- |
| $$0.1$$             | $$276$$               | $$2.76$$    | $$344$$             | $$3.44$$    | $$0.68$$  |
| $$0.01$$            | $$31\,016$$           | $$3.1016$$  | $$31\,796$$         | $$3.1796$$  | $$0.078$$ |
| $$0.001$$           | $$3\,137\,548$$       | $$3.1375$$  | $$3\,145\,520$$     | $$3.1456$$  | $$0.008$$ |

Each count is the number of squares times the area of one square: for example
$$276 \cdot 0.1^2 = 2.76$$. Each time the squares get ten times smaller, the gap gets about ten
times smaller too, as the edge band of :ref{#edge-band} predicts. The last row shows that
$$3.1375 < \pi < 3.1456$$, so $$\pi = 3.14$$ to two decimal places. More careful methods give

```math
\pi = 3.14159265\ldots
```

and the digits go on for ever without settling into a repeating pattern.

## The area of a disc

With $$\pi$$ named, the formula for every disc follows from zooming. The slider below changes the
radius. The orange square has the radius as its side, so its area is $$r^2$$. Four such squares make
the square around the disc, and the disc always takes up the same share of them: $$\pi$$ of the
four, a little more than three.

<DiscZoom />

:::theorem[Area of a disc]{#disc-area-formula}

A disc of radius $$r$$ has area $$\pi r^2$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Area_of_a_circle"}

Let $$O$$ be the centre, let $$D_1$$ be the disc of radius $$1$$ centred at $$O$$, and let $$D_r$$
be the disc of radius $$r$$ centred at $$O$$. The first step is to show that the zoom by $$r$$ about
$$O$$ sends $$D_1$$ exactly onto $$D_r$$.

Let $$P$$ be a point of $$D_1$$, so $$d(O, P) \le 1$$. The zoom sends it to the point $$rP$$ on the
ray $$OP$$ with $$d(O, rP) = r \cdot d(O, P) \le r$$ (:ref{#isometries/dilation}), so $$rP$$ is in
$$D_r$$. In the other direction, let $$Q$$ be a point of $$D_r$$. The zoom by $$\frac{1}{r}$$ sends
it to a point at distance $$\frac{1}{r} \cdot d(O, Q) \le 1$$ from $$O$$, which is in $$D_1$$, and
the zoom by $$r$$ sends that point back to $$Q$$ (:ref{#zoom-twice}). So the zoom of $$D_1$$ is the
whole of $$D_r$$.

By :ref{#area-scaling}, the area of $$D_r$$ is $$r^2$$ times the area of $$D_1$$. The area of
$$D_1$$ is $$\pi$$ (:ref{#pi}), so the area of $$D_r$$ is $$\pi r^2$$.

:::

This proves rule 1 of :ref{#distance-and-angles/disc-area}, which that chapter took as given. All it
needed was the definition of $$\pi$$ and the fact that zooming multiplies area by $$r^2$$. The
section on sectors below proves rule 2.

:::example[A goat on a rope]

A goat is tied to a stake with a rope $$5$$ metres long. It can reach every point within $$5$$
metres of the stake: a disc of radius $$5$$, with area $$25\pi \approx 78.5$$ square metres.

On a $$10$$ metre rope, it can reach a disc of radius $$10$$, with area $$100\pi \approx 314.2$$
square metres. Doubling the rope gives the goat four times the grass, since
$$100\pi = 4 \cdot 25\pi$$.

:::

<GoatTether caption="Drawn to the same scale: twice the rope reaches a disc with twice the radius and four times the area." />

Now the pizza question from [Zooming and area](./scaling-areas.mdx) can be answered in square
inches. A 12-inch pizza has radius $$6$$ inches and area $$36\pi \approx 113.1$$ square inches. Two
8-inch pizzas have radius $$4$$ inches each and together cover
$$2 \cdot 16\pi = 32\pi \approx 100.5$$ square inches. The ratio $$\frac{36\pi}{32\pi} = 1.125$$
matches the answer found there without $$\pi$$.

## Sectors keep their share

A slice of pizza cut at the centre is a sector (:ref{#distance-and-angles/sector}). Cut a large
pizza and a small pizza into eighths, and each slice is an eighth of its own pizza, even though the
large slices are bigger. Zooming explains why: a zoom about the centre moves every point straight
out along its own ray from the centre, so it turns a slice of the small pizza into a slice of the
large one with the same angle.

:::proposition[Sectors and zooming]{#sector-scaling}

Take a disc centred at $$O$$ and a sector of it with angle $$x^\circ$$. The zoom by $$r$$ about
$$O$$ sends the sector onto the sector with the same angle in the zoomed disc. Both the sector and
the disc have their areas multiplied by $$r^2$$, so the sector takes up the same fraction of each
disc.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circular_sector#Area"}

The zoom sends each point $$P \ne O$$ to a point of the ray $$OP$$ (:ref{#isometries/dilation}), so
it sends each ray from $$O$$ into itself. A point lies inside the angle exactly when its ray from
$$O$$ does, so points inside the angle go to points inside the angle, and points outside it go to
points outside it. The proof of :ref{#disc-area-formula} shows that the disc of radius $$s$$ goes
exactly onto the disc of radius $$rs$$, and the same argument works with $$s$$ in place of $$1$$. So
the points of the disc that are inside the angle, which make up the sector, go exactly onto the
points of the zoomed disc that are inside the angle.

By :ref{#area-scaling}, both areas are multiplied by $$r^2$$. Writing $$A$$ for the area of the
sector and $$D$$ for the area of the disc, the fraction after the zoom is

```math
\frac{r^2 A}{r^2 D} = \frac{A}{D}
```

which is the fraction before.

:::

This proves rule 2 of :ref{#distance-and-angles/disc-area}: the fraction of a disc that an angle's
sector takes up is the same for every disc centred at the angle's vertex. Any two such discs are
zooms of each other about the vertex, since the zoom by $$\frac{t}{s}$$ turns radius $$s$$ into
radius $$t$$. So the measure of an angle in degrees, defined by that fraction, depends on the angle
alone.

Rule 3, that a line through the centre cuts a disc into two halves of equal area, comes from a flip
rather than a zoom. The flip in that line keeps the centre where it is and keeps distances, so it
sends the disc onto itself. It swaps the two sides of the line, so it carries one half onto the
other, and isometries keep area (:ref{#isometries/isometry-area}). So all three rules about discs
that [Distance and angles](../distance-and-angles/introduction.mdx) took as given are now explained.

:::example[A sprinkler that sweeps an arc]

A garden sprinkler swings back and forth through $$120^\circ$$ and reaches $$6$$ metres. It wets a
sector of $$120^\circ$$ in a disc of radius $$6$$. That is $$\frac{120}{360} = \frac{1}{3}$$ of the
disc, so it wets $$\frac{1}{3} \cdot 36\pi = 12\pi \approx 37.7$$ square metres
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/sector-area}).

:::

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on the area of a disc, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-disc-area-rope}

A goat is tied with a rope $$4$$ metres long.

1. How much grass can it reach?
2. How long a rope would give it three times as much?

:::solution

1. A disc of radius $$4$$, with area $$16\pi \approx 50.3$$ square metres
   (:ref{#disc-area-formula}).
2. Three times the area needs a zoom by the number whose square is $$3$$ (:ref{#area-scaling}), so
   the rope must be $$4\sqrt{3} \approx 6.93$$ metres. Check: $$\pi(4\sqrt{3})^2 = 48\pi$$, which is
   $$3 \cdot 16\pi$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-disc-area-ring}

A round paddling pool has radius $$3$$ metres and is surrounded by a paved ring $$2$$ metres wide.
What is the area of the paving?

:::solution

The pool and the paving together make a disc of radius $$3 + 2 = 5$$, with area $$25\pi$$. The pool
is a disc of radius $$3$$, with area $$9\pi$$ (:ref{#disc-area-formula}). The two pieces don't
overlap, so the areas add (:ref{#distance-and-angles/area-rules}):

```math
\text{area of the paving} = 25\pi - 9\pi = 16\pi \approx 50.3 \text{ square metres}
```

The paving has more area than the pool, even though it is narrower than the pool's radius.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-disc-area-hexagon}

Six points are spaced evenly round the unit circle and joined in order, making a regular hexagon.
Joining each corner to the centre cuts it into six triangles whose sides all have length $$1$$.

1. Find the height of one triangle and its area.
2. Use the hexagon to show that $$\pi > 2.5$$.

:::solution

1. Take one triangle with base $$1$$. The opposite corner is the same distance from both ends of the
   base, so it lies on the perpendicular bisector of the base
   (:ref{#distance-and-angles/perpendicular-bisector-rule}). So the perpendicular from that corner
   meets the base at its midpoint, and cuts off a right triangle with hypotenuse $$1$$ and one leg
   $$\frac{1}{2}$$, and by :ref{#distance-and-angles/pythagoras} the height is
   $$\sqrt{1 - \frac{1}{4}} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \approx 0.866$$. The area is
   $$\frac{1}{2} \cdot 1 \cdot \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4} \approx 0.433$$
   (:ref{#distance-and-angles/triangle-area}).
2. The hexagon has area $$6 \cdot \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4} \approx 2.598$$ and lies inside the unit disc,
   so $$\pi \ge 2.598 > 2.5$$ (:ref{#area-compare}). This beats the inner square of
   :ref{#pi-bounds}, which only gave $$\pi > 2$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-disc-area-double}

A circular pond has radius $$10$$ metres. It is to be enlarged to twice the area, keeping it
circular. What is the new radius?

:::solution

Twice the area needs a zoom by $$\sqrt{2}$$ (:ref{#area-scaling}), so the new radius is
$$10\sqrt{2} \approx 14.14$$ metres. Check: $$\pi(10\sqrt{2})^2 = 200\pi$$, and the old area was
$$100\pi$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-disc-area-slices}

A 12-inch pizza is cut into $$8$$ equal slices and a 10-inch pizza into $$6$$ equal slices. Which
slice is bigger, and by how much?

:::solution

The 12-inch pizza has radius $$6$$ and area $$36\pi$$, so each of its slices has area
$$\frac{36\pi}{8} = 4.5\pi \approx 14.1$$ square inches. The 10-inch pizza has radius $$5$$ and area
$$25\pi$$, so each of its slices has $$\frac{25\pi}{6} \approx 4.17\pi \approx 13.1$$ square inches
(:ref{#disc-area-formula}). The slice of the 12-inch pizza is bigger, by about $$1$$ square inch.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-disc-area-darts}

A square dartboard has side $$1$$. A quarter of a disc of radius $$1$$ is painted on it, centred at
one corner. Darts thrown at random land anywhere on the board with equal chance, so the share that
land in the painted part is about the share of the area it covers.

1. What share of the board is painted?
2. Out of $$1000$$ darts, $$786$$ land in the painted part. What estimate of $$\pi$$ does that give?

:::solution

1. The painted part is a sector of $$90^\circ$$ in a disc of radius $$1$$, so its area is
   $$\frac{90}{360} \cdot \pi = \frac{\pi}{4}$$ (:ref{#distance-and-angles/sector-area}). The board
   has area $$1$$, so the share is $$\frac{\pi}{4} \approx 0.785$$.
2. Setting $$\frac{\pi}{4} \approx \frac{786}{1000}$$ gives $$\pi \approx 4 \cdot 0.786 = 3.144$$.
   More darts give a better estimate, in the same way that a finer grid does.

:::
