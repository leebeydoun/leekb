title: Zooming and length
description: What a zoom does to lengths, and why a zoomed shape keeps its shape.

Look closely at a model railway carriage built at $$1 : 76$$. It's $$76$$ times shorter than the
real one, and also $$76$$ times narrower and $$76$$ times lower, so it looks like the real thing in
miniature. That's what a zoom does: it multiplies every length by the same number. In this guide
we'll see why, and use it to read real distances off a map and a real height off a shadow.

## Zooming, again

The [Isometries](../isometries/maps.mdx) chapter met the zoom, also called a dilation
(:ref{#isometries/dilation}). Pick a centre $$O$$ and a number $$r > 0$$. Each point $$P$$ slides
along the ray from $$O$$ through $$P$$ until it is $$r$$ times as far from $$O$$ as it was. The
centre stays put. With $$r = 2$$ everything spreads out to twice the distance, like a photocopier
set to $$200\%$$; with $$r = \frac{1}{2}$$ everything is pulled halfway in.

The number $$r$$ is called the **scale factor**. With the centre $$O$$ fixed, this chapter writes
$$rP$$ for the point where the zoom by $$r$$ sends $$P$$, and $$rS$$ for the zoomed copy of a shape
$$S$$, made by zooming every one of its points. So $$2P$$ is "the point twice as far from $$O$$ as
$$P$$, in the same direction", and $$rS$$ is "the shape $$S$$ zoomed by $$r$$".

The definition only talks about distances from the centre. The question for this guide is what
happens to every other length: the side of a shape, its diagonal, the distance all the way round.
Try it in the diagram below. The orange diagonal, the purple roof slope, a wall and the whole
perimeter all change by the same factor.

## When both points lie on one ray

Start with the case the definition handles directly. Two points on the same ray from the centre
slide out along that ray together. Their distances from $$O$$ are both multiplied by $$r$$, so the
gap between them is multiplied by $$r$$ as well.

:::proposition[Zooming along a ray]{#zoom-ray}

Let $$P$$ and $$Q$$ be points on one ray from the centre $$O$$. Then

```math
d(rP, rQ) = r \cdot d(P, Q)
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homothety#Derivation_of_the_properties"}

Write $$p = d(O, P)$$ and $$q = d(O, Q)$$, and suppose $$p \le q$$ (otherwise swap the names). A ray
has exactly one point at each distance from its start, as the definition of a zoom explains
(:ref{#isometries/dilation}). The segment $$OQ$$ lies along the ray and has a point at distance
$$p$$ from $$O$$ (rule 2 of :ref{#distance-and-angles/segment-rules}), so that point is $$P$$. So
$$P$$ lies on the segment $$OQ$$, and rule 1 of the same rules gives

```math
q = p + d(P, Q), \qquad \text{so} \qquad d(P, Q) = q - p
```

The zoom keeps both points on the same ray, at distances $$rp$$ and $$rq$$ from $$O$$, with
$$rp \le rq$$. The same reasoning gives

```math
d(rP, rQ) = rq - rp = r(q - p) = r \cdot d(P, Q)
```

:::

:::example[Two marks on a line]

Zoom by $$3$$ about $$O$$. Take $$P$$ and $$Q$$ on one ray from $$O$$, at distances $$2$$ and $$5$$.
They are $$5 - 2 = 3$$ apart. After the zoom they sit at distances $$6$$ and $$15$$, so they are
$$15 - 6 = 9$$ apart, which is $$3 \cdot 3$$.

:::

## A right angle at the centre

Now take two points in different directions from $$O$$, with the angle between their directions a
right angle. Each slides out along its own ray, so the rays don't change and neither does the angle
between them. The two legs of the right triangle are each multiplied by $$r$$, and the Pythagoras
theorem then says what happens to the third side.

```plane
point O 0 0 sw
point P 2 0 s
point Q 0 1.5 w
point 2P 4 0 s
point 2Q 0 3 w

segment O 2Q color=muted

right P O Q
caption "Zooming by 2 doubles both legs of the right triangle, and so doubles the third side"
```

:::proposition[Zooming a right angle at the centre]{#zoom-right-angle}

Let $$P$$ and $$Q$$ be points with the angle $$\angle POQ$$ a right angle. Then

```math
d(rP, rQ) = r \cdot d(P, Q)
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homothety#Derivation_of_the_properties"}

Write $$a = d(O, P)$$, $$b = d(O, Q)$$ and $$c = d(P, Q)$$. The triangle $$POQ$$ has a right angle
at $$O$$ with legs $$a$$ and $$b$$, so $$c^2 = a^2 + b^2$$ by :ref{#distance-and-angles/pythagoras}.

The point $$rP$$ lies on the ray $$OP$$, so the ray from $$O$$ through $$rP$$ is the ray $$OP$$
itself (:ref{#distance-and-angles/ray}). In the same way, the ray through $$rQ$$ is the ray $$OQ$$.
So the angle at $$O$$ in the triangle $$(rP)O(rQ)$$ is made by the same two rays, and is still a
right angle. Its legs are $$d(O, rP) = ra$$ and $$d(O, rQ) = rb$$, by the definition of the zoom.
The Pythagoras theorem gives

```math
d(rP, rQ)^2 = (ra)^2 + (rb)^2 = r^2(a^2 + b^2) = r^2 c^2
```

Both $$d(rP, rQ)$$ and $$rc$$ are numbers that are not negative, with the same square, so they are
equal: $$d(rP, rQ) = rc$$.

:::

:::example[A zoomed 3, 4, 5 triangle]

Let $$d(O, P) = 3$$ and $$d(O, Q) = 4$$, with a right angle at $$O$$. Then $$d(P, Q) = 5$$. Zoom by
$$2.5$$: the legs become $$7.5$$ and $$10$$, and the third side is
$$\sqrt{7.5^2 + 10^2} = \sqrt{156.25} = 12.5$$, which is $$2.5 \cdot 5$$.

:::

## The rule for every length

The two propositions cover two special arrangements of points. The same thing happens for any two
points, wherever they are: every distance is multiplied by $$r$$. The cleanest proof uses
coordinates, so here it is taken as given, together with two facts that come with it. The
[Coordinates](../coordinates/geometry-from-numbers.mdx) chapter proves the rule about distances
(:ref{#coordinates/origin-zoom}). The
[Segments, rays and lines](../segments-rays-and-lines/rules-proved.mdx) chapter proves the rule
about lines (:ref{#segments-rays-and-lines/moves-keep-lines}). The rule about angles needs angles in
coordinates, and the [Trigonometry](../trigonometry/triangles.mdx) chapter proves it
(:ref{#trigonometry/zoom-angles}).

:::remark[Rules for zooms]{#zoom-rules}

Let a zoom have scale factor $$r > 0$$. Then:

1. **Distances**: for any points $$P$$ and $$Q$$, $$d(rP, rQ) = r \cdot d(P, Q)$$
2. **Lines**: the zoom sends each line to a line, and each segment $$PQ$$ to the segment from $$rP$$
   to $$rQ$$
3. **Angles**: the zoom sends each angle to an angle of the same size

These are taken as given.

::see-also{src="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homothety#Properties"}

:::

Rule 1 is the reason a zoomed shape looks the same. The ratio of any two lengths in the shape, such
as height to width, is unchanged, because both are multiplied by $$r$$ and the $$r$$ cancels. Rule 3
says the same thing about corners: a square corner stays square.

:::example[Reading a map]

A walking map is drawn at $$1 : 50\,000$$. Think of it as the land zoomed by
$$r = \frac{1}{50\,000}$$ and then laid on the table, which moves it without changing any distance.
By rule 1, every distance on the map is $$\frac{1}{50\,000}$$ of the real one, so every real
distance is $$50\,000$$ times the distance on the map. Two villages $$4$$ cm apart on the map are
$$4 \cdot 50\,000 = 200\,000$$ cm apart, which is $$2$$ km. The rule works for any pair of points,
so a single scale bar serves the whole map.

:::

## Same shape, different size

Two photos of the same view, one printed small and one large, are the same shape. So are two maps of
one country at different scales, or a toy car and the real car it copies. Maths calls such shapes
similar: you can zoom one to the size of the other and then move it into place.

:::definition[Similar shapes]{#similar}

A shape $$S$$ is **similar** to a shape $$S'$$ if some zoom of $$S$$ is congruent to $$S'$$
(:ref{#isometries/congruent}). In other words, you can zoom $$S$$ and then slide, turn or flip the
result so that it lies exactly on $$S'$$.

:::

Congruent shapes are similar, with the zoom by $$1$$. Any two circles are similar too. For example,
zooming a circle of radius $$2$$ about its centre by $$\frac{3}{2}$$ gives a circle of radius $$3$$,
and any two circles of radius $$3$$ are congruent (:ref{#isometries/circles-congruent}). In general,
a circle of radius $$a$$ zoomed by $$\frac{b}{a}$$ has radius $$b$$.

The A sizes of paper are a neat example. An A3 sheet cut in half across the middle makes two A4
sheets, and an A4 sheet cut in half makes two A5 sheets. The sizes are chosen so that every one of
them is the same shape.

<PaperSizes caption="An A3 sheet cut across the middle makes two A4 sheets, and an A4 sheet cut the same way makes two A5 sheets. Every size is the same shape." />

:::example[A4 and A3 are similar]

An A4 sheet is $$210$$ mm by $$297$$ mm, and an A3 sheet is $$297$$ mm by $$420$$ mm. Comparing the
short sides and then the long sides:

```math
\frac{297}{210} \approx 1.414, \qquad \frac{420}{297} \approx 1.414
```

Both ratios are the same, about $$\sqrt{2}$$. So a zoom by $$1.414$$ turns an A4 sheet into the
shape and size of an A3 sheet. That is why a photocopier has a button marked $$141\%$$ for enlarging
A4 to A3, and one marked $$71\%$$ for the way back: $$\frac{1}{1.414} \approx 0.71$$.

:::

## Zooming twice

Photocopy a picture at $$150\%$$, then photocopy the copy at $$150\%$$ again. The result is not
$$300\%$$ of the original. Each copy multiplies every length by $$1.5$$, so two copies multiply by
$$1.5 \cdot 1.5 = 2.25$$: the second picture is $$225\%$$ of the first. Zooms combine by
multiplying, not adding.

:::proposition[Zooming twice]{#zoom-twice}

About the same centre $$O$$, the zoom by $$r$$ followed by the zoom by $$s$$ is the zoom by $$rs$$.
In particular, the zoom by $$\frac{1}{r}$$ undoes the zoom by $$r$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homothety#Composition"}

Both zooms leave $$O$$ where it is, and so does the zoom by $$rs$$. Take any other point $$P$$. The
first zoom sends it to the point $$rP$$ on the ray $$OP$$, at distance $$r \cdot d(O, P)$$. That
point lies on the ray $$OP$$ and isn't $$O$$, so its own ray from $$O$$ is the ray $$OP$$
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/ray}). The second zoom sends it along that same ray to distance
$$s \cdot r \cdot d(O, P)$$.

So $$P$$ ends up on the ray $$OP$$, at distance $$rs \cdot d(O, P)$$ from $$O$$. That is exactly
where the zoom by $$rs$$ sends it. For the second part, take $$s = \frac{1}{r}$$: then $$rs = 1$$,
and the zoom by $$1$$ moves nothing.

:::

## The length of a curve

A road on a map bends and winds, so you can't measure it with a ruler in one go. A common trick is
to lay a piece of string along it, then straighten the string and measure that. Another is to step
along it with a pair of compasses set to a small gap, counting the steps. Both measure the curve by
breaking it into short, nearly straight pieces.

That is also how mathematicians define the length of a curve. Mark points along the curve, join
neighbouring marks with straight segments, and add up the segments. With more marks, closer
together, the total gets as close as you like to one number, and that number is the **length** of
the curve. A careful version of this uses calculus; the picture is enough here.

<WindingRoad caption="Five straight segments with their ends on a winding road: their total is close to the road's length" />

:::theorem[Length of a curve under a zoom]{#curve-length-scaling}

A zoom by $$r$$ multiplies the length of every curve by $$r$$. In particular, it multiplies the
perimeter of every shape by $$r$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Similarity_(geometry)#In_Euclidean_space"}

Mark points along the curve and join neighbouring marks by segments, as in the definition of length.
Zoom everything. The zoomed marks lie on the zoomed curve, and by rule 2 of :ref{#zoom-rules} each
segment goes to the segment joining the zoomed marks. By rule 1, each of those segments is $$r$$
times as long as before, so their total is $$r$$ times the old total.

So each marking of the original curve gives a marking of the zoomed curve. Every marking of the
zoomed curve arises this way, because the zoom by $$\frac{1}{r}$$ undoes the zoom by $$r$$
(:ref{#zoom-twice}). And marks that are close together stay close together, at $$r$$ times the gap.

As the marks get closer together, the old totals get as close as you like to the length of the
curve, and the new totals get as close as you like to the length of the zoomed curve. Each new total
is always $$r$$ times the old one, so the two lengths must be in the same ratio: the zoomed curve is
$$r$$ times as long. For a shape with straight sides, there is no approximating to do: each side is
multiplied by $$r$$, so their sum is.

:::

:::example[A river on a map]

On a map at $$1 : 25\,000$$, a piece of string laid along a river from its source to a bridge
measures $$36$$ cm. The map is the land zoomed by $$\frac{1}{25\,000}$$, so the real river is
$$25\,000$$ times as long: $$36 \cdot 25\,000 = 900\,000$$ cm, which is $$9$$ km.

:::

## Shadows: a zoom you can see

Hold a card between a small lamp and a wall, with the card facing the wall squarely. The light from
the bulb spreads out in straight lines, so every point of the card's edge throws its shadow onto the
wall along the line from the bulb. Say the card is $$1$$ m from the bulb and the wall is $$3$$ m
from it. Then each shadow point is $$3$$ times as far from the bulb as the card point that throws
it, so the shadow is the card zoomed by $$3$$, with the bulb as the centre. The step about distances
is taken from the picture, not proved.

<LampShadow caption="The rays from the bulb past the card spread out in straight lines. The wall is 3 times as far from the bulb as the card, so the shadow is 3 times as tall." />

So a card $$20$$ cm tall throws a shadow $$60$$ cm tall, and every other length of the shadow is
$$3$$ times the matching length of the card. Moving the card to $$1.5$$ m from the bulb, halfway
between the bulb and the wall, makes the factor $$\frac{3}{1.5} = 2$$, and the shadow shrinks to
twice the card's size. Shadow puppeteers use this to make a figure loom larger by stepping towards
the lamp.

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on zooming and length, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-scaling-lengths-map}

A map is drawn at $$1 : 50\,000$$.

1. Two towns are $$7.5$$ cm apart on the map. How far apart are they?
2. A straight road is $$12$$ km long. How long is it on the map?

:::solution

The map is the land zoomed by $$\frac{1}{50\,000}$$, so by rule 1 of :ref{#zoom-rules} every real
distance is $$50\,000$$ times the map distance.

1. $$7.5 \cdot 50\,000 = 375\,000$$ cm, which is $$3\,750$$ m or $$3.75$$ km.
2. $$12$$ km is $$1\,200\,000$$ cm, and $$\frac{1\,200\,000}{50\,000} = 24$$, so the road is $$24$$
   cm long on the map.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-scaling-lengths-ray}

Zoom by $$4$$ about $$O$$. The points $$P$$ and $$Q$$ lie on one ray from $$O$$, at distances
$$1.5$$ and $$4$$ from $$O$$. Find $$d(P, Q)$$ and $$d(4P, 4Q)$$ directly from the distances to
$$O$$, and check that the second is $$4$$ times the first.

:::solution

As in the proof of :ref{#zoom-ray}, $$P$$ lies on the segment $$OQ$$, so
$$d(P, Q) = 4 - 1.5 = 2.5$$. The zoomed points lie on the same ray at distances $$4 \cdot 1.5 = 6$$
and $$4 \cdot 4 = 16$$, so $$d(4P, 4Q) = 16 - 6 = 10$$. And $$10 = 4 \cdot 2.5$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-scaling-lengths-right-angle}

The angle $$\angle POQ$$ is a right angle, with $$d(O, P) = 6$$ and $$d(O, Q) = 8$$. Find
$$d(P, Q)$$. Then zoom by $$\frac{1}{4}$$ about $$O$$ and find the three side lengths of the new
triangle without using rule 1 of :ref{#zoom-rules}.

:::solution

By :ref{#distance-and-angles/pythagoras}, $$d(P, Q) = \sqrt{36 + 64} = \sqrt{100} = 10$$.

The zoom by $$\frac{1}{4}$$ sends $$P$$ and $$Q$$ along their own rays, to distances
$$\frac{6}{4} = 1.5$$ and $$\frac{8}{4} = 2$$ from $$O$$. The angle at $$O$$ is still a right angle,
as in the proof of :ref{#zoom-right-angle}, so the third side is
$$\sqrt{1.5^2 + 2^2} = \sqrt{2.25 + 4} = \sqrt{6.25} = 2.5$$. The new sides are $$1.5$$, $$2$$ and
$$2.5$$, each a quarter of the old ones.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-scaling-lengths-photocopier}

A photocopier enlarges or reduces by a percentage.

1. You copy a drawing at $$125\%$$ and then copy the copy at $$80\%$$. How big is the result?
2. What single setting turns A5 into A3? Use the $$141\%$$ setting that turns A5 into A4 and A4 into
   A3.
3. You copy at $$120\%$$ three times in a row. Is the result bigger or smaller than one copy at
   $$160\%$$?

:::solution

Each copy is a zoom, and zooms combine by multiplying (:ref{#zoom-twice}).

1. $$1.25 \cdot 0.8 = 1$$, so the result is the same size as the original drawing.
2. Two steps of $$141\%$$ give $$1.41 \cdot 1.41 \approx 1.99$$, so the setting is $$200\%$$. This
   makes sense: an A3 sheet holds four A5 sheets, two across and two down, so each length doubles.
3. $$1.2^3 = 1.2 \cdot 1.2 \cdot 1.2 = 1.728$$, so three copies at $$120\%$$ give $$172.8\%$$, which
   is bigger than $$160\%$$. Adding up the steps ($$20\% + 20\% + 20\%$$) would wrongly suggest
   $$160\%$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-scaling-lengths-rectangles}

Is every rectangle similar to every other rectangle? Show that a $$1$$ by $$1$$ square and a $$1$$
by $$2$$ rectangle are not similar.

:::solution

**No.** Suppose a zoom by $$r$$ of the square were congruent to the $$1$$ by $$2$$ rectangle
(:ref{#similar}). The zoom multiplies every length by $$r$$ (rule 1 of :ref{#zoom-rules}), so the
zoomed square has all four sides equal to $$r$$. An isometry keeps every length
(:ref{#isometries/isometry}) and sends segments to segments, so it would lay the four equal sides of
the zoomed square onto the four sides of the rectangle. But the rectangle has sides $$1$$ and $$2$$,
which are not equal. (That the isometry sends corners to corners is taken from the picture.)

The same reasoning shows that similar shapes have the same ratio of long side to short side: a zoom
multiplies both by $$r$$, and an isometry changes neither. Two rectangles with ratios $$1$$ and
$$2$$ can't be similar.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-scaling-lengths-shadow}

A hand is held $$40$$ cm from a small lamp, and the wall is $$2$$ m from the lamp.

1. What is the scale factor of the shadow?
2. The hand is $$12$$ cm across. How wide is its shadow?
3. Where should the hand be held so that its shadow is $$36$$ cm across?

:::solution

1. The wall is $$\frac{200}{40} = 5$$ times as far from the bulb as the hand, so the shadow is the
   hand zoomed by $$5$$ about the bulb.
2. Every length is multiplied by $$5$$ (rule 1 of :ref{#zoom-rules}), so the shadow is
   $$5 \cdot 12 = 60$$ cm across.
3. The scale factor must be $$\frac{36}{12} = 3$$, so the wall must be $$3$$ times as far from the
   bulb as the hand. The hand goes $$\frac{200}{3} \approx 66.7$$ cm from the lamp.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-scaling-lengths-fence}

A plan of a field is drawn at $$1 : 2\,000$$, and its boundary on the plan measures $$46$$ cm. How
many metres of fencing go round the real field? If the plan were redrawn at $$1 : 500$$, how long
would the boundary be on the new plan?

:::solution

The real field is the plan zoomed by $$2\,000$$, so its perimeter is $$2\,000$$ times as long
(:ref{#curve-length-scaling}): $$46 \cdot 2\,000 = 92\,000$$ cm, which is $$920$$ m of fencing.

The new plan is the field zoomed by $$\frac{1}{500}$$, so the boundary is
$$\frac{92\,000}{500} = 184$$ cm long on it. Going straight from one plan to the other, the scale
factor is $$\frac{2\,000}{500} = 4$$ (:ref{#zoom-twice}), and $$4 \cdot 46 = 184$$ as well.

:::
