These exercises draw on the whole chapter, and most of them need ideas from more than one guide.
Some ask for a number, some ask you to compare two choices, and a few ask you to explain why
something is true. Try each one before opening its solution, and say which rule or result justifies
each step, as the guides do.

Many of them turn on one question: is the change a zoom, which multiplies every length by the same
number, or something else? Once you know the scale factor $$r$$, lengths go up by $$r$$, areas by
$$r^2$$ and volumes by $$r^3$$.

## Comparing sizes

::::exercise{#ex-area-and-scaling-pizza-deal}

A pizza shop offers three deals:

- one 16-inch pizza for $$18$$ pounds
- two 10-inch pizzas for $$14$$ pounds
- one 12-inch pizza for $$10$$ pounds

The sizes are the distances across. All the pizzas are equally thick.

1. Which deal gives the most pizza for each pound?
2. Someone who loves crust wants the most edge for each square inch of pizza. Which size should they
   pick?

:::solution

1. A pizza $$d$$ inches across is a disc of radius $$\frac{d}{2}$$, so by :ref{#disc-area-formula}
   its area is $$\pi \left(\frac{d}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{\pi d^2}{4}$$ square inches. The three deals
   give:

   - **16-inch**: $$\frac{\pi \cdot 256}{4} = 64\pi \approx 201.1$$ square inches, so about
     $$201.1 \div 18 \approx 11.17$$ for each pound
   - **Two 10-inch**: $$2 \cdot \frac{\pi \cdot 100}{4} = 50\pi \approx 157.1$$ square inches, so
     about $$157.1 \div 14 \approx 11.22$$ for each pound
   - **12-inch**: $$\frac{\pi \cdot 144}{4} = 36\pi \approx 113.1$$ square inches, so about
     $$113.1 \div 10 \approx 11.31$$ for each pound

   The single 12-inch pizza is the best value, though only by a little. The three deals are much
   closer than the prices suggest, because a pizza's area grows with the square of its width.

2. The edge of a pizza $$d$$ inches across has length $$\pi d$$ (:ref{#pi-ratio}). For each square
   inch, that is

   ```math
   \frac{\pi d}{\pi d^2 / 4} = \frac{4}{d}
   ```

   This is biggest when $$d$$ is smallest, so the crust lover should pick the 10-inch pizzas:
   $$0.4$$ inches of edge for each square inch, against $$0.33$$ for the 12-inch and $$0.25$$ for
   the 16-inch. A zoom by $$r$$ multiplies the edge by $$r$$ (:ref{#curve-length-scaling}) but the
   area by $$r^2$$ (:ref{#area-scaling}), so bigger pizzas have less edge for their size.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-area-and-scaling-cake-tin}

A recipe fills a round cake tin $$20$$ cm across to a depth of $$4$$ cm. You want to make twice the
recipe and bake it to the same depth. Will a round tin $$28$$ cm across do?

:::solution

The batter is a slab of fixed depth, so its volume is the depth times the area of the bottom of the
tin. Twice the recipe at the same depth needs twice the area.

The $$28$$ cm tin is the $$20$$ cm tin zoomed by $$\frac{28}{20} = 1.4$$. By :ref{#area-scaling},
its bottom has $$1.4^2 = 1.96$$ times the area. That is close to $$2$$, so the doubled recipe fits,
slightly deeper than $$4$$ cm: about $$4 \cdot \frac{2}{1.96} \approx 4.08$$ cm.

A tin $$40$$ cm across, twice as wide, would hold four times the batter at the same depth. Doubling
the area needs a scale factor of $$\sqrt{2} \approx 1.41$$, not $$2$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-area-and-scaling-model-shed}

An architect builds a model of a garden shed at a scale of $$1 : 20$$. The real shed needs $$15$$
litres of paint, and the air inside it fills $$18$$ cubic metres.

1. How much paint does the model need, if the paint goes on equally thick?
2. How much air does the model hold?

:::solution

The model is the shed zoomed by $$r = \frac{1}{20}$$.

1. The paint covers the surface, and a zoom by $$r$$ multiplies surface area by $$r^2$$
   (:ref{#surface-scaling}). So the model needs $$15 \cdot \frac{1}{400} = 0.0375$$ litres, which is
   $$37.5$$ millilitres.
2. Volume is multiplied by $$r^3$$ (:ref{#volume-scaling}). So the model holds
   $$18 \cdot \frac{1}{8000} = 0.00225$$ cubic metres, which is $$2.25$$ litres.

The two answers shrink by different amounts: the paint by $$400$$ times and the air by $$8000$$
times. That gap is the square-cube law (:ref{#square-cube}) at work.

:::

## Measuring with grids

::::exercise{#ex-area-and-scaling-forest-map}

A forest is drawn on a map at a scale of $$1 : 50\,000$$. You lay a grid of $$1$$ cm squares over it
and find $$14$$ squares wholly inside the forest and $$22$$ that overlap it.

1. What do these counts tell you about the real area of the forest, in square kilometres?
2. You cut each square into four, making a grid of $$0.5$$ cm squares. Now $$66$$ squares are wholly
   inside and $$82$$ overlap. What do you know now?
3. Explain why cutting each square into four can never lower the inner count or raise the outer
   count.

:::solution

1. On the map, the inner count is $$14$$ square centimetres and the outer count is $$22$$, so by
   :ref{#grid-squeeze} the forest covers between $$14$$ and $$22$$ square centimetres of the map.
   The land is the map zoomed by $$50\,000$$, so $$1$$ cm on the map is $$50\,000$$ cm, or $$500$$
   m, on the ground. A $$1$$ cm square on the map stands for a square of side $$500$$ m, with area
   $$0.25$$ square kilometres (:ref{#rectangle-scaling}). So the forest covers between
   $$14 \cdot 0.25 = 3.5$$ and $$22 \cdot 0.25 = 5.5$$ square kilometres.
2. Each new square has side $$0.5$$ cm on the map, which stands for $$250$$ m, so each one stands
   for $$0.0625$$ square kilometres. The forest covers between $$66 \cdot 0.0625 = 4.125$$ and
   $$82 \cdot 0.0625 = 5.125$$ square kilometres. The gap has shrunk from $$2$$ to $$1$$.
3. **Inner count**: a square wholly inside the forest is cut into four smaller squares, and each of
   them is inside it too. So every bit of area counted before is still counted, and the inner count
   can only stay the same or grow.

   **Outer count**: a small square that overlaps the forest lies inside one of the big squares, and
   that big square overlaps the forest too. So every small square counted now lies inside a big
   square counted before, and the outer count can only stay the same or shrink.

   Finer grids squeeze the area from both sides, which is why :ref{#grid-rule} can pin it down.

:::

## Circles

::::exercise{#ex-area-and-scaling-goat-wall}

A goat is tied by a $$6$$ metre rope to a ring in the middle of a long straight wall. The goat can
graze on one side of the wall only.

1. How much grass can the goat reach?
2. How long a rope would let it reach twice as much grass?
3. How long is the curved edge of the grazed patch with the $$6$$ metre rope?

:::solution

1. The goat can reach every point within $$6$$ metres of the ring on its side of the wall. That is
   half a disc of radius $$6$$: the wall runs through the centre, and a line through the centre
   halves a disc. The whole disc has area $$\pi \cdot 6^2 = 36\pi$$ (:ref{#disc-area-formula}), so
   the goat reaches $$18\pi \approx 56.5$$ square metres.
2. The patch for a rope of length $$\ell$$ is the $$6$$ metre patch zoomed by $$\frac{\ell}{6}$$
   about the ring, so its area is multiplied by $$\left(\frac{\ell}{6}\right)^2$$
   (:ref{#area-scaling}). For twice the area, $$\left(\frac{\ell}{6}\right)^2 = 2$$, so
   $$\ell = 6\sqrt{2} \approx 8.49$$ metres. Twice the grass needs only about $$41\%$$ more rope.
3. The curved edge is half the circle of radius $$6$$. The whole circle has length
   $$2\pi \cdot 6 = 12\pi$$ (:ref{#circumference-formula}), so the edge is $$6\pi \approx 18.8$$
   metres long.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-area-and-scaling-bike-computer}

A bike computer counts how many times the front wheel turns and works out the distance from that. It
has been set up for a wheel $$70$$ cm across, but the bike's wheel is $$66$$ cm across. After a
ride, it shows $$35$$ km. How far did the bike actually go?

:::solution

Each turn of the wheel lays its edge flat along the road, so the bike moves one circumference for
each turn. By :ref{#pi-ratio}, a wheel $$70$$ cm across has circumference $$70\pi$$ cm and a wheel
$$66$$ cm across has circumference $$66\pi$$ cm.

The computer showed $$35$$ km because it counted $$N$$ turns and multiplied by $$70\pi$$ cm. The
real distance is the same $$N$$ turns times $$66\pi$$ cm. So the real distance is

```math
35 \cdot \frac{66\pi}{70\pi} = 35 \cdot \frac{66}{70} = 33 \text{ km}
```

The $$\pi$$ cancels: only the ratio of the two widths matters. That is :ref{#curve-length-scaling}
again, since the smaller wheel's edge is the larger one's zoomed by $$\frac{66}{70}$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-area-and-scaling-fence}

A farmer has $$400$$ metres of fence. Compare the area of a square field and of a round field that
the fence can surround.

:::solution

**Square**: each side is $$\frac{400}{4} = 100$$ metres, so the area is $$100^2 = 10\,000$$ square
metres (:ref{#distance-and-angles/area-rules}).

**Round**: the fence is the circumference, so $$2\pi r = 400$$ (:ref{#circumference-formula}), and
the radius is $$r = \frac{400}{2\pi} \approx 63.66$$ metres. The area is

```math
\pi r^2 = \pi \left(\frac{400}{2\pi}\right)^2 = \frac{400^2}{4\pi} \approx 12\,732
```

square metres (:ref{#disc-area-formula}). The same fence encloses about $$27\%$$ more ground when it
is laid in a circle. In fact no shape with a $$400$$ metre boundary encloses more than the circle
does, though proving that is beyond this chapter.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-area-and-scaling-one-pi}

A friend says: "The $$\pi$$ in the area $$\pi r^2$$ and the $$\pi$$ in the circumference $$2\pi r$$
could be two different numbers that happen to be close." Explain, in a few sentences, why they are
the same number.

:::solution

In this chapter, $$\pi$$ is defined once, as the area of a disc of radius $$1$$ (:ref{#pi}). The
area formula $$\pi r^2$$ uses that number directly (:ref{#disc-area-formula}). The question is why
the circumference involves it too.

The link is the regular polygon in a circle. It is made of $$n$$ equal triangles from the centre, so
its area is half its perimeter times the height of one triangle (:ref{#inscribed-area}). As $$n$$
grows, the area closes in on the disc's area $$\pi r^2$$, the perimeter closes in on the
circumference $$C$$, and the height closes in on $$r$$. So $$\pi r^2 = \frac{1}{2} C r$$, which
gives $$C = 2\pi r$$ (:ref{#circumference-formula}).

So the circumference is pinned to the area by the slices. Cutting a pizza into thin slices and
laying them head to tail makes a near-rectangle half the circumference long and one radius tall, and
its area is the pizza's area. There is no room for a second number.

:::

## Stretching and zooming

::::exercise{#ex-area-and-scaling-oval-rug}

An oval rug is an ellipse $$3$$ m long and $$2$$ m wide.

1. Find its area.
2. A dolls' house has a copy of the rug at a scale of $$1 : 12$$. Find the area of the copy, in
   square centimetres.
3. A second rug is the first one stretched by $$1.5$$ along its length only. Find its area, and say
   whether it is similar to the first rug.

:::solution

1. The half-widths are $$1.5$$ m and $$1$$ m, so by :ref{#ellipse-area} the area is
   $$\pi \cdot 1.5 \cdot 1 = 1.5\pi \approx 4.71$$ square metres.
2. The copy is the rug zoomed by $$\frac{1}{12}$$, so its area is multiplied by $$\frac{1}{144}$$
   (:ref{#area-scaling}). That is about $$\frac{4.712}{144} \approx 0.0327$$ square metres, or about
   $$327$$ square centimetres, about the size of two postcards.
3. A stretch by $$1.5$$ multiplies area by $$1.5$$ (:ref{#stretch-area}), so the second rug has area
   $$1.5 \cdot 1.5\pi = 2.25\pi \approx 7.07$$ square metres. It is $$4.5$$ m long and still $$2$$ m
   wide. The first rug is $$1.5$$ times as long as it is wide, and the second is $$2.25$$ times as
   long. A zoom keeps that ratio, since it multiplies both widths by the same number
   (:ref{#zoom-rules}), and an isometry keeps both widths. So no zoom followed by an isometry turns
   one rug into the other, and they are not similar (:ref{#similar}).

:::

::::exercise{#ex-area-and-scaling-photo-resize}

A photo is $$1200$$ pixels wide and $$800$$ tall, and it shows a round clock face $$200$$ pixels
across. You resize the photo in two ways:

- **Stretched**: $$1800$$ wide and still $$800$$ tall
- **Zoomed**: $$1800$$ wide and $$1200$$ tall

For each, describe the clock face and find how its area changes. Then explain how the two answers
fit together.

:::solution

**Stretched**: the width is multiplied by $$1.5$$ and the height is unchanged. That is a stretch by
$$1.5$$ away from an upright line (:ref{#stretch}). The clock becomes an ellipse $$300$$ pixels wide
and $$200$$ tall (:ref{#ellipse}), and its area is multiplied by $$1.5$$ (:ref{#stretch-area}).
Round things look fat.

**Zoomed**: both directions are multiplied by $$1.5$$, so this is a zoom by $$1.5$$. The clock stays
a circle, now $$300$$ pixels across, and its area is multiplied by $$1.5^2 = 2.25$$
(:ref{#area-scaling}).

**How they fit**: the zoom is the stretch you already did, followed by a second stretch by $$1.5$$
away from a level line, which takes the height from $$800$$ to $$1200$$. Each stretch multiplies
area by $$1.5$$, so together they multiply it by $$1.5 \cdot 1.5 = 2.25$$, the same answer as the
zoom gives.

:::

## The square-cube law

::::exercise{#ex-area-and-scaling-chicken-turkey}

A chicken of $$1.5$$ kg and a turkey of $$12$$ kg have, roughly, the same shape. In an oven, heat
gets into a bird through its skin.

1. What is the scale factor from the chicken to the turkey?
2. How many times as much skin does the turkey have?
3. How much skin does the turkey have for each kilogram, compared with the chicken? What does that
   suggest about cooking times?

:::solution

1. Meat has the same weight in every cubic centimetre, so weight goes up with volume. The turkey has
   $$\frac{12}{1.5} = 8$$ times the volume. A zoom by $$r$$ multiplies volume by $$r^3$$
   (:ref{#volume-scaling}), so $$r^3 = 8$$ and $$r = 2$$. The turkey is the chicken with every
   length doubled.
2. Surface is multiplied by $$r^2 = 4$$ (:ref{#surface-scaling}), so the turkey has $$4$$ times the
   skin.
3. The turkey has $$4$$ times the skin for $$8$$ times the meat, so each kilogram gets half as much
   skin to let heat in (:ref{#square-cube}). Heat also has twice as far to travel to reach the
   middle. So each kilogram of turkey heats more slowly than a kilogram of chicken, and the turkey
   needs more cooking time for each kilogram, not the same. That is why large roasts are cooked for
   a long time at a gentle heat.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-area-and-scaling-cat-tiger}

A $$4$$ kg cat and a $$200$$ kg tiger are, roughly, the same shape. Suppose the tiger were the cat
zoomed exactly. By how many times would the load on each square centimetre of its leg bones go up?
What does that say about the legs real tigers have?

:::solution

The tiger has $$\frac{200}{4} = 50$$ times the weight, so $$50$$ times the volume. A zoom by $$r$$
multiplies volume by $$r^3$$ (:ref{#volume-scaling}), so $$r^3 = 50$$ and
$$r = \sqrt[3]{50} \approx 3.68$$.

A bone's strength depends on the area of its cross-section, which is multiplied by
$$r^2 \approx 13.6$$ (:ref{#area-scaling}). The weight is multiplied by $$50$$. So each square
centimetre of bone carries about $$\frac{50}{13.6} \approx 3.68$$ times the load, which is $$r$$
again (:ref{#square-cube}).

Bone is the same material in both animals, so an exact zoom of a cat would have legs under far more
strain. Real tigers are not exact zooms of cats: their legs are thicker for their size, which gives
the extra bone area the square-cube law calls for.

:::
