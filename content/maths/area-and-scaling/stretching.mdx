---
title: Stretching one way
description:
  Pulling a shape in one direction multiplies its area by the stretch, which gives the area of an
  ellipse.
---

Roll out a ball of pastry with strokes in one direction only, and a round disc becomes an oval:
longer one way, the same width the other. Look at a round plate on a table from where you sit, and
it looks squashed in the same way, because you see it at a slant. A change like this is called a
stretch. Unlike a zoom, it changes lengths in one direction only.

In this guide we'll see what a stretch does to area, and use it to find the area of an oval called
an ellipse.

## A stretch away from a line

Think of a rubber sheet pinned along a straight line $$L$$. Pull the sheet away from the line on
both sides, evenly, so that every point ends up twice as far from $$L$$ as it was. Points on the
line don't move, and every other point moves straight away from the line, square on to it. Nothing
slides along the line. That is a stretch by $$2$$ away from $$L$$.

:::definition[Stretch away from a line]{#stretch}

Fix a line $$L$$ and a number $$a > 0$$. The **stretch by** $$a$$ **away from** $$L$$ is the map
that sends each point $$P$$ to a point $$P'$$ as follows:

- **$$P$$ on $$L$$**: $$P' = P$$, so the point stays where it is
- **$$P$$ not on $$L$$**: let $$F$$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $$P$$ to $$L$$. Then
  $$P'$$ is the point of the ray $$FP$$ with

```math
d(F, P') = a \cdot d(F, P)
```

So $$P'$$ is on the same side of $$L$$ as $$P$$, and $$a$$ times as far from $$L$$.

:::

For $$a > 1$$ the sheet is pulled out, and for $$a < 1$$ it is squashed towards the line. A stretch
by $$1$$ moves nothing. Each point moves as if it were zoomed by $$a$$ about its own foot $$F$$, so
a stretch is like a zoom with a different centre for every point, all lined up along $$L$$. With
coordinates and $$L$$ as the horizontal axis, a stretch is the rule $$(x, y) \mapsto (x, ay)$$, as
the [Geometry from numbers](../coordinates/geometry-from-numbers.mdx) guide shows.

```plane

point B 3.5 0 hidden
point F -1.5 0 s
point P -1.5 1 w
point P' -1.5 2 w
point G 1.5 0 n
point Q 1.5 -0.75 e
point Q' 1.5 -1.5 e
line A B color=purple
text 3.3 0.35 "L"
segment F P
segment P P' color=orange dashed
segment G Q
segment Q Q' color=orange dashed
right B F P
right A G Q
caption "A stretch by 2 away from L: each point moves square on to L, ending twice as far from it"
```

## Stretching a rectangle

Start with the shape whose area you know best. Take a rectangle lying on one side of $$L$$, with two
of its sides parallel to $$L$$. Stretching pulls its two parallel sides further from $$L$$, and the
sides square on to $$L$$ get longer. The parallel sides keep their length, because all their points
move by the same amount in the same direction.

:::proposition[Stretching a rectangle]{#stretch-rectangle}

Let $$S$$ be a rectangle of width $$w$$ and height $$h$$, with its width parallel to $$L$$, lying on
one side of $$L$$ or touching it. The stretch by $$a$$ away from $$L$$ sends $$S$$ to a rectangle of
width $$w$$ and height $$a h$$. So it multiplies the area by $$a$$:

```math
\text{area of the stretched rectangle} = w \cdot a h = a \cdot (w h)
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squeeze_mapping"}

Say the two sides parallel to $$L$$ are at distances $$s$$ and $$s + h$$ from $$L$$. Every point of
a line parallel to $$L$$ is the same distance from $$L$$
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/parallel-distance}), so all the points of the nearer side move straight
out, square on to $$L$$, to distance $$as$$. They all move by the same amount in the same direction,
so the side moves without changing its length: it stays a segment of length $$w$$, parallel to
$$L$$. In the same way, the far side ends up at distance $$a(s + h)$$, still of length $$w$$.

The other two sides lie on lines square on to $$L$$, and the stretch moves points along exactly
those lines. A side running from distance $$s$$ to distance $$s + h$$ goes to the segment from
$$as$$ to $$a(s + h)$$ on the same line, of length $$a(s + h) - as = ah$$. So the image has two
sides of length $$w$$ parallel to $$L$$ and two of length $$ah$$ square on to it, meeting at right
angles: a rectangle $$w$$ by $$ah$$. By :ref{#distance-and-angles/area-rules}, its area is
$$w \cdot ah = a \cdot wh$$.

:::

A rectangle that crosses $$L$$ can be cut along $$L$$ into two rectangles, one on each side. Each
piece has its area multiplied by $$a$$, so the whole does too.

## Stretching any shape

For a curved shape, use the same idea as for zooms: lay a grid over it, with lines parallel and
square on to $$L$$ and with $$L$$ itself as one of the lines. The stretch turns each little square
into a tall thin rectangle (or a short wide one, if $$a < 1$$), all with area $$a$$ times the
square's. So the inner and outer counts are both multiplied by $$a$$, and the area caught between
them is too. The proof below checks that the squares counted for the shape match the rectangles
counted for the stretched shape.

<StretchDisc />

:::theorem[Stretching multiplies area by the stretch factor]{#stretch-area}

The stretch by $$a$$ away from a line $$L$$ multiplies the area of every shape by $$a$$:

```math
\text{area of the stretched shape} = a \cdot (\text{area of the shape})
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavalieri%27s_principle"}

Lay a grid of squares of side $$c$$ with lines parallel and square on to $$L$$, and $$L$$ one of the
grid lines. Each square lies on one side of $$L$$, so by :ref{#stretch-rectangle} the stretch sends
it to a rectangle $$c$$ by $$ac$$, with area $$a c^2$$. These rectangles fit together into a grid of
their own, with no gaps or overlaps.

The stretch by $$\frac{1}{a}$$ away from $$L$$ undoes the stretch by $$a$$
(:ref{#ex-stretching-undo}), so a point lies in the stretched shape exactly when it comes from a
point of the shape. So a square lies wholly inside the shape exactly when its rectangle lies wholly
inside the stretched shape, and a square overlaps the shape exactly when its rectangle overlaps the
stretched shape. Count with rectangles in place of squares, in the same way as :ref{#grid-counts}:
the inner and outer counts of the stretched shape are $$a$$ times those of the shape. The argument
of :ref{#grid-squeeze} works for rectangles too, since it uses only :ref{#area-compare} and
:ref{#distance-and-angles/area-rules}, so the area of the stretched shape lies between these two
counts.

As $$c$$ shrinks, the counts for the shape close in on its area (:ref{#grid-rule}). The rectangles
also shrink in both directions, and the same band argument as :ref{#edge-band}, with the rectangle's
diagonal in place of the square's, closes the counts in on the area of the stretched shape. That
last step is taken from the picture, as for squares, and calculus makes it precise. Both areas are
pinned down by counts that differ by the factor $$a$$, so the area of the stretched shape is $$a$$
times the area of the shape.

:::

A zoom fits into this picture too. Take two lines crossing square on at $$O$$. Stretch by $$r$$ away
from one line, then by $$r$$ away from the other. Every point ends up $$r$$ times as far from both
lines. The zoom by $$r$$ about $$O$$ also sends each point $$r$$ times as far from both lines, so
the two stretches together are the zoom. That last step is taken from the picture here. In
coordinates it is a short calculation: the two stretches send $$(x, y)$$ to $$(x, ry)$$ and then to
$$(rx, ry)$$, which is the zoom (:ref{#coordinates/origin-zoom}). The two stretches multiply the
area by $$r$$ and then by $$r$$ again, giving the factor $$r^2$$ of :ref{#area-scaling}.

## The ellipse

Stretch a disc in two directions at right angles by different amounts, and you get the oval shape of
a squashed circle. It turns up wherever a circle is seen at a slant: the top of a mug in a photo, a
round window drawn in perspective, the shadow of a ball on the ground in the late afternoon.

:::definition[Ellipse]{#ellipse}

Take the disc of radius $$1$$ with centre $$O$$, and two lines crossing square on at $$O$$. Stretch
it by $$a$$ away from the first line, and then by $$b$$ away from the second. The result is an
**ellipse** (strictly, the region inside one), with **half-widths** $$a$$ and $$b$$. The first
stretch moves points square on to the first line, so the ellipse reaches $$a$$ from $$O$$ in that
direction. In the same way it reaches $$b$$ from $$O$$ square on to the second line.

:::

A disc of radius $$r$$ is the case $$a = b = r$$, since two stretches by $$r$$ make the zoom by
$$r$$ (see [Stretching any shape](#stretching-any-shape)). Ellipses can also be described by a fixed
total distance from two points, which is how gardeners draw an oval bed with two pegs and a loop of
string. That description gives the same shapes, but this guide only needs the stretched circle.

:::theorem[Area of an ellipse]{#ellipse-area}

An ellipse with half-widths $$a$$ and $$b$$ has area

```math
\pi a b
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellipse#Area"}

The disc of radius $$1$$ has area $$\pi$$ (:ref{#pi}). The first stretch multiplies its area by
$$a$$, and the second by $$b$$ (:ref{#stretch-area}). So the ellipse has area
$$\pi \cdot a \cdot b$$.

:::

With $$a = b = r$$ this gives $$\pi r^2$$, the area of a disc (:ref{#disc-area-formula}), so the
formula fits what you already know.

:::example[An oval table]

An oval table in the shape of an ellipse is $$2$$ metres long and $$1.2$$ metres wide, so its
half-widths are $$1$$ and $$0.6$$ metres. Its area is

```math
\pi \cdot 1 \cdot 0.6 \approx 1.88 \text{ square metres}
```

A rectangular table of the same length and width would have $$2 \cdot 1.2 = 2.4$$ square metres. The
oval fills $$\frac{1.88}{2.4} \approx 0.785$$ of it, which is $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$: the same share that
a round table fills of the square it fits in.

:::

## Lengths under a stretch

Area behaves well under a stretch: every shape's area is multiplied by the same factor $$a$$.
Lengths do not. A segment parallel to $$L$$ keeps its length, a segment square on to $$L$$ is
multiplied by $$a$$, and a slanted segment is changed by some amount in between, depending on its
direction.

:::example[A stretched square]

Take a square of side $$1$$ with two sides parallel to $$L$$, and stretch it by $$2$$ away from
$$L$$. It becomes a rectangle $$1$$ by $$2$$. The sides parallel to $$L$$ stay $$1$$ long, the other
sides double, and the diagonal goes from $$\sqrt{2} \approx 1.41$$ to
$$\sqrt{1 + 4} = \sqrt{5} \approx 2.24$$ (:ref{#distance-and-angles/pythagoras}). That is $$1.58$$
times as long, neither $$1$$ nor $$2$$.

:::

So there is no tidy formula for the distance round an ellipse. A circle's length could be found from
the circle of radius $$1$$ by zooming, which multiplies every length by the same amount
(:ref{#curve-length-scaling}). A stretch multiplies different parts of the curve by different
amounts, so that argument breaks down. The distance round an ellipse can be written as an endless
sum, and good approximations exist, such as one found by Ramanujan. But there is no formula as clean
as $$\pi a b$$ for the area.

::see-also{src="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellipse#Circumference"}

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on stretching, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-stretching-rectangle}

A rectangle is $$3$$ cm wide and $$2$$ cm tall, with its width parallel to a line $$L$$ and its
bottom side on $$L$$. Describe the image under the stretch by $$4$$ away from $$L$$, and check that
the area is multiplied by $$4$$. Where does the top side end up?

:::solution

The bottom side lies on $$L$$, so it stays where it is. The top side is $$2$$ cm from $$L$$, so it
moves out to $$4 \cdot 2 = 8$$ cm, keeping its length $$3$$ cm. The image is a rectangle $$3$$ cm by
$$8$$ cm (:ref{#stretch-rectangle}).

The area goes from $$3 \cdot 2 = 6$$ to $$3 \cdot 8 = 24$$ square centimetres, and
$$24 = 4 \cdot 6$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-stretching-ellipse}

An ellipse has half-widths $$5$$ cm and $$3$$ cm. Find its area. What is the radius of the disc with
the same area?

:::solution

By :ref{#ellipse-area}, the area is $$\pi \cdot 5 \cdot 3 = 15\pi \approx 47.1$$ square centimetres.
A disc of radius $$r$$ has area $$\pi r^2$$ (:ref{#disc-area-formula}), so the same area needs
$$r^2 = 15$$, that is $$r = \sqrt{15} \approx 3.87$$ cm. That radius lies between the two
half-widths, as you would expect.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-stretching-share}

Show that every ellipse fills the same share $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$ of the rectangle that fits round it,
with sides $$2a$$ and $$2b$$. Then explain the same fact using stretches alone, without the area
formula.

:::solution

The ellipse has area $$\pi a b$$ (:ref{#ellipse-area}) and the rectangle $$2a \cdot 2b = 4ab$$. The
share is $$\frac{\pi a b}{4 a b} = \frac{\pi}{4}$$.

With stretches: start from the disc of radius $$1$$ inside its $$2$$ by $$2$$ square, with the
square's sides parallel to the two lines. The disc fills a share $$\frac{\pi}{4}$$ of it. The two
stretches that make the ellipse turn the square into the rectangle $$2a$$ by $$2b$$ round it
(:ref{#stretch-rectangle}). Each stretch multiplies both areas by the same factor
(:ref{#stretch-area}), so the share doesn't change.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-stretching-undo}

1. What single stretch undoes the stretch by $$a$$ away from $$L$$?
2. Stretch by $$a$$ away from $$L$$, then by $$b$$ away from the same line. What single map is this?

:::solution

1. The stretch by $$\frac{1}{a}$$ away from $$L$$. A point at distance $$s$$ from $$L$$ goes out to
   $$as$$ and then back to $$\frac{1}{a} \cdot as = s$$, on the same perpendicular and the same
   side, so it returns to where it started. Points of $$L$$ never move.
2. The stretch by $$ab$$ away from $$L$$. A point at distance $$s$$ goes to $$as$$ and then to
   $$b \cdot as = ab\,s$$, always along its own perpendicular to $$L$$ and on its own side. The
   areas agree: first $$\times a$$, then $$\times b$$, which is $$\times ab$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-stretching-diagonal}

A square of side $$2$$ has two sides parallel to $$L$$. It is stretched by $$\frac{1}{2}$$ away from
$$L$$. Find the lengths of the sides and the diagonal of the image, and the factor each was
multiplied by.

:::solution

The sides parallel to $$L$$ keep their length $$2$$, a factor of $$1$$. The sides square on to $$L$$
become $$\frac{1}{2} \cdot 2 = 1$$, a factor of $$\frac{1}{2}$$ (:ref{#stretch-rectangle}).

The diagonal was $$\sqrt{2^2 + 2^2} = \sqrt{8} \approx 2.83$$ and becomes
$$\sqrt{2^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{5} \approx 2.24$$ (:ref{#distance-and-angles/pythagoras}). The factor is
$$\frac{\sqrt{5}}{\sqrt{8}} \approx 0.79$$, between $$\frac{1}{2}$$ and $$1$$. Lengths in different
directions change by different amounts, while the area changes by exactly $$\frac{1}{2}$$: from
$$4$$ to $$2$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-stretching-plate}

A round plate $$24$$ cm across, seen at a slant in a photo, appears as an ellipse $$24$$ cm wide and
$$9$$ cm deep (at the photo's scale). By what factor has it been squashed, and what fraction of the
plate's area does the image show?

:::solution

The width stays $$24$$, so the half-widths are $$12$$ and $$4.5$$. The depth went from $$24$$ to
$$9$$, a stretch by $$\frac{9}{24} = \frac{3}{8}$$ away from the line across the middle of the
plate.

The plate has area $$\pi \cdot 12^2 = 144\pi \approx 452$$ and the image
$$\pi \cdot 12 \cdot 4.5 = 54\pi \approx 170$$ square centimetres (:ref{#ellipse-area}). The share
is $$\frac{54}{144} = \frac{3}{8}$$, the stretch factor, as :ref{#stretch-area} says.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-stretching-not-zoom}

A shape has area $$10$$. It is stretched by $$3$$ away from a line $$L$$, and then by $$3$$ away
from a line $$K$$ that crosses $$L$$ square on. What is the area now? Is the result similar to the
original shape?

:::solution

Each stretch multiplies the area by $$3$$ (:ref{#stretch-area}), so the area is
$$3 \cdot 3 \cdot 10 = 90$$.

The two stretches together send every point to $$3$$ times as far from both lines, which is the zoom
by $$3$$ about the crossing point, as [Stretching any shape](#stretching-any-shape) explains. So the
result is a zoom of the original shape, and similar to it (:ref{#similar}). The area agrees with
:ref{#area-scaling}: $$3^2 \cdot 10 = 90$$. With two different factors, the shape would be squashed
one way, and in general no longer similar.

:::
