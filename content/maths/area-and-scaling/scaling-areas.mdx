---
title: Zooming and area
description:
  Why a zoom by r multiplies every area by r squared, from rectangles to any shape at all.
---

A pizza shop sells a 12-inch pizza and an 8-inch pizza. Most people guess that two small pizzas are
more food than one large one, since two 8s make 16, which is more than 12. But the large pizza wins.
When a shape is zoomed, its area is multiplied by the zoom factor twice over.

[Zooming and length](./scaling-lengths.mdx) showed that a zoom by $$r$$ multiplies every length by
$$r$$ (:ref{#zoom-rules}). In this guide we'll see that it multiplies every area by
$$r \cdot r = r^2$$: first for rectangles, then for triangles, and then, with the grid from
[Measuring area with a grid](./grids.mdx), for any shape at all.

## Rectangles first

Take a rectangle $$3$$ wide and $$2$$ tall, cut into $$6$$ unit squares. Zoom it by $$2$$ and it
becomes $$6$$ wide and $$4$$ tall. Each unit square has become a square of side $$2$$, which holds
$$2 \cdot 2 = 4$$ unit squares. So the area goes from $$6$$ to $$6 \cdot 4 = 24$$: it is multiplied
by $$4$$, not by $$2$$.

The doubling happens twice because a rectangle has two directions to grow in. Doubling the width
alone would double the area. Doubling the height as well doubles it again.

:::proposition[Zooming a rectangle]{#rectangle-scaling}

Let $$S$$ be a rectangle with sides of lengths $$a$$ and $$b$$, and let $$r > 0$$. The zoom of $$S$$
by $$r$$ is a rectangle with sides of lengths $$ra$$ and $$rb$$. So its area is

```math
(ra)(rb) = r^2 ab
```

which is $$r^2$$ times the area of $$S$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Similarity_(geometry)#Area_ratio_and_volume_ratio"}

A rectangle is made of four segments meeting at right angles (:ref{#distance-and-angles/rectangle}).
By :ref{#zoom-rules}, the zoom sends each segment to a segment $$r$$ times as long, and keeps every
angle. So the four corners go to four corners that are joined by segments of lengths $$ra$$, $$rb$$,
$$ra$$ and $$rb$$, meeting at right angles. That is a rectangle with sides $$ra$$ and $$rb$$. By
rule 1 of :ref{#distance-and-angles/area-rules}, its area is $$(ra)(rb) = r^2 ab$$.

:::

:::example[Enlarging a photo]

A photo is $$10$$ cm by $$15$$ cm, so it has area $$150$$ square centimetres. Enlarged by $$2$$, it
becomes $$20$$ cm by $$30$$ cm, with area $$600$$ square centimetres. The ink covers four times the
paper, so the print needs about four times the ink.

Shrinking works the same way. A zoom by $$\frac{1}{2}$$ gives a print $$5$$ cm by $$7.5$$ cm, with
area $$37.5$$: a quarter of the original, since $$\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{4}$$.

:::

The common mistake is to think that doubling the sides doubles the area. Doubling the sides
quadruples it, and tripling the sides multiplies it by $$9$$. Only the lengths go up by the scale
factor. The area goes up by the scale factor squared.

## Triangles

A triangle's area is half its base times its height (:ref{#distance-and-angles/triangle-area}). A
zoom by $$r$$ makes the base $$r$$ times as long. It also makes the height $$r$$ times as long,
since the height is a length too. So half the base times the height is multiplied by $$r$$ twice,
which gives $$r^2$$ again.

:::proposition[Zooming a triangle]{#triangle-scaling}

Let $$S$$ be a triangle and let $$r > 0$$. The zoom of $$S$$ by $$r$$ is a triangle whose area is
$$r^2$$ times the area of $$S$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Similarity_(geometry)#Area_ratio_and_volume_ratio"}

Let $$S$$ be the triangle $$\triangle ABC$$, with base $$AB$$ of length $$b$$. Let $$F$$ be the foot
of the perpendicular from $$C$$ to the line $$AB$$, so the height is $$h = d(C, F)$$
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/height}).

By :ref{#zoom-rules}, the zoom sends lines to lines and keeps angles. So $$rA$$, $$rB$$ and $$rC$$
are the corners of a triangle, the point $$rF$$ lies on the line through $$rA$$ and $$rB$$, and the
angle at $$rF$$ between that line and the segment to $$rC$$ is still a right angle. So $$rF$$ is the
foot of the perpendicular from $$rC$$, and the zoomed triangle has base and height

```math
d(rA, rB) = rb \qquad d(rC, rF) = rh
```

again by :ref{#zoom-rules}. Its area is $$\frac{1}{2}(rb)(rh) = r^2 \cdot \frac{1}{2}bh$$, which is
$$r^2$$ times the area of $$S$$.

:::

## Any shape at all

A pizza, a lake or a leaf has no base and height to multiply. The way in is the grid from the
[previous guide](./grids.mdx). Lay graph paper over the shape and count the squares that lie inside
it. Now zoom the shape and the graph paper together, as a photocopier would. Every little square
becomes a bigger square, with sides $$r$$ times as long and so $$r^2$$ times the area. The squares
that were inside the shape are still inside it, and there are exactly as many of them as before.

So the count of squares stays the same, but each square is worth $$r^2$$ times as much. Move the
slider below to see this with a shape made of three squares.

<ZoomSquares />

The proof makes the counting argument exact. It uses the inner and outer counts
(:ref{#grid-counts}): the inner count adds up the squares wholly inside the shape, and the outer
count adds up the squares that overlap it, meaning they share some area with it. The area always
lies between them (:ref{#grid-squeeze}), and the rule :ref{#grid-rule} says the gap between them can
be made as small as you like with a fine enough grid.

:::theorem[Zooming multiplies area by r squared]{#area-scaling}

Let $$S$$ be a shape whose area is pinned down by grids (:ref{#grid-rule}), and let $$r > 0$$. Then
the zoom $$rS$$ has area

```math
\text{area of } rS = r^2 \cdot (\text{area of } S)
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Similarity_(geometry)#Area_ratio_and_volume_ratio"}

Take the zoom about a point $$O$$. Lay a grid of squares of side $$c$$ over the plane, with $$O$$ at
one of its corners, and zoom the grid as well. By :ref{#rectangle-scaling}, each square of side
$$c$$ goes to a square of side $$rc$$, and these squares form the grid of side $$rc$$ with a corner
at $$O$$. Every square of the new grid comes from exactly one square of the old grid, since the zoom
by $$\frac{1}{r}$$ undoes the zoom by $$r$$ (:ref{#zoom-twice}).

**Inside goes to inside.** A square lies wholly inside $$S$$ exactly when its zoom lies wholly
inside $$rS$$, because the points of $$rS$$ are exactly the zooms of the points of $$S$$, and the
zoom by $$\frac{1}{r}$$ brings each point back.

**Overlapping goes to overlapping.** Suppose a square $$Q$$ overlaps $$S$$, so the part they have in
common has some area. The edge of that common part is made of pieces of the edges of $$Q$$ and
$$S$$, so it has a length. By :ref{#grid-rule}, a fine enough grid has a little square lying wholly
inside that common part, since otherwise its inner count would stay at $$0$$ and couldn't close in
on an area bigger than $$0$$. Inside goes to inside, so the zoom of that little square lies wholly
inside both the zoom of $$Q$$ and $$rS$$, and it is a square of side bigger than $$0$$. So the zoom
of $$Q$$ shares some area with $$rS$$: it overlaps. The zoom by $$\frac{1}{r}$$ gives the reverse.

So the two grids give the same numbers of squares in each count, and each square of the new grid has
$$r^2$$ times the area of its partner (:ref{#rectangle-scaling}). Writing $$i$$ and $$o$$ for the
inner and outer counts of $$S$$ with side $$c$$, the counts of $$rS$$ with side $$rc$$ are $$r^2 i$$
and $$r^2 o$$.

By :ref{#grid-squeeze}, applied to each shape,

```math
i \le \text{area of } S \le o \qquad r^2 i \le \text{area of } rS \le r^2 o
```

Multiplying the first line by $$r^2$$ gives $$r^2 i \le r^2 \cdot (\text{area of } S) \le r^2 o$$.
So the two numbers $$\text{area of } rS$$ and $$r^2 \cdot (\text{area of } S)$$ both lie between
$$r^2 i$$ and $$r^2 o$$, and they differ by at most $$r^2(o - i)$$.

By :ref{#grid-rule}, a fine enough grid makes $$o - i$$ as small as you like, and so makes
$$r^2(o - i)$$ as small as you like too. Two fixed numbers that differ by less than every positive
amount are equal. So the area of $$rS$$ is $$r^2$$ times the area of $$S$$.

:::

In words: **twice as wide means four times the area**, for any shape. Three times as wide means nine
times the area, and half as wide means a quarter of the area.

## Pizzas, maps and paper

The pizza question from the top of the page needs no formula for the area of a disc. An 8-inch pizza
zoomed by $$1.5$$ is a 12-inch pizza, since $$1.5 \cdot 8 = 12$$. So the large pizza has
$$1.5^2 = 2.25$$ times the area of a small one, while two small pizzas have only $$2$$ times. The
large pizza is $$12.5\%$$ more food, since $$2.25 \div 2 = 1.125$$.

<PizzaCompare caption="Drawn to the same scale: the 12-inch pizza is the 8-inch one zoomed by 1.5, so it holds 2.25 times as much." />

:::example[Areas on a map]

A walking map has scale $$1 : 50\,000$$: every length on the map is the real length zoomed by
$$\frac{1}{50\,000}$$. So $$1$$ cm on the map stands for $$50\,000$$ cm, which is $$500$$ metres.

Areas shrink by the square of that. A square $$1$$ cm on each side on the map stands for a square
$$500$$ metres on each side on the ground, with area $$500 \cdot 500 = 250\,000$$ square metres, or
a quarter of a square kilometre. A wood covering $$8$$ square centimetres of the map covers
$$8 \cdot 250\,000 = 2\,000\,000$$ square metres, which is $$2$$ square kilometres.

:::

Going backwards needs a square root. To double an area, zoom by the number whose square is $$2$$,
which is $$\sqrt{2} \approx 1.414$$. Paper sizes are built on this. An A3 sheet, $$297$$ mm by
$$420$$ mm, is an A4 sheet, $$210$$ mm by $$297$$ mm, zoomed by about $$1.414$$: you can check that
$$210 \cdot 1.414 \approx 297$$ and $$297 \cdot 1.414 \approx 420$$. So an A3 sheet has twice the
area of an A4 sheet, and a photocopier set to $$141\%$$ enlarges an A4 page to fill an A3 sheet
exactly.

:::example[Turf for a bigger lawn]

A rectangular lawn needs $$30$$ square metres of turf. A new lawn of the same shape is to be $$1.5$$
times as long and $$1.5$$ times as wide. It needs $$1.5^2 \cdot 30 = 2.25 \cdot 30 = 67.5$$ square
metres of turf, more than twice as much, even though no side has doubled.

:::

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on zooming and area, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-scaling-areas-factors}

A shape has area $$12$$. Find the area after a zoom by each factor:

1. $$3$$
2. $$\frac{1}{2}$$
3. $$1.5$$
4. $$\frac{2}{3}$$

:::solution

By :ref{#area-scaling}, the area is multiplied by the square of the factor:

1. $$3^2 \cdot 12 = 9 \cdot 12 = 108$$
2. $$\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^2 \cdot 12 = \frac{12}{4} = 3$$
3. $$1.5^2 \cdot 12 = 2.25 \cdot 12 = 27$$
4. $$\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^2 \cdot 12 = \frac{4}{9} \cdot 12 = \frac{48}{9} = \frac{16}{3}$$

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-scaling-areas-backwards}

A shape of area $$20$$ is zoomed, and the result has area $$45$$. What was the scale factor? What
factor would turn the same shape into one of area $$5$$?

:::solution

By :ref{#area-scaling}, $$r^2 \cdot 20 = 45$$, so $$r^2 = \frac{45}{20} = 2.25$$. The scale factor
is positive, so $$r = 1.5$$.

For area $$5$$, $$r^2 \cdot 20 = 5$$, so $$r^2 = \frac{1}{4}$$ and $$r = \frac{1}{2}$$. Halving
every length leaves a quarter of the area.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-scaling-areas-value}

A 10-inch pizza costs $$8$$ pounds and a 14-inch pizza costs $$15$$ pounds. Without working out
either area, decide which gives more pizza for each pound.

:::solution

The 14-inch pizza is the 10-inch one zoomed by $$\frac{14}{10} = 1.4$$. By :ref{#area-scaling}, it
has $$1.4^2 = 1.96$$ times the area. Its price is $$\frac{15}{8} = 1.875$$ times as much. You get
$$1.96$$ times the pizza for $$1.875$$ times the money, so the 14-inch pizza gives more for each
pound.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-scaling-areas-map}

A map has scale $$1 : 25\,000$$. A lake covers $$12$$ square centimetres of the map. What is its
real area, in square kilometres?

:::solution

Each length on the map is the real length zoomed by $$\frac{1}{25\,000}$$, so $$1$$ cm on the map is
$$25\,000$$ cm, which is $$250$$ metres. A map square $$1$$ cm on each side stands for a real square
$$250$$ metres on each side, with area $$250 \cdot 250 = 62\,500$$ square metres
(:ref{#area-scaling}).

The lake covers $$12 \cdot 62\,500 = 750\,000$$ square metres. A square kilometre is
$$1000 \cdot 1000 = 1\,000\,000$$ square metres, so the lake covers $$0.75$$ square kilometres.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-scaling-areas-twice}

A shape of area $$18$$ is zoomed by $$2$$ about a point $$O$$, and the result is then zoomed by
$$\frac{1}{3}$$ about $$O$$. What is the final area? Do it two ways: one zoom at a time, and as a
single zoom.

:::solution

**One zoom at a time.** The zoom by $$2$$ gives area $$4 \cdot 18 = 72$$, and the zoom by
$$\frac{1}{3}$$ then gives $$\frac{1}{9} \cdot 72 = 8$$ (:ref{#area-scaling}).

**As a single zoom.** By :ref{#zoom-twice}, the two zooms together are the zoom by
$$2 \cdot \frac{1}{3} = \frac{2}{3}$$. That multiplies area by $$\frac{4}{9}$$, and
$$\frac{4}{9} \cdot 18 = 8$$. The two ways agree, as they must.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-scaling-areas-square}

A square has side $$1$$, so its perimeter is $$4$$ and its area is $$1$$. It is zoomed by $$r$$.

1. Find the perimeter and the area of the zoomed square in terms of $$r$$.
2. For which $$r$$ are the two numbers equal?
3. A friend says "a shape whose perimeter equals its area is special". Explain why that depends on
   the unit of length chosen.

:::solution

1. Each side becomes $$r$$ (:ref{#zoom-rules}), so the perimeter is $$4r$$. The area is
   $$r^2 \cdot 1 = r^2$$ (:ref{#area-scaling}).
2. $$4r = r^2$$ with $$r > 0$$ gives $$r = 4$$: a square of side $$4$$ has perimeter $$16$$ and area
   $$16$$.
3. Measure the same square in a unit half as long. Its side becomes $$8$$, its perimeter $$32$$ and
   its area $$64$$. The perimeter doubled but the area went up four times, so they are no longer
   equal. A length and an area are different kinds of quantity, and comparing their numbers only
   makes sense once a unit is fixed.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-scaling-areas-paper}

An A4 sheet is $$210$$ mm by $$297$$ mm. An A5 sheet is an A4 sheet folded in half across its long
side, so it is $$148.5$$ mm by $$210$$ mm.

1. What fraction of the area of A4 is A5?
2. A5 has the same shape as A4. What zoom takes A4 to A5? Check it against the lengths.

:::solution

1. Folding in half halves the area, so A5 is $$\frac{1}{2}$$ of A4. In numbers,
   $$210 \cdot 297 = 62\,370$$ and $$148.5 \cdot 210 = 31\,185$$, which is half.
2. The zoom by $$r$$ multiplies area by $$r^2$$ (:ref{#area-scaling}), so $$r^2 = \frac{1}{2}$$ and
   $$r = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.707$$. Check: $$297 \cdot 0.707 \approx 210$$ and
   $$210 \cdot 0.707 \approx 148.5$$. The long side of A5 is the short side of A4, and the short
   side of A5 is half the long side of A4.

:::
