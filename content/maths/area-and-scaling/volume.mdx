---
title: Volume and the square-cube law
description: How a zoom changes volume and surface, and why size changes how things work.
---

Put a block of ice in one drink and the same amount of crushed ice in another. The crushed ice melts
far sooner. Breaking up the ice keeps its volume the same but gives it much more surface for the
warm drink to touch. In this guide we'll work out how surface and volume change when a thing is
zoomed, and use that to explain why giants can't stand, why small things are strong, and why a
bigger orange gives more fruit for its peel.

## Measuring space

Area counts how many unit squares it takes to cover a flat shape. Volume does the same job in space:
it counts how many unit cubes, such as sugar cubes of side $$1$$ cm, it takes to fill a solid. A box
$$3$$ cubes long, $$2$$ wide and $$4$$ high holds $$3 \cdot 2 = 6$$ cubes in each layer, and there
are $$4$$ layers, so it holds $$24$$ cubes.

The rules for volume are the rules for area with one more side. As with area, they are taken from
the picture of filling things with cubes rather than proved.

:::remark[Rules for volume]{#volume-rules}

1. **Boxes**: a box whose edges have lengths $$a$$, $$b$$ and $$c$$ has volume $$abc$$. In
   particular, a cube of side $$a$$ has volume $$a^3$$.
2. **Pieces add**: if a solid is cut into pieces that don't overlap, its volume is the total of the
   volumes of the pieces.
3. **Inside means no more**: a solid inside another has no more volume than it.
4. **Moving keeps volume**: sliding, turning or flipping a solid in space doesn't change its volume.
5. **Zooms in space**: a zoom by $$r$$ about a point in space multiplies every distance by $$r$$,
   sends flat faces to flat faces and keeps angles, as in the plane (:ref{#zoom-rules}).
6. **Little cubes pin down volume**: fill space with a grid of little cubes. The cubes lying wholly
   inside a solid, and the cubes that overlap it at all, have total volumes that get as close as you
   like to each other as the cubes get smaller, so they pin down its volume. This is the same idea
   as :ref{#grid-rule}, one side up.

These are taken as given.

::see-also{src="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volume"}

:::

The **surface area** of a solid is the total area of its outside: the area of the wrapping paper
that covers it exactly, with no overlap. A cube of side $$a$$ has $$6$$ square faces, each of area
$$a^2$$, so its surface area is $$6a^2$$. In what follows, "surface" means surface area.

## Zooming a box

Take a cube of side $$1$$ and build a cube of side $$2$$ from copies of it. The bigger cube needs
$$2$$ cubes along, $$2$$ across and $$2$$ up: $$8$$ in all. A cube of side $$3$$ needs
$$3 \cdot 3 \cdot 3 = 27$$. So doubling every length gives $$8$$ times the volume, and tripling
gives $$27$$ times. Meanwhile each face only grows $$4$$ times and then $$9$$ times, since a face is
a flat square (:ref{#area-scaling}).

<ThreeCubes caption="Cubes of side 1, 2 and 3. The surface grows 4 and then 9 times, but the volume grows 8 and then 27 times." />

:::proposition[Zooming a box]{#box-scaling}

A zoom by $$r$$ sends a box with edges $$a$$, $$b$$ and $$c$$ to a box with edges $$ra$$, $$rb$$ and
$$rc$$. So it multiplies the volume of a box by $$r^3$$:

```math
(ra)(rb)(rc) = r^3 \cdot abc
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Similarity_(geometry)#Area_ratio_and_volume_ratio"}

The faces of the box are flat rectangles meeting at right angles. A zoom sends flat faces to flat
faces and keeps angles (rule 5 of :ref{#volume-rules}), so the image is again a box. Every distance
is multiplied by $$r$$, so its edges have lengths $$ra$$, $$rb$$ and $$rc$$. By rule 1, its volume
is $$(ra)(rb)(rc) = r^3 \cdot abc$$, which is $$r^3$$ times the volume $$abc$$ of the box you
started with.

:::

:::example[A box, three times as big]

A shoebox is $$30$$ cm long, $$20$$ cm wide and $$10$$ cm high, so its volume is
$$30 \cdot 20 \cdot 10 = 6000$$ cm³. Zoom it by $$3$$ and it is $$90$$ by $$60$$ by $$30$$ cm, with
volume $$162\,000$$ cm³. That is $$27$$ times as much, and $$27 = 3^3$$.

:::

## Zooming any solid

A ball, a potato or a statue isn't a box. But it can be filled with little cubes, as a flat shape
can be covered with little squares in :ref{#grid-counts}. Zoom the solid and the grid of little
cubes together: each little cube becomes a bigger cube, with $$r^3$$ times the volume. The cubes
that were inside the solid are inside the zoomed solid, and the cubes that overlapped it overlap the
zoomed solid. So both counts are multiplied by $$r^3$$, and the volume caught between them is too.

:::theorem[Zooming multiplies volume by r cubed]{#volume-scaling}

Let $$S$$ be a solid and $$r > 0$$. The zoom by $$r$$ multiplies its volume by $$r^3$$:

```math
\text{volume of } rS = r^3 \cdot (\text{volume of } S)
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Similarity_(geometry)#Area_ratio_and_volume_ratio"}

Fill space with a grid of little cubes of side $$c$$. Let $$m$$ be the total volume of the cubes
lying wholly inside $$S$$, and $$M$$ the total volume of the cubes that overlap $$S$$ at all. The
cubes inside $$S$$ don't overlap and fill part of $$S$$, so by rules 2 and 3 of :ref{#volume-rules},
$$m \le \text{volume of } S$$. In the same way, $$S$$ lies inside the cubes that overlap it, so
$$\text{volume of } S \le M$$.

Now zoom everything by $$r$$. Each little cube becomes a cube of side $$rc$$, with $$r^3$$ times the
volume (:ref{#box-scaling}), and together they make a grid of cubes of side $$rc$$. A cube lies
wholly inside $$S$$ exactly when its image lies wholly inside $$rS$$, and a cube overlaps $$S$$
exactly when its image overlaps $$rS$$, since the zoom can be undone by the zoom by $$\frac{1}{r}$$
(:ref{#zoom-twice}). So, for the new grid, the two totals are $$r^3 m$$ and $$r^3 M$$, and

```math
r^3 m \le \text{volume of } rS \le r^3 M
```

Multiplying $$m \le \text{volume of } S \le M$$ by $$r^3$$ shows that $$r^3$$ times the volume of
$$S$$ lies between the same two numbers. As $$c$$ gets smaller, $$m$$ and $$M$$ get as close as you
like to each other (rule 6), and so do $$r^3 m$$ and $$r^3 M$$. Two fixed numbers that are both
caught between them must be equal.

:::

:::example[Balls]

A ball of radius $$1$$ has volume $$\frac{4}{3}\pi$$, about $$4.19$$. Proving this needs more than
cubes and zooms, so it is taken from the link below. A ball of radius $$r$$ is the ball of radius
$$1$$ zoomed by $$r$$ about its centre, so its volume is

```math
\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3
```

A football of radius $$11$$ cm holds $$11^3 = 1331$$ times as much air as a ball of radius $$1$$ cm:
about $$\frac{4}{3} \cdot 3.14 \cdot 1331 \approx 5570$$ cm³, or $$5.6$$ litres.

::see-also{src="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sphere"}

:::

## Zooming a surface

A surface is made of flat pieces, or can be cut into pieces so small that each is nearly flat, like
the panels of a football. A zoom stretches each piece the way it stretches any flat shape, so each
piece gains $$r^2$$ times its area. So surfaces follow the area rule, not the volume rule.

:::proposition[Surface area under a zoom]{#surface-scaling}

A zoom by $$r$$ multiplies the surface area of a solid by $$r^2$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Similarity_(geometry)#Area_ratio_and_volume_ratio"}

Take first a solid with flat faces, such as a box. The zoom sends each face to a flat shape with
every distance multiplied by $$r$$ (rule 5 of :ref{#volume-rules}). Unless the centre of the zoom
lies in the face's plane, that image sits in a different, parallel plane. But it is the face zoomed
by $$r$$ within its own plane and then moved into place, and moving doesn't change area. This step
is taken from the picture. So each face's area is multiplied by $$r^2$$ (:ref{#area-scaling}), and
so is their total.

A curved surface is approximated by small flat pieces, and its area is what their total area
approaches as the pieces get smaller. That is taken from the picture, as for the length of a curve
in :ref{#curve-length-scaling}. Each piece's area is multiplied by $$r^2$$, so the total is too.

:::

A ball of radius $$1$$ has surface area $$4\pi$$. This is also taken as given, from the link in the
example on balls. A ball of radius $$r$$ is that ball zoomed by $$r$$, so its surface area is
$$4\pi r^2$$.

## The square-cube law

Put the two facts side by side. Make a thing $$r$$ times as big in every direction, and its surface
is multiplied by $$r^2$$, while its volume is multiplied by $$r^3$$. Volume wins. A cube twice as
wide has $$4$$ times the wrapping but $$8$$ times the inside, so each unit of its inside has only
half as much wrapping as before.

<SquareCube />

The table below lists cubes of four sizes. The last column is the surface for each unit of volume,
and it falls as the cube grows.

| Side | Surface | Volume | Surface for each unit of volume |
| ---- | ------- | ------ | ------------------------------- |
| 1    | 6       | 1      | 6                               |
| 2    | 24      | 8      | 3                               |
| 3    | 54      | 27     | 2                               |
| 10   | 600     | 1000   | 0.6                             |

:::theorem[The square-cube law]{#square-cube}

Let a solid have surface area $$A$$ and volume $$V$$, and zoom it by $$r$$. Then the new solid has

- **surface area** $$r^2 A$$
- **volume** $$r^3 V$$
- **surface for each unit of volume** $$\frac{1}{r} \cdot \frac{A}{V}$$

So growing by $$r$$ divides the surface for each unit of volume by $$r$$, and shrinking does the
opposite.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square%E2%80%93cube_law"}

The surface area is $$r^2 A$$ by :ref{#surface-scaling}, and the volume is $$r^3 V$$ by
:ref{#volume-scaling}. Dividing one by the other,

```math
\frac{r^2 A}{r^3 V} = \frac{1}{r} \cdot \frac{A}{V}
```

since $$\frac{r^2}{r^3} = \frac{1}{r}$$.

:::

The same reasoning works for any two things you can measure on a shape. Anything that behaves like
an area, such as the cross-section of a bone or a muscle, grows by $$r^2$$. Anything that behaves
like a volume, such as weight, the amount of food inside or the heat stored, grows by $$r^3$$. The
rest of this page is that one idea, applied to the world.

## Why giants can't stand

A bone holds up the weight above it. How much it can hold depends on how thick it is: on the area of
its cross-section, the flat face you would see if you sawed through it. Double the area of the
cross-section and the bone can carry about twice the load. The load on each square centimetre of the
cross-section is called the **pressure**, and a bone breaks when the pressure gets too high.

Now zoom a person by $$10$$, to a giant about $$18$$ metres tall. The giant's weight comes from its
volume, so it is $$10^3 = 1000$$ times as much. The cross-section of each thigh bone is an area, so
it is only $$10^2 = 100$$ times as much. Each square centimetre of bone carries
$$\frac{1000}{100} = 10$$ times the load it carries in you, and the bones would break at the first
step.

<PillarLoad caption="A load on a pillar, and the same thing zoomed by 3. The load grows 27 times but the top of the pillar only 9 times, so each square centimetre carries 3 times as much." />

That is why large animals are not zoomed copies of small ones. An elephant's legs are thick pillars,
far thicker for its height than a deer's. To keep the load on each square centimetre the same, a
creature $$10$$ times as tall needs bones with $$1000$$ times the cross-section, which means bones
about $$\sqrt{1000} \approx 32$$ times as wide, not $$10$$ times.

## Why small things are strong

Run the zoom the other way. Shrink a person by $$10$$, so $$r = \frac{1}{10}$$. Their weight is
multiplied by $$\frac{1}{1000}$$, but the cross-section of their muscles, which decides how hard
they can pull, is multiplied only by $$\frac{1}{100}$$. So for its weight, the small person is
$$10$$ times as strong.

This is the geometry behind an ant carrying a leaf many times its own weight, or a flea leaping far
above its own height. Small animals also survive falls that would hurt a large one: the air pushes
against their surface, which is large for their weight, so they fall slowly, and the weight that
hits the ground is tiny. None of this needs special muscles: it comes from being small.

## Heat, ice and cooking

Heat comes in and goes out through a surface, and the heat stored is spread through a volume. So the
square-cube law decides how fast things warm up and cool down. Small things have a lot of surface
for their volume, so they heat up and cool down fast. Large things have little surface for their
volume, so they change temperature slowly.

:::example[Crushed ice melts faster]

An ice cube $$4$$ cm on a side has volume $$4^3 = 64$$ cm³ and surface $$6 \cdot 4^2 = 96$$ cm².
Crush it into $$64$$ cubes of side $$1$$ cm. The volume of ice is the same, $$64$$ cm³, by rule 2 of
:ref{#volume-rules}. But each small cube has surface $$6$$ cm², so together they have
$$64 \cdot 6 = 384$$ cm², which is $$4$$ times as much. The drink touches $$4$$ times as much ice,
so the ice takes in heat about $$4$$ times as fast, and melts sooner.

The factor $$4$$ is the square-cube law: each small cube is the big one zoomed by $$\frac{1}{4}$$,
so the surface for each unit of volume is multiplied by $$4$$.

:::

<CrushedIce caption="One 4 cm ice cube beside the same ice crushed into 64 cubes of 1 cm. Same volume, four times the surface." />

The same idea runs through warm-blooded animals. An animal makes heat throughout its body and loses
it through its skin. A mouse has a great deal of skin for its size, so it loses heat fast and must
eat a large share of its weight in food every day. An elephant has the opposite problem: it makes a
great deal of heat and has little skin to lose it through, This is one reason for its large, thin
ears: they add surface without adding much volume.

Cooking works the same way in reverse. A roast twice as wide as another has $$8$$ times the meat but
only $$4$$ times the outside for heat to come in through, and the middle is twice as far from the
surface. So a big roast or turkey takes far longer than a small one, and the outside can burn while
the middle is still cold. Cutting potatoes into small pieces adds surface, so they cook faster.

## Buying by size

Fruit, eggs and pizzas are priced by the item, but what you eat is the volume or the area. A small
change in width makes a large change in volume, so it pays to think in cubes.

:::example[Oranges]

One orange is $$10\%$$ wider than another, so it is the smaller one zoomed by $$r = 1.1$$. Its
volume is multiplied by

```math
1.1^3 = 1.331
```

so it has about $$33\%$$ more fruit. Its peel, a surface, is multiplied by $$1.1^2 = 1.21$$: only
$$21\%$$ more. Big oranges give more fruit for each gram of peel.

:::

The same holds for anything you pay for by the item. A melon $$20\%$$ wider has $$1.2^3 = 1.728$$
times the fruit, nearly three quarters more, for what looks like a small change.

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on volume and the square-cube law, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-volume-box}

A box is $$2$$ cm by $$3$$ cm by $$4$$ cm. Find its volume and its surface area. Then zoom it by
$$3$$, and find the new volume and surface area in two ways: from the new edge lengths, and from
:ref{#box-scaling} and :ref{#surface-scaling}.

:::solution

The volume is $$2 \cdot 3 \cdot 4 = 24$$ cm³. The box has two faces of each kind, $$2 \times 3$$,
$$2 \times 4$$ and $$3 \times 4$$, so its surface area is

```math
2(6 + 8 + 12) = 52 \text{ cm}^2
```

After the zoom, the edges are $$6$$, $$9$$ and $$12$$ cm. The volume is $$6 \cdot 9 \cdot 12 = 648$$
cm³, and the surface area is

```math
2(54 + 72 + 108) = 468 \text{ cm}^2
```

From the rules: $$3^3 \cdot 24 = 27 \cdot 24 = 648$$ and $$3^2 \cdot 52 = 9 \cdot 52 = 468$$. Both
ways agree.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-volume-model-train}

A model railway is built at a scale of $$1 : 76$$, so every length on the model is $$\frac{1}{76}$$
of the real length. A real locomotive weighs $$120$$ tonnes ($$120\,000$$ kg). If the model were
solid and made of the same materials, how much would it weigh? The real locomotive needs $$40$$
litres of paint; how much would the model need?

:::solution

The model is the locomotive zoomed by $$r = \frac{1}{76}$$. Weight goes with volume, which is
multiplied by $$r^3$$ (:ref{#volume-scaling}). Since $$76^3 = 438\,976$$,

```math
\frac{120\,000}{438\,976} \approx 0.27 \text{ kg}
```

so the model would weigh about $$270$$ grams. Paint goes with surface, which is multiplied by
$$r^2$$ (:ref{#surface-scaling}). Since $$76^2 = 5776$$, the model needs
$$\frac{40}{5776} \approx 0.0069$$ litres, about $$7$$ millilitres.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-volume-double}

A cube is zoomed so that its volume doubles. About how many times longer is each edge, and how many
times larger is the surface area?

:::solution

If the zoom is by $$r$$, the volume is multiplied by $$r^3$$ (:ref{#volume-scaling}), so
$$r^3 = 2$$. Trying values, $$1.26^3 \approx 2.000$$, so each edge is about $$1.26$$ times as long,
a little over a quarter longer.

The surface is multiplied by $$r^2 \approx 1.26^2 \approx 1.59$$ (:ref{#surface-scaling}). So
doubling the volume adds only about $$59\%$$ to the surface.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-volume-oranges}

A shop sells oranges $$8$$ cm wide for $$50$$p each and oranges $$10$$ cm wide for $$90$$p each.
Treat each orange as a ball. Which gives more fruit for your money?

:::solution

The big orange is the small one zoomed by $$r = \frac{10}{8} = 1.25$$, so it has

```math
1.25^3 \approx 1.95
```

times the fruit (:ref{#volume-scaling}). It costs $$\frac{90}{50} = 1.8$$ times as much. Since
$$1.95$$ is more than $$1.8$$, the big oranges give more fruit for each penny, by about
$$\frac{1.95}{1.8} \approx 1.085$$: around $$8$$ or $$9\%$$ more.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-volume-ice}

An ice cube $$3$$ cm on a side is crushed into cubes of side $$1$$ cm. By how many times does the
surface grow? What if it is crushed into cubes of side $$\frac{1}{2}$$ cm instead?

:::solution

The big cube has surface $$6 \cdot 3^2 = 54$$ cm² and volume $$27$$ cm³. Cubes of side $$1$$ cm each
have volume $$1$$, so there are $$27$$ of them (rule 2 of :ref{#volume-rules}), with surface
$$27 \cdot 6 = 162$$ cm². That is $$3$$ times as much.

Cubes of side $$\frac{1}{2}$$ cm each have volume $$\frac{1}{8}$$, so there are $$27 \cdot 8 = 216$$
of them. Each has surface $$6 \cdot \frac{1}{4} = \frac{3}{2}$$, so together they have
$$216 \cdot \frac{3}{2} = 324$$ cm², which is $$6$$ times as much. In each case the factor is the
big side divided by the small side, as :ref{#square-cube} says.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-volume-giant-bones}

A giant is a person zoomed by $$10$$, except that the giant's thigh bones are made thicker so that
each square centimetre of bone carries the same load as in an ordinary person. By how much must the
area of the bone's cross-section grow? By how much must its width grow?

:::solution

The giant's weight is a volume, so it is $$10^3 = 1000$$ times as much (:ref{#volume-scaling}). For
the load on each square centimetre to stay the same, the cross-section must carry $$1000$$ times the
load with the same pressure, so its area must be $$1000$$ times as much.

The cross-section is a flat shape. If its width is multiplied by $$w$$, keeping its shape, its area
is multiplied by $$w^2$$ (:ref{#area-scaling}). So $$w^2 = 1000$$ and
$$w = \sqrt{1000} \approx 31.6$$. The bone must be about $$32$$ times as wide, though the giant is
only $$10$$ times as tall: thick, elephant-like legs.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-volume-balloon}

A balloon of radius $$10$$ cm is blown up to radius $$20$$ cm. How many times as much air does it
hold? How many times as much rubber surface is there? Since the amount of rubber doesn't change,
what happens to its thickness?

:::solution

The bigger balloon is the smaller one zoomed by $$2$$. Its volume is $$2^3 = 8$$ times as much
(:ref{#volume-scaling}), so it holds $$8$$ times the air. Its surface area is $$2^2 = 4$$ times as
much (:ref{#surface-scaling}).

The same rubber is now spread over $$4$$ times the area. The volume of rubber is its area times its
thickness, roughly, for a thin sheet, so the thickness falls to about $$\frac{1}{4}$$ of what it
was. That is why a balloon gets more fragile as it grows.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-volume-ratio}

Show that a cube of side $$s$$ has $$\frac{6}{s}$$ units of surface for each unit of volume. Use
this to check :ref{#square-cube} for a cube zoomed by $$r$$.

:::solution

A cube of side $$s$$ has $$6$$ faces of area $$s^2$$, so surface $$6s^2$$, and volume $$s^3$$
(:ref{#volume-rules}). The surface for each unit of volume is

```math
\frac{6s^2}{s^3} = \frac{6}{s}
```

Zooming by $$r$$ gives a cube of side $$rs$$, with $$\frac{6}{rs} = \frac{1}{r} \cdot \frac{6}{s}$$.
That is the old amount multiplied by $$\frac{1}{r}$$, as :ref{#square-cube} says.

:::
