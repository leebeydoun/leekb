Imagine a ship crossing a calm sea on a steady course at a steady speed. Every hour the navigator
writes its position in the log, in kilometres east and north of a lighthouse: $$(1, 2)$$ at noon,
$$(4, 3)$$ at one o'clock and $$(7, 4)$$ at two. Each entry is the one before plus the same step
$$(3, 1)$$. Mark the entries on a chart and they fall on one straight line, and so does every place
the ship passed between entries, or before the first one.

So a straight track is a starting point and a step, repeated any number of times, forwards or
backwards, whole or in part. Mathematicians describe a line in the same way, as a point plus every
multiple of a direction. In this guide we'll write lines like this, and use the formula to find
where two lines meet and whether two ships collide.

## A point and a direction

The log gives the ship's position for whole hours, but the same rule works for any time. Write
$$P = (1, 2)$$ for the noon position and $$A = (3, 1)$$ for the step the ship makes each hour. Then
$$t$$ hours after noon the ship is at $$P + tA$$. At half past twelve, $$t = \frac{1}{2}$$ and the
ship is at $$(1, 2) + (1.5, 0.5) = (2.5, 2.5)$$. At eleven o'clock, $$t = -1$$ and it was at
$$(1, 2) - (3, 1) = (-2, 1)$$.


Letting $$t$$ run through every real number, positive, negative or zero, gives the whole track. That
is the definition of a line in $$\mathbb{R}^2$$.

:::definition[Line in coordinates]{#line-formula}

Let $$P$$ be a point and $$A$$ a point other than $$O$$. The **line through** $$P$$ **with
direction** $$A$$ is the set of all points

```math
P + tA
```

for every real number $$t$$, positive, negative or zero. The number $$t$$ is called the
**parameter**, and a description of a line in this form is called **parametric**.

:::

As a set (:ref{#basic-proofs/set}), the line is written

```math
\{P + tA : t \in \mathbb{R}\}
```

read "all points $$P + tA$$ with $$t$$ a real number". The part before the colon is a recipe for the
elements, and the part after it says which numbers to feed in. So a point $$X$$ lies on the line
exactly when $$X = P + tA$$ for some number $$t$$.

Each point of the line comes from only one parameter. If $$P + tA = P + sA$$, then taking $$P$$ from
both sides gives $$tA = sA$$, and since $$A \ne O$$, :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/scale-cancel}
gives $$t = s$$. For the ship, that says it is at each point of its track at one time only.

The direction must not be $$O$$, since $$P + tO = P$$ for every $$t$$, and a ship whose step is
$$O$$ stays where it is.

In the picture, the points $$P + tA$$ lie on the straight line through $$P$$ and $$P + A$$, as the
ship's log shows. From now on, in $$\mathbb{R}^2$$ this formula is what a line is. The guide
[The rules for lines, proved](./rules-proved.mdx) checks that lines defined this way obey the rules
the picture gave, starting with :ref{#line-rule-two-points}.

## One line, many descriptions

The navigator could log the position every half hour instead, adding the step $$(1.5, 0.5)$$ each
time. Or they could start counting time from one o'clock, at $$(4, 3)$$. Either way the ship sails
the same track, so the same line has many descriptions. Any point of it can be the starting point,
and any non-zero multiple of the step can be the direction.

:::proposition[Same line, other descriptions]{#line-descriptions}

Let $$L$$ be the line through $$P$$ with direction $$A$$.

1. **Other directions**: for every number $$c \ne 0$$, the line through $$P$$ with direction $$cA$$
   is $$L$$
2. **Other starting points**: for every point $$P'$$ of $$L$$, the line through $$P'$$ with
   direction $$A$$ is $$L$$
3. **No other directions**: if $$L$$ is also the line through some point $$P'$$ with direction
   $$B$$, then $$B = cA$$ for some number $$c \ne 0$$

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_(geometry)#Parametric_equation"}

Two sets are equal when each is a subset of the other (:ref{#basic-proofs/set-equality}), so for
parts 1 and 2 we show that every point of each line lies on the other.

**Part 1.** Write $$K$$ for the line through $$P$$ with direction $$cA$$. A point of $$K$$ is
$$P + t(cA)$$ for some $$t$$, and by :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/scale-twice} that is
$$P + (tc)A$$, a point of $$L$$ with parameter $$tc$$. A point of $$L$$ is $$P + tA$$ for some
$$t$$. Since $$c \ne 0$$, the number $$\frac{t}{c}$$ exists, and

```math
P + tA = P + \left(\tfrac{t}{c}\,c\right)A = P + \tfrac{t}{c}(cA)
```

again by :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/scale-twice}. That is a point of $$K$$ with parameter
$$\frac{t}{c}$$.

**Part 2.** Since $$P'$$ is on $$L$$, $$P' = P + uA$$ for some number $$u$$. Write $$K$$ for the
line through $$P'$$ with direction $$A$$. By :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/sum-rules} and
:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/scale-sum-rules}, a point of $$K$$ is

```math
P' + tA = P + uA + tA = P + (u + t)A
```

a point of $$L$$. In the other direction, a point of $$L$$ is

```math
P + tA = P + uA + (t - u)A = P' + (t - u)A
```

a point of $$K$$.

**Part 3.** The points $$P'$$ and $$P' + B$$ lie on the line through $$P'$$ with direction $$B$$,
with parameters $$0$$ and $$1$$. That line is $$L$$, so $$P' = P + uA$$ and $$P' + B = P + vA$$ for
some numbers $$u$$ and $$v$$. Subtracting, with the same rules,

```math
B = (P + vA) - (P + uA) = vA - uA = (v - u)A
```

If $$v - u$$ were $$0$$, this would give $$B = 0A = O$$, but a direction is never $$O$$. So
$$B = cA$$ with $$c = v - u \ne 0$$.

:::

For the ship, the line through $$(4, 3)$$ with direction $$(6, 2) = 2A$$ is the same track. Its
point with parameter $$\frac{1}{2}$$ is $$(4, 3) + (3, 1) = (7, 4)$$, the two o'clock entry. So is
the line through $$(4, 3)$$ with direction $$(-3, -1) = -A$$, which runs along the track backwards:
its point with parameter $$-1$$ is $$(4, 3) + (3, 1) = (7, 4)$$ again.

## The line through two points

Two entries in the log are enough to recover the whole track. The step from one entry to the next is
the hourly step, and every other position is the first entry plus some multiple of it. In the same
way, two different points $$P$$ and $$Q$$ fix a line: start at $$P$$ and use the step $$Q - P$$ as
the direction.

:::proposition[The line through two points]{#two-point-line}

Let $$P \ne Q$$. The line through $$P$$ with direction $$Q - P$$ contains $$P$$ and $$Q$$, and it is
the only line that contains both.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_(geometry)#Vectors"}

Since $$P \ne Q$$, the step $$Q - P$$ is not $$O$$, so it can be a direction. Write $$L$$ for the
line through $$P$$ with direction $$Q - P$$. Its point with parameter $$0$$ is $$P$$. Its point with
parameter $$1$$ is $$P + (Q - P) = Q$$, by :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/difference-step}.

Now let $$K$$ be any line through $$P$$ and $$Q$$, say the line through $$M$$ with direction $$B$$.
Then $$P = M + uB$$ and $$Q = M + vB$$ for some numbers $$u$$ and $$v$$, so

```math
Q - P = (M + vB) - (M + uB) = (v - u)B
```

by :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/sum-rules} and :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/scale-sum-rules}.
If $$v - u$$ were $$0$$, this would give $$Q - P = O$$ and $$Q = P$$. So $$Q - P = cB$$ with
$$c = v - u \ne 0$$. By part 1 of :ref{#line-descriptions}, $$K$$ is the line through $$M$$ with
direction $$Q - P$$. The point $$P$$ is on $$K$$, so by part 2, $$K$$ is the line through $$P$$ with
direction $$Q - P$$, which is $$L$$.

:::

:::example[The line through two points]

Take $$P = (-1, 4)$$ and $$Q = (2, -2)$$. The step is $$Q - P = (3, -6)$$, so the line is

```math
(-1, 4) + t(3, -6) = (-1 + 3t,\ 4 - 6t)
```

The point $$(5, -8)$$ is on it, with $$t = 2$$: the first coordinate gives $$-1 + 3t = 5$$, so
$$t = 2$$, and then $$4 - 6 \cdot 2 = -8$$ matches the second. By part 1 of
:ref{#line-descriptions}, the direction $$\frac{1}{3}(3, -6) = (1, -2)$$ gives the same line, with
smaller numbers.

:::

Since the line containing $$P$$ and $$Q$$ is unique, we call it the **line** $$PQ$$, as in
:ref{#distance-and-angles/line-rules}. For every $$t$$ between $$0$$ and $$1$$, the point
$$P + t(Q - P)$$ is on the segment $$PQ$$ of :ref{#segment-formula}. So the line $$PQ$$ is the
segment $$PQ$$ carried on past both ends.

## Multiples of a point

The guide [Scaling points](../adding-and-scaling-points/scaling-points.mdx) plotted the multiples
$$3A$$, $$\frac{1}{2}A$$, $$-A$$ and $$-2A$$ of $$A = (2, 1)$$, and they all looked to lie on one
line through $$O$$ and $$A$$. Proving it had to wait for lines in coordinates. Now it takes one line
of algebra: the multiples of $$A$$ are $$O$$ plus multiples of $$A$$, which is the line through
$$O$$ with direction $$A$$.

:::proposition[Multiples lie on a line]{#multiples-line}

Let $$A \ne O$$. The multiples $$tA$$, for all real numbers $$t$$, are exactly the points of the
line through $$O$$ and $$A$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_(geometry)#Vectors"}

By :ref{#two-point-line}, with $$O$$ and $$A$$ in place of $$P$$ and $$Q$$, the only line through
$$O$$ and $$A$$ is the line through $$O$$ with direction $$A - O = A$$. Its points are
$$O + tA = tA$$, by :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/sum-rules}.

:::

## Parallel lines

Picture a second ship sailing $$2$$ km north of the first, on the same heading and at the same
speed. Its track runs alongside the first and never meets it. A ship on that second track sailing
the opposite way, or twice as fast, still keeps to a track alongside the first. What the two tracks
share is their direction, up to a non-zero multiple.

:::definition[Parallel lines in coordinates]{#parallel-formula}

Lines $$L$$ and $$K$$ with directions $$A$$ and $$B$$ are **parallel**, written $$L \parallel K$$,
when $$B = cA$$ for some number $$c \ne 0$$.

In the same way, the segments $$PQ$$ and $$MN$$, or the arrows $$\overrightarrow{PQ}$$ and
$$\overrightarrow{MN}$$, are **parallel** when $$N - M = c(Q - P)$$ for some number $$c \ne 0$$.

:::

A line has many directions, so the definition needs a check: the answer must not depend on which
direction we use for each line. By part 3 of :ref{#line-descriptions}, every direction of $$L$$ is a
non-zero multiple of $$A$$, and every direction of $$K$$ is a non-zero multiple of $$B$$. A non-zero
multiple of a non-zero multiple of $$A$$ is again a non-zero multiple of $$A$$, by
:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/scale-twice}, so any choice of directions gives the same answer.

Taking $$c = 1$$ shows that every line is parallel to itself, as in the
[Lines and rays](../distance-and-angles/lines.mdx) guide. With $$c > 0$$ the two directions are the
same direction (:ref{#same-direction}), and with $$c < 0$$ they are opposite, as for the second ship
sailing the other way.

The parallelogram of :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/parallelogram-law} has equal opposite sides.
Its name also promises that the opposite sides are parallel, and with the definition in hand we can
check it. The side from $$B$$ to $$A + B$$ is the step $$A$$ taken from $$B$$, the same step as the
side from $$O$$ to $$A$$.

:::proposition[The sides of the parallelogram]{#parallelogram-parallel}

Let $$A$$ and $$B$$ be points other than $$O$$. In the four-sided shape with corners $$O$$, $$A$$,
$$A + B$$ and $$B$$, the side $$OA$$ is parallel to the side from $$B$$ to $$A + B$$, and the side
$$OB$$ is parallel to the side from $$A$$ to $$A + B$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallelogram#Characterizations"}

By :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/sum-rules}, $$(A + B) - B = A = A - O$$ and
$$(A + B) - A = B = B - O$$. So each pair of opposite sides has the same step, and they are parallel
by :ref{#parallel-formula} with $$c = 1$$.

:::

## Where two lines meet

Two tracks on a chart cross where one point lies on both. A point of the first line is $$P + tA$$
for some $$t$$, and a point of the second is $$Q + sB$$ for some $$s$$. So finding where they meet
means solving $$P + tA = Q + sB$$, which is two equations, one for each coordinate, in the two
unknowns $$t$$ and $$s$$. The Linear equations chapter showed that the determinant of such a system
decides how many solutions it has.

For these equations the determinant turns out to be $$a_1b_2 - a_2b_1$$, built from the two
directions alone. The first step is to see that this number is zero exactly when the lines are
parallel.

:::lemma[The parallel test]{#parallel-determinant}

Let $$A = (a_1, a_2)$$ and $$B = (b_1, b_2)$$ be points other than $$O$$. Then $$B = cA$$ for some
number $$c \ne 0$$ exactly when

```math
a_1b_2 - a_2b_1 = 0
```

So lines with directions $$A$$ and $$B$$ are parallel exactly when $$a_1b_2 - a_2b_1 = 0$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_independence#Alternative_method_using_determinants"}

If $$B = cA$$, then $$b_1 = ca_1$$ and $$b_2 = ca_2$$, so

```math
a_1b_2 - a_2b_1 = a_1(ca_2) - a_2(ca_1) = 0
```

Now suppose $$a_1b_2 = a_2b_1$$. Since $$A \ne O$$, at least one of $$a_1$$ and $$a_2$$ is not zero.

**If $$a_1 \ne 0$$**, let $$c = \frac{b_1}{a_1}$$, so $$b_1 = ca_1$$. Dividing $$a_1b_2 = a_2b_1$$
by $$a_1$$ gives $$b_2 = a_2 \frac{b_1}{a_1} = ca_2$$.

**If $$a_1 = 0$$**, then $$a_2 \ne 0$$. Let $$c = \frac{b_2}{a_2}$$, so $$b_2 = ca_2$$. Now
$$a_2b_1 = a_1b_2 = 0$$, so $$b_1 = 0$$ by :ref{#numbers/zero-product}, and $$b_1 = ca_1$$ holds as
well, since $$ca_1 = 0$$.

Either way $$B = cA$$. Finally $$c \ne 0$$, since $$c = 0$$ would give $$B = 0A = O$$.

:::

:::theorem[Where two lines meet]{#line-intersection}

Let $$L$$ be the line through $$P$$ with direction $$A$$, and $$K$$ the line through $$Q$$ with
direction $$B$$.

1. If $$L$$ and $$K$$ are not parallel, they meet in exactly one point.
2. If $$L$$ and $$K$$ are parallel, they are either the same line or never meet.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line%E2%80%93line_intersection#Given_two_points_on_each_line"}

A point $$X$$ lies on both lines exactly when $$X = P + tA$$ and $$X = Q + sB$$ for some numbers
$$t$$ and $$s$$.

**Part 1.** Written out in coordinates, $$P + tA = Q + sB$$ says $$p_1 + ta_1 = q_1 + sb_1$$ and
$$p_2 + ta_2 = q_2 + sb_2$$. Rearranged, that is the system

```math
\begin{aligned}
a_1t - b_1s &= q_1 - p_1 \\
a_2t - b_2s &= q_2 - p_2
\end{aligned}
```

in the unknowns $$t$$ and $$s$$. By :ref{#linear-equations/determinant}, its determinant is
$$a_1(-b_2) - (-b_1)a_2 = -(a_1b_2 - a_2b_1)$$. The lines are not parallel, so by
:ref{#parallel-determinant} this is not zero. By :ref{#linear-equations/determinant-test}, the
system has exactly one solution $$(t, s)$$, so the lines meet, at $$X = P + tA$$.

They meet nowhere else. Any meeting point $$X'$$ is $$P + t'A = Q + s'B$$ for some solution
$$(t', s')$$. There is only one solution, so $$t' = t$$ and $$X' = X$$.

**Part 2.** Now $$B = cA$$ for some $$c \ne 0$$. Suppose the lines share a point $$X$$. By part 2 of
:ref{#line-descriptions}, $$L$$ is the line through $$X$$ with direction $$A$$, and $$K$$ is the
line through $$X$$ with direction $$cA$$. By part 1, that is the line through $$X$$ with direction
$$A$$ as well. So $$K = L$$. If the lines share no point, they never meet.

:::

In part 2 the determinant is zero, and :ref{#linear-equations/determinant-zero} agrees: the system
has no solution when the lines never meet, and infinitely many when they are the same line.

:::example[Two tracks that cross]

Take the ship's track, through $$P = (1, 2)$$ with direction $$A = (3, 1)$$, and a second track
through $$Q = (2, -1)$$ with direction $$B = (1, 2)$$. The test number is
$$a_1b_2 - a_2b_1 = 3 \cdot 2 - 1 \cdot 1 = 5$$, which is not zero, so the tracks cross once. To
find where, solve $$(1 + 3t, 2 + t) = (2 + s, -1 + 2s)$$:

```math
1 + 3t = 2 + s \qquad 2 + t = -1 + 2s
```

The first equation gives $$s = 3t - 1$$. Putting that into the second gives $$2 + t = -1 + 6t - 2$$,
so $$5t = 5$$ and $$t = 1$$, and then $$s = 2$$. The meeting point is $$P + A = (4, 3)$$. As a
check, $$Q + 2B = (2, -1) + (2, 4) = (4, 3)$$.

:::

For the line $$K$$ through $$O$$ with direction $$(-6, -2)$$, drawn in
[Parallel lines](#parallel-lines), the test number with $$A = (3, 1)$$ is
$$3 \cdot (-2) - 1 \cdot (-6) = 0$$. So $$K$$ is parallel to the ship's track. It contains $$O$$,
and the ship's track doesn't: $$1 + 3t = 0$$ needs $$t = -\frac{1}{3}$$, and then the second
coordinate is $$2 - \frac{1}{3} = \frac{5}{3}$$, not $$0$$. So the two lines never meet.

The [Lines and rays](../distance-and-angles/lines.mdx) guide called two different lines parallel
when they never meet. By :ref{#line-intersection}, in $$\mathbb{R}^2$$ two different lines never
meet exactly when they are parallel in the sense of :ref{#parallel-formula}. So the two meanings of
parallel agree, and the theorem is the coordinate form of :ref{#distance-and-angles/crossing-lines}.

## Crossing the axes

The $$x$$-axis is the set of points whose second coordinate is $$0$$, and the $$y$$-axis is the set
of points whose first coordinate is $$0$$. So to find where a line crosses an axis, set one
coordinate of $$P + tA$$ to $$0$$, solve for $$t$$, and put that $$t$$ back in.

:::example[Where the ship's track crosses the axes]

The ship's track is $$(1 + 3t, 2 + t)$$. Its second coordinate is $$0$$ when $$t = -2$$, which gives
the point $$(1 - 6, 0) = (-5, 0)$$ on the $$x$$-axis. So the ship crossed the east to west line
through the lighthouse at ten o'clock, $$5$$ km west of it.

Its first coordinate is $$0$$ when $$t = -\frac{1}{3}$$, which gives
$$\left(0, 2 - \frac{1}{3}\right) = \left(0, \frac{5}{3}\right)$$ on the $$y$$-axis. That was $$20$$
minutes before noon, about $$1.67$$ km north of the lighthouse.

:::

Some lines miss an axis. If the direction has $$a_2 = 0$$, the second coordinate $$p_2 + ta_2$$ is
$$p_2$$ for every $$t$$, so the line is horizontal. It never crosses the $$x$$-axis, unless
$$p_2 = 0$$ and it is the $$x$$-axis. In the same way, a line whose direction has $$a_1 = 0$$ is
vertical, and it meets the $$y$$-axis exactly when it is the $$y$$-axis.

## Crossing paths and collisions

Read $$t$$ as a time, as in the ship's log. Then $$P + tA$$ is the position of something moving at a
steady speed along a line. Between the times $$t$$ and $$t'$$ it moves from $$P + tA$$ to
$$P + t'A$$, and by :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/distance-norm} and
:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/norm-scale} the distance between those points is

```math
\big|(t' - t)A\big| = |t' - t|\,|A|
```

So each hour it moves $$|A|$$: its **speed** is the length of its direction. The ship with step
$$(3, 1)$$ sails at $$\sqrt{10} \approx 3.16$$ km per hour.

Now put two ships on the sea at once, the first at $$P + tA$$ and the second at $$Q + tB$$, with the
same clock $$t$$ for both. Their tracks cross where $$P + tA = Q + sB$$, allowing different
parameters. But the ships collide exactly when they are at the same place at the same time, and that
means one number $$t$$ with

```math
P + tA = Q + tB
```

If the tracks are not parallel, they cross at one point, and each ship passes it at one time. So the
ships collide exactly when those two times are the same.

:::example[Crossing tracks, no collision]

The first ship is at $$(1, 2) + t(3, 1)$$ and the second at $$(2, -1) + t(1, 2)$$, with $$t$$ in
hours after noon. These are the tracks of [Where two lines meet](#where-two-lines-meet), which cross
at $$(4, 3)$$ with $$t = 1$$ for the first and $$s = 2$$ for the second. So the first ship passes
the crossing at one o'clock and the second at two, and they don't collide.

At one o'clock the second ship is at $$(3, 1)$$, so the gap between them is
$$|(4, 3) - (3, 1)| = |(1, 2)| = \sqrt{5} \approx 2.24$$ km. In general the gap at time $$t$$ is the
length of

```math
(1 + 3t, 2 + t) - (2 + t, -1 + 2t) = (-1 + 2t, 3 - t)
```

and its square is $$(-1 + 2t)^2 + (3 - t)^2 = 5t^2 - 10t + 10 = 5(t - 1)^2 + 5$$, completing the
square (:ref{#quadratics/complete-square}). This is smallest when $$t = 1$$. So the ships are never
closer than $$\sqrt{5}$$ km, and they are that close at one o'clock.

:::

:::example[A collision]

Keep the first ship, and give the second the same velocity $$(1, 2)$$ but a noon position of
$$(3, 1)$$. The ships collide when $$(1 + 3t, 2 + t) = (3 + t, 1 + 2t)$$. The first coordinates give
$$2t = 2$$ and the second give $$1 = t$$, and both say $$t = 1$$. So at one o'clock both ships are
at $$(4, 3)$$: they collide.

:::

On a ship's radar, the officer of the watch tracks each nearby vessel in this way. The smallest gap
between two ships is called the **closest point of approach**, and the first example found it for
the two ships there. A crossing of tracks alone is no danger: what matters is the gap at the times
the ships are near the crossing.

::::exercise{#ex-lines-points}

Let $$L$$ be the line through $$(-2, 5)$$ with direction $$(4, -3)$$.

1. Find the points of $$L$$ with parameters $$t = 0$$, $$2$$, $$-1$$ and $$\frac{1}{2}$$.
2. Is $$(10, -4)$$ on $$L$$? Is $$(4, 0)$$?

:::solution

The points of $$L$$ are $$(-2 + 4t, 5 - 3t)$$ (:ref{#line-formula}).

1. $$t = 0$$ gives $$(-2, 5)$$, $$t = 2$$ gives $$(6, -1)$$, $$t = -1$$ gives $$(-6, 8)$$ and
   $$t = \frac{1}{2}$$ gives $$\left(0, \frac{7}{2}\right)$$.
2. For $$(10, -4)$$, the first coordinate needs $$-2 + 4t = 10$$, so $$t = 3$$. Then the second
   coordinate is $$5 - 9 = -4$$, which matches, so $$(10, -4)$$ is on $$L$$. For $$(4, 0)$$, the
   first coordinate needs $$t = \frac{3}{2}$$, and then the second is
   $$5 - \frac{9}{2} = \frac{1}{2}$$, not $$0$$. Each point of $$L$$ has only one parameter, so no
   other $$t$$ could work, and $$(4, 0)$$ is not on $$L$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-lines-two-points}

Write the line through $$(3, -1)$$ and $$(-1, 1)$$ in parametric form. Find where it crosses each
axis, and check that $$(5, -2)$$ is on it.

:::solution

By :ref{#two-point-line}, the direction is $$(-1, 1) - (3, -1) = (-4, 2)$$, so the line is

```math
(3, -1) + t(-4, 2) = (3 - 4t,\ -1 + 2t)
```

The second coordinate is $$0$$ when $$t = \frac{1}{2}$$, giving $$(1, 0)$$ on the $$x$$-axis. The
first coordinate is $$0$$ when $$t = \frac{3}{4}$$, giving $$\left(0, \frac{1}{2}\right)$$ on the
$$y$$-axis. Finally $$t = -\frac{1}{2}$$ gives $$(3 + 2, -1 - 1) = (5, -2)$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-lines-same}

Show that the line through $$(1, 0)$$ with direction $$(2, -1)$$ and the line through $$(-3, 2)$$
with direction $$(-6, 3)$$ are the same line.

:::solution

The directions satisfy $$(-6, 3) = -3(2, -1)$$, so by part 1 of :ref{#line-descriptions} the second
line is also the line through $$(-3, 2)$$ with direction $$(2, -1)$$. Now $$(-3, 2)$$ is on the
first line: $$(1, 0) + t(2, -1) = (-3, 2)$$ when $$t = -2$$, since $$(1 - 4, 0 + 2) = (-3, 2)$$. So
by part 2 of :ref{#line-descriptions}, the line through $$(-3, 2)$$ with direction $$(2, -1)$$ is
the first line.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-lines-meet}

Let $$L$$ be the line through $$(0, 1)$$ with direction $$(2, 1)$$.

1. Find where $$L$$ meets the line through $$(7, -3)$$ with direction $$(-1, 2)$$.
2. Show that $$L$$ never meets the line through $$(1, 5)$$ with direction $$(-4, -2)$$.

:::solution

1. The test number of :ref{#parallel-determinant} is $$2 \cdot 2 - 1 \cdot (-1) = 5 \ne 0$$, so the
   lines meet once (:ref{#line-intersection}). Solve $$(2t, 1 + t) = (7 - s, -3 + 2s)$$. The first
   equation gives $$s = 7 - 2t$$, and the second becomes $$1 + t = -3 + 14 - 4t$$, so $$5t = 10$$
   and $$t = 2$$. Then $$s = 3$$, and the meeting point is $$(4, 3)$$. Check:
   $$(7, -3) + 3(-1, 2) = (4, 3)$$.
2. The test number is $$2 \cdot (-2) - 1 \cdot (-4) = 0$$, so the lines are parallel. They are
   different lines, since $$(1, 5)$$ is not on $$L$$: $$2t = 1$$ needs $$t = \frac{1}{2}$$, and then
   the second coordinate is $$\frac{3}{2}$$, not $$5$$. By part 2 of :ref{#line-intersection}, they
   never meet.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-lines-midpoints}

Let $$P$$, $$Q$$ and $$R$$ be the corners of a triangle, and let $$M$$ and $$N$$ be the midpoints of
$$PQ$$ and $$PR$$.

1. Show that $$N - M = \frac{1}{2}(R - Q)$$, and deduce that the segment $$MN$$ is parallel to
   $$QR$$ and half as long.
2. Check with $$P = (0, 0)$$, $$Q = (6, 2)$$ and $$R = (2, 6)$$.

:::solution

1. By :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/midpoint-formula}, $$M = \frac{1}{2}(P + Q)$$ and
   $$N = \frac{1}{2}(P + R)$$. By :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/scale-sum-rules} and
   :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/sum-rules},

   ```math
   N - M = \tfrac{1}{2}P + \tfrac{1}{2}R - \tfrac{1}{2}P - \tfrac{1}{2}Q = \tfrac{1}{2}(R - Q)
   ```

   So $$MN$$ is parallel to $$QR$$ by :ref{#parallel-formula}, with $$c = \frac{1}{2}$$. By
   :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/distance-norm} and :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/norm-scale},
   $$d(M, N) = \frac{1}{2}|R - Q| = \frac{1}{2}d(Q, R)$$.

2. $$M = (3, 1)$$ and $$N = (1, 3)$$, so $$N - M = (-2, 2)$$, and $$R - Q = (-4, 4)$$, which is
   twice $$(-2, 2)$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-lines-ships}

At time $$t$$ hours after noon, one ship is at $$(0, 4) + t(2, -1)$$ and another at
$$(1, -4) + t(1, 2)$$, in kilometres.

1. Find where the tracks cross, and when each ship is there. Do the ships collide?
2. How far apart are they at two o'clock?
3. The second ship could have had a different noon position and the same velocity $$(1, 2)$$. Which
   noon position would have made it collide with the first ship at the crossing?

:::solution

1. Solve $$(2t, 4 - t) = (1 + s, -4 + 2s)$$. The first equation gives $$s = 2t - 1$$, and the second
   becomes $$4 - t = -4 + 4t - 2$$, so $$5t = 10$$, $$t = 2$$ and $$s = 3$$. The tracks cross at
   $$(4, 2)$$. The first ship is there at two o'clock and the second at three, so they don't
   collide.
2. At $$t = 2$$ the first ship is at $$(4, 2)$$ and the second at $$(1, -4) + (2, 4) = (3, 0)$$.
   They are $$|(1, 2)| = \sqrt{5} \approx 2.24$$ km apart.
3. The first ship is at $$(4, 2)$$ at $$t = 2$$, so the second must be there then too. Its noon
   position $$Q$$ must satisfy $$Q + 2(1, 2) = (4, 2)$$, so $$Q = (4, 2) - (2, 4) = (2, -2)$$ by
   :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/difference-step}.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-lines-through-origin}

Let $$L$$ be the line through $$P$$ with direction $$A$$. Show that $$L$$ passes through $$O$$
exactly when $$P$$ is a multiple of $$A$$, and that in that case $$L$$ is the line through $$O$$ and
$$A$$.

:::solution

If $$O$$ is on $$L$$, then $$O = P + tA$$ for some $$t$$, so $$P = -tA$$ by
:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/difference-step}, a multiple of $$A$$. If $$P = cA$$, then the point
of $$L$$ with parameter $$-c$$ is $$cA + (-c)A = (c - c)A = O$$, by
:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/scale-sum-rules}, so $$O$$ is on $$L$$.

In that case, part 2 of :ref{#line-descriptions} says $$L$$ is the line through $$O$$ with direction
$$A$$. By :ref{#multiples-line}, that is the line through $$O$$ and $$A$$.

:::
