Imagine writing a geometry app. You tap two points and the app draws the line through them, then you
tap a third point and ask for the parallel through it. Behind the screen there is no ruler, only
pairs of numbers and the formula $$P + t(Q - P)$$, worked out for many values of $$t$$. If the app
is to be trusted, the lines it draws have to behave like lines on paper.

The earlier chapters leaned on a few rules about lines, segments and rays that came from the picture
and were taken as given. Now that each of these objects is a formula, each rule is a claim about
numbers, and a claim about numbers can be proved. In this guide we'll prove that the formulas obey
every rule for lines and segments that ruler and pencil gave us.

## What there is to prove

On paper, a line was something you could draw, and facts about it were read off the drawing. In
$$\mathbb{R}^2$$ a line is a set of points given by a formula, so nothing can be read off. Every
fact has to come from the formula, the same way the rules for distance came from the distance
formula in [Geometry from numbers](../coordinates/geometry-from-numbers.mdx).

In this guide, these are the meanings of the words, all from earlier guides of this chapter:

- **Segment** $$PQ$$, for $$P \ne Q$$: the points $$P + t(Q - P)$$ with $$0 \le t \le 1$$
  (:ref{#segment-formula})
- **Between**: a point of the segment $$PQ$$ other than $$P$$ and $$Q$$ lies between $$P$$ and
  $$Q$$, as in :ref{#distance-and-angles/segment}
- **Ray** from $$P$$ in direction $$A$$: the points $$P + tA$$ with $$t \ge 0$$ (:ref{#ray-formula})
- **Line** through $$P$$ with direction $$A$$: the points $$P + tA$$ for every real $$t$$
  (:ref{#line-formula})
- **Parallel**: lines with directions $$A$$ and $$B$$ are parallel when $$B = cA$$ for some
  $$c \ne 0$$ (:ref{#parallel-formula})
- **Perpendicular**: lines with directions $$A$$ and $$B$$ are perpendicular when $$A \cdot B = 0$$
  (:ref{#perpendicular-formula})

A direction is always a point other than $$O$$. The proofs below use these definitions, the rules
for adding and scaling points, and results already proved from them. They never use a rule from the
picture, because those rules are what we're proving.

## Lines through points

Stretch a string between two nails and it always takes the same path. In $$\mathbb{R}^2$$ that
becomes a claim about sets: of all the sets $$P + tA$$, exactly one contains both points. The same
line has many descriptions, with a different starting point or a stretched direction, so "exactly
one line" means one set, however it is written.

:::theorem[Two points, one line]{#line-rule-two-points}

Two different points $$P$$ and $$Q$$ of $$\mathbb{R}^2$$ lie on exactly one line: the line through
$$P$$ with direction $$Q - P$$. This is rule 1 of :ref{#distance-and-angles/line-rules}, and this
line is the line $$PQ$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_(geometry)#Parametric_equation"}

First, $$Q - P$$ is not $$O$$. By :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/difference-step},
$$P + (Q - P) = Q$$, so if $$Q - P$$ were $$O$$ this would say $$P = Q$$
(:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/sum-rules}, rule 3). So $$Q - P$$ can be used as a direction.

By :ref{#two-point-line}, the line through $$P$$ with direction $$Q - P$$ contains $$P$$ and $$Q$$,
and it is the only line that contains both.

:::

One line has many directions. The line through $$O$$ with direction $$(2, 1)$$ is also the line
through $$O$$ with direction $$(4, 2)$$, or $$(-2, -1)$$. Part 3 of :ref{#line-descriptions} says
there are no others: any two directions of one line are nonzero multiples of each other. That is why
the tests for parallel and perpendicular give the same answer whichever directions we pick, as the
checks after :ref{#parallel-formula} and :ref{#perpendicular-formula} show.

The second rule for lines is about parallels. Through a point $$X$$, the line that runs the same way
as $$L$$ is the one with the same direction, and stretching that direction or moving the starting
point along the line gives nothing new.

:::theorem[One parallel through a point]{#line-rule-parallel}

For any line $$L$$ and any point $$X$$ of $$\mathbb{R}^2$$, exactly one line through $$X$$ is
parallel to $$L$$. This is rule 2 of :ref{#distance-and-angles/line-rules}.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Playfair%27s_axiom"}

Let $$A$$ be a direction of $$L$$, and let $$K$$ be the line through $$X$$ with direction $$A$$.

**There is one.** $$K$$ contains $$X = X + 0A$$, and its direction is $$1A$$, so $$K$$ is parallel
to $$L$$.

**There is only one.** Let $$K'$$ be any line through $$X$$ parallel to $$L$$, and let $$B$$ be a
direction of $$K'$$. Since $$X$$ is on $$K'$$, part 2 of :ref{#line-descriptions} lets $$X$$ be its
starting point, so $$K'$$ is the line through $$X$$ with direction $$B$$. Since $$K' \parallel L$$,
$$B = cA$$ for some $$c \ne 0$$, whichever directions are used (:ref{#parallel-formula}). By part 1
of :ref{#line-descriptions}, the direction $$cA$$ gives the same line as $$A$$. So $$K' = K$$.

:::

If $$X$$ is on $$L$$, the line $$K$$ is $$L$$ itself, which matches the picture's convention that a
line counts as parallel to itself.

In the picture, two lines were parallel when they were the same line or never met. In
$$\mathbb{R}^2$$ parallel means directions that are multiples of each other, and
:ref{#line-intersection} links that to meeting. So the two meanings agree. The two facts that the
picture proved from its rules for lines, about crossing lines and chains of parallels, now follow
from the formulas.

:::proposition[Parallel and crossing lines]{#rules-proved-crossing}

For lines $$L_1$$, $$L_2$$ and $$L_3$$ of $$\mathbb{R}^2$$:

1. **Parallel means the same or apart**: $$L_1 \parallel L_2$$ exactly when $$L_1$$ and $$L_2$$ are
   the same line or never meet
2. **Crossing lines**: two different lines meet in at most one point, and two lines that are not
   parallel meet in exactly one point, as :ref{#distance-and-angles/crossing-lines} says
3. **Parallel to a parallel**: if $$L_1 \parallel L_2$$ and $$L_2 \parallel L_3$$, then
   $$L_1 \parallel L_3$$, as :ref{#distance-and-angles/parallel-transitive} says

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line%E2%80%93line_intersection"}

**Part 1.** If $$L_1 \parallel L_2$$, then by :ref{#line-intersection} they are the same line or
never meet. For the other way, if they are the same line, any two of its directions are nonzero
multiples of each other (part 3 of :ref{#line-descriptions}), so the line is parallel to itself. If
they never meet, they can't be non-parallel, since non-parallel lines meet by
:ref{#line-intersection}. So they are parallel.

**Part 2.** Take two different lines. If they are not parallel, they meet in exactly one point by
:ref{#line-intersection}. If they are parallel, part 1 says they never meet, since they are not the
same line. Either way they meet in at most one point.

**Part 3.** Let $$A_1$$, $$A_2$$ and $$A_3$$ be directions of the three lines. By the checks after
:ref{#parallel-formula} it doesn't matter which ones. Then $$A_2 = cA_1$$ and $$A_3 = c'A_2$$ with
$$c, c' \ne 0$$, so $$A_3 = c'(cA_1) = (c'c)A_1$$ (:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/scale-twice}).
Since $$c'c \ne 0$$ (:ref{#numbers/zero-product}), $$L_1 \parallel L_3$$.

:::

## A detour of no extra length

Walk from $$P$$ to $$M$$ by way of a third point $$Q$$. The triangle inequality says the detour is
never shorter than the direct route, and when $$Q$$ sits on the segment from $$P$$ to $$M$$ the two
are the same length. Rule 1 of :ref{#distance-and-angles/segment-rules} says more: those are the
only points where the detour adds nothing. In $$\mathbb{R}^2$$, the work is in finding when the
bound of :ref{#coordinates/product-bound} is an equality.

### When the bound is an equality

Recall the bound: for steps $$(a, b)$$ and $$(s, t)$$, the number $$as + bt$$ is at most the product
of their lengths, $$\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}\,\sqrt{s^2 + t^2}$$. Try the steps $$(2, 1)$$ and $$(6, 3)$$,
where the second is the first one tripled. Then $$as + bt = 12 + 3 = 15$$ and
$$\sqrt{5}\,\sqrt{45} = \sqrt{225} = 15$$, so the two sides are equal.

Now turn the second step round to $$(-4, -2)$$, which is $$-2$$ times the first. The lengths
multiply to $$\sqrt{5}\,\sqrt{20} = 10$$ as before, but $$as + bt = -8 - 2 = -10$$. The bound holds,
with room to spare. So equality needs more than one step being a multiple of the other: the multiple
can't be negative.

:::lemma[When the bound is an equality]{#product-bound-equality}

Let $$a$$, $$b$$, $$s$$ and $$t$$ be real numbers with $$(a, b) \ne (0, 0)$$. Then

```math
as + bt = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}\,\sqrt{s^2 + t^2}
```

exactly when $$(s, t) = c(a, b)$$ for some number $$c \ge 0$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy%E2%80%93Schwarz_inequality#R2_-_The_plane"}

Write $$R = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}\,\sqrt{s^2 + t^2}$$. Since $$(a, b) \ne (0, 0)$$, the number
$$a^2 + b^2$$ is not $$0$$ (part 2 of :ref{#real-numbers/squares-positive}), and it is not negative,
so it is positive.

**If $$(s, t) = c(a, b)$$ with $$c \ge 0$$.** Then $$s = ca$$ and $$t = cb$$, so
$$as + bt = c(a^2 + b^2)$$. Also $$s^2 + t^2 = c^2(a^2 + b^2)$$, and since $$c \ge 0$$,
$$\sqrt{c^2} = c$$ (:ref{#real-numbers/square-root}). By :ref{#real-numbers/root-product},
$$\sqrt{s^2 + t^2} = \sqrt{c^2}\,\sqrt{a^2 + b^2} = c\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$$, so

```math
R = c\left(\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}\right)^2 = c(a^2 + b^2) = as + bt
```

**If $$as + bt = R$$.** A product of square roots is not negative, so $$as + bt \ge 0$$, and the
squares of the two sides are equal. The proof of :ref{#coordinates/product-bound} showed that

```math
R^2 - (as + bt)^2 = (at - bs)^2
```

so $$(at - bs)^2 = 0$$, and therefore $$at - bs = 0$$ (part 1 of
:ref{#real-numbers/squares-positive}). That is, $$at = bs$$. Now let

```math
c = \frac{as + bt}{a^2 + b^2}
```

which is at least $$0$$, as a number $$\ge 0$$ divided by a positive one. Using $$at = bs$$ to
replace $$abt$$ by $$b^2s$$ and $$abs$$ by $$a^2t$$,

```math
ca = \frac{a^2s + abt}{a^2 + b^2} = \frac{a^2s + b^2s}{a^2 + b^2} = s \qquad cb = \frac{abs + b^2t}{a^2 + b^2} = \frac{a^2t + b^2t}{a^2 + b^2} = t
```

So $$(s, t) = c(a, b)$$ with $$c \ge 0$$.

:::

In the language of points, the lemma says that the bound is an equality exactly when the second step
is a multiple of the first by a number $$c \ge 0$$. When $$c > 0$$, the two steps have the same
direction (:ref{#same-direction}), and when $$c = 0$$ the second step is $$O$$.

### When a detour is no longer

Take $$P = (1, 1)$$ and $$M = (7, 4)$$. The point a third of the way along is
$$Q = P + \frac{1}{3}(M - P) = (3, 2)$$, and the distances are $$d(P, Q) = \sqrt{5}$$,
$$d(Q, M) = \sqrt{20} = 2\sqrt{5}$$ and $$d(P, M) = \sqrt{45} = 3\sqrt{5}$$. So the detour through
$$Q$$ adds nothing. Through $$Q' = (3, 3)$$, off the segment, it is
$$\sqrt{8} + \sqrt{17} \approx 6.95$$, which beats $$3\sqrt{5} \approx 6.71$$.

```plane
point P 1 1 sw
point Q 3 2 se
point M 7 4 ne
point Q' 3 3 nw
segment P M color=blue
segment P Q' color=orange dashed
segment Q' M color=orange dashed
caption "Q = (3, 2) is on the segment from P = (1, 1) to M = (7, 4), so the detour through it adds nothing; the detour through Q' = (3, 3) is longer"
```

The proof follows the triangle inequality's own proof, which squared both sides and compared the
middle terms. The two sides are equal exactly when those middle terms are, and the lemma says when
that happens.

:::theorem[When a detour is no longer]{#triangle-equality}

Let $$P \ne M$$ be points of $$\mathbb{R}^2$$. For any point $$Q$$,

```math
d(P, M) = d(P, Q) + d(Q, M)
```

exactly when $$Q$$ lies on the segment $$PM$$. This is rule 1 of
:ref{#distance-and-angles/segment-rules}.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triangle_inequality#Triangle_equality"}

The letter $$t$$ is needed below for a step, so write the points of the segment $$PM$$ as
$$P + u(M - P)$$ with $$0 \le u \le 1$$.

**On the segment.** Let $$Q = P + u(M - P)$$ with $$0 \le u \le 1$$. By :ref{#fraction-along},
$$d(P, Q) = u\,d(P, M)$$ and $$d(Q, M) = (1 - u)\,d(P, M)$$. These add up to $$d(P, M)$$.

**Equal distances.** Suppose $$d(P, M) = d(P, Q) + d(Q, M)$$. If $$Q = P$$, then $$Q$$ is on the
segment, with $$u = 0$$. Otherwise, write the steps as in
[Trips as steps](../coordinates/geometry-from-numbers.mdx#trips-as-steps): $$(a, b) = Q - P$$, which
is not $$O$$, and $$(s, t) = M - Q$$. The proof of :ref{#coordinates/triangle-inequality-plane}
worked out

```math
d(P, M)^2 = (a^2 + b^2) + 2(as + bt) + (s^2 + t^2)
```

```math
\big(d(P, Q) + d(Q, M)\big)^2 = (a^2 + b^2) + 2\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}\,\sqrt{s^2 + t^2} + (s^2 + t^2)
```

The distances are equal, so their squares are, and so the middle terms are:
$$as + bt = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}\,\sqrt{s^2 + t^2}$$. By :ref{#product-bound-equality},
$$M - Q = c(Q - P)$$ for some $$c \ge 0$$.

Next, $$P + (Q - P) + (M - Q) = Q + (M - Q) = M$$, so $$(Q - P) + (M - Q)$$ is a step from $$P$$ to
$$M$$. By the uniqueness in :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/difference-step}, it is $$M - P$$. So

```math
M - P = c(Q - P) + 1(Q - P) = (1 + c)(Q - P)
```

by :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/scale-sum-rules}. Let $$u = \frac{1}{1 + c}$$, so $$0 < u \le 1$$
since $$1 + c \ge 1$$. Then $$u(M - P) = Q - P$$ (:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/scale-twice}), so
$$Q = P + u(M - P)$$, a point of the segment.

:::

In the example, $$Q - P = (2, 1)$$ and $$M - Q = (4, 2)$$, so $$c = 2$$ and $$u = \frac{1}{3}$$: the
point a third of the way along, as it should be.

## The rules for segments

Every point of a line has a label, its parameter $$t$$, like the distance markers along a motorway.
Which point is between which is then a question about the labels: a car at marker $$40$$ is between
markers $$30$$ and $$70$$, and not between $$30$$ and $$35$$. The lemma below turns that into a
statement about the formula.

:::lemma[Reading a line by its parameters]{#rules-proved-parameters}

Let $$L$$ be the line through $$P$$ with direction $$A$$, and write $$X_t = P + tA$$ for the point
with parameter $$t$$.

1. **One point, one parameter**: $$X_t = X_u$$ exactly when $$t = u$$
2. **Segments are intervals**: for numbers $$r \ne w$$, the segment from $$X_r$$ to $$X_w$$ is made
   of the points $$X_t$$ with $$t$$ from $$r$$ to $$w$$, ends included, whichever of $$r$$ and $$w$$
   is larger. So $$X_t$$ lies between $$X_r$$ and $$X_w$$ exactly when $$t$$ lies strictly between
   $$r$$ and $$w$$

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_segment"}

**Part 1.** If $$P + tA = P + uA$$, adding $$-P$$ to both sides gives $$tA = uA$$
(:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/sum-rules}). Since $$A \ne O$$, this gives $$t = u$$ by part 2 of
:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/scale-cancel}. And equal parameters give the same point.

**Part 2.** By part 1, $$X_r \ne X_w$$, so the segment from $$X_r$$ to $$X_w$$ is defined. The step
between its ends is $$X_w - X_r = (w - r)A$$, since $$X_r + (w - r)A = P + wA$$ by
:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/scale-sum-rules}. So the points of the segment are

```math
X_r + v(w - r)A = P + \big(r + v(w - r)\big)A = X_{r + v(w - r)} \qquad 0 \le v \le 1
```

using :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/scale-twice} and
:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/scale-sum-rules}. As $$v$$ runs from $$0$$ to $$1$$, the number
$$t = r + v(w - r)$$ runs from $$r$$ to $$w$$, and each $$t$$ from $$r$$ to $$w$$ comes from
$$v = \frac{t - r}{w - r}$$, which lies from $$0$$ to $$1$$. So the segment is the set of $$X_t$$
with $$t$$ from $$r$$ to $$w$$.

The ends $$X_r$$ and $$X_w$$ have parameters $$r$$ and $$w$$, and by part 1 no other parameter gives
them. So the points between $$X_r$$ and $$X_w$$ are the $$X_t$$ with $$t$$ from $$r$$ to $$w$$ other
than $$r$$ and $$w$$, that is, strictly between them.

:::

With the lemma and :ref{#triangle-equality}, all three rules for segments follow.

:::theorem[The rules for segments hold]{#segment-rules-proved}

In $$\mathbb{R}^2$$, with segments as in :ref{#segment-formula}, the three rules of
:ref{#distance-and-angles/segment-rules} hold. Let $$P \ne M$$.

1. **Points on the segment**: for any point $$Q$$, $$d(P, M) = d(P, Q) + d(Q, M)$$ exactly when
   $$Q$$ lies on the segment $$PM$$
2. **Every length is reached**: for every number $$c$$ with $$0 \le c \le d(P, M)$$, exactly one
   point $$Q$$ of the segment $$PM$$ has $$d(P, Q) = c$$
3. **One point in the middle**: of three different points on one line, exactly one lies between the
   other two

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_segment"}

**Rule 1** is :ref{#triangle-equality}.

**Rule 2.** Since $$P \ne M$$, $$d(P, M) > 0$$ (:ref{#coordinates/distance-zero}). Each point of the
segment is $$Q_t = P + t(M - P)$$ for some $$t$$ with $$0 \le t \le 1$$, and by
:ref{#fraction-along}, $$d(P, Q_t) = t\,d(P, M)$$. So $$d(P, Q_t) = c$$ exactly when
$$t = \frac{c}{d(P, M)}$$. That number lies from $$0$$ to $$1$$, because $$0 \le c \le d(P, M)$$, so
the point $$Q_t$$ with this $$t$$ is on the segment and has $$d(P, Q_t) = c$$. Any point of the
segment at distance $$c$$ from $$P$$ has this same $$t$$, so it is the same point.

**Rule 3.** Let the line be the line through a point $$N$$ with direction $$A$$, and write
$$X_t = N + tA$$. Write the three points as $$X_r$$, $$X_s$$ and $$X_w$$. The points are different,
so the parameters are different (part 1 of :ref{#rules-proved-parameters}). Name them so that
$$r < s < w$$. By part 2 of the same lemma:

- $$X_s$$ lies between $$X_r$$ and $$X_w$$, since $$s$$ is strictly between $$r$$ and $$w$$
- $$X_r$$ doesn't lie between $$X_s$$ and $$X_w$$, since $$r$$ is less than both $$s$$ and $$w$$
- $$X_w$$ doesn't lie between $$X_r$$ and $$X_s$$, since $$w$$ is more than both $$r$$ and $$s$$

So exactly one of the three points, $$X_s$$, lies between the other two.

:::

## Rays

A torch beam starts at the torch and runs on in one direction. The [Rays and direction](./rays.mdx)
guide wrote that as $$P + tA$$ with $$t \ge 0$$. The picture described the same beam another way:
take the whole line, and throw away the points on the far side of $$P$$ from $$Q$$, the points where
$$P$$ sits between them and $$Q$$. On the line, those are the points with negative parameters.

```plane
point X -1 0 se
point P 1 1 se
point Q 3 2 se
point Y 5 3 se
ray P Y extend=0.4 color=blue
segment X P color=muted dashed
text -1 0.7 "t = −1"
text 1 1.7 "t = 0"
text 3 2.7 "t = 1"
text 5 3.7 "t = 2"
caption "The line through P = (1, 1) and Q = (3, 2): the ray from P through Q (blue) is the part with t ≥ 0; P lies between X, at t = −1, and Q"
```

:::proposition[The two descriptions of a ray agree]{#ray-agrees}

Let $$P \ne Q$$, and let $$A = Q - P$$. The ray from $$P$$ through $$Q$$ of :ref{#ray-formula}, the
points $$P + tA$$ with $$t \ge 0$$, is the same set as the ray $$PQ$$ of
:ref{#distance-and-angles/ray}: the point $$P$$ together with every point $$X$$ of the line $$PQ$$
for which $$P$$ doesn't lie between $$X$$ and $$Q$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_(geometry)#Ray"}

By :ref{#line-rule-two-points}, the line $$PQ$$ is the line through $$P$$ with direction $$A$$.
Write its points as $$X_t = P + tA$$, so $$X_0 = P$$ and $$X_1 = P + (Q - P) = Q$$. The point
$$P = X_0$$ belongs to both sets. Take any other point $$X_t$$ of the line, so $$t \ne 0$$.

**If $$t = 1$$**, then $$X_t = Q$$. Between needs a segment with two different ends, so $$P$$
doesn't lie between $$Q$$ and $$Q$$, and $$Q$$ is in the picture's ray. It is also in the formula
ray, since $$1 \ge 0$$.

**If $$t \ne 1$$**, part 2 of :ref{#rules-proved-parameters} with ends $$X_t$$ and $$X_1$$ says that
$$P = X_0$$ lies between $$X_t$$ and $$Q$$ exactly when $$0$$ is strictly between $$t$$ and $$1$$.
Since $$0 < 1$$, that happens exactly when $$t < 0$$. So $$X_t$$ is in the picture's ray exactly
when $$t > 0$$, which is exactly when it is in the formula ray.

:::

So the beam from the formula and the beam from the picture are the same set of points. The ray from
$$P$$ in a direction $$A$$ is the ray from $$P$$ through $$P + A$$, since its direction is
$$(P + A) - P = A$$, so every formula ray is covered.

## Perpendicular lines

A carpenter's set square fits only one way against a straight edge at a given point, and sliding it
along to a parallel edge keeps it square. In $$\mathbb{R}^2$$ a square corner is a dot product of
$$0$$, and the quarter turn of :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/quarter-turn} hands us a direction
square on to any direction. For $$A = (3, 1)$$, the quarter turn gives $$(-1, 3)$$, and
$$(3, 1) \cdot (-1, 3) = -3 + 3 = 0$$.

:::theorem[The rules for perpendicular lines hold]{#perpendicular-rules-proved}

In $$\mathbb{R}^2$$, with perpendicular as in :ref{#perpendicular-formula}:

1. **One perpendicular through a point**: for any line $$L$$ and point $$X$$, exactly one line
   through $$X$$ is perpendicular to $$L$$
2. **Perpendicular to parallels**: if $$L_1 \perp L_2$$ and $$L_2 \parallel L_3$$, then
   $$L_1 \perp L_3$$
3. **Perpendicular lines cross**: two perpendicular lines meet in exactly one point
4. **Two perpendiculars are parallel**: if $$L_1 \perp L_2$$ and $$L_2 \perp L_3$$, then
   $$L_1 \parallel L_3$$

Rules 1 and 2 are the two rules of :ref{#distance-and-angles/perpendicular-rules}, and rule 4 is
:ref{#distance-and-angles/perpendiculars-parallel}.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perpendicular#In_relationship_to_parallel_lines"}

By the check after :ref{#perpendicular-formula}, any direction of each line may be used.

**Rule 1.** Let $$A = (a_1, a_2)$$ be a direction of $$L$$ and let $$G = G_{90}(A) = (-a_2, a_1)$$.
Its coordinates are those of $$A$$ swapped, one with its sign changed, so $$G \ne O$$. And
$$A \cdot G = -a_1a_2 + a_2a_1 = 0$$. So the line $$K$$ through $$X$$ with direction $$G$$ is
perpendicular to $$L$$.

Now we show that every direction $$B = (b_1, b_2)$$ with $$A \cdot B = 0$$ is a nonzero multiple of
$$G$$. The parallel test for $$G$$ and $$B$$ (:ref{#parallel-determinant}) uses the number

```math
(-a_2)b_2 - a_1b_1 = -(a_1b_1 + a_2b_2) = -(A \cdot B) = 0
```

so $$B = cG$$ for some $$c \ne 0$$. Let $$K'$$ be any line through $$X$$ perpendicular to $$L$$,
with direction $$B$$. By part 2 of :ref{#line-descriptions}, $$K'$$ is the line through $$X$$ with
direction $$B = cG$$, and by part 1 that is the line through $$X$$ with direction $$G$$. So
$$K' = K$$.

**Rule 2.** Let $$A_1$$, $$A_2$$ and $$A_3$$ be directions of the three lines, with
$$A_1 \cdot A_2 = 0$$ and $$A_3 = cA_2$$. By :ref{#dot-rules},
$$A_1 \cdot A_3 = A_1 \cdot (cA_2) = c\,(A_1 \cdot A_2) = 0$$.

**Rule 3.** Let the lines have directions $$A$$ and $$B$$ with $$A \cdot B = 0$$. If they were
parallel, then $$B = cA$$ with $$c \ne 0$$, and :ref{#dot-rules} would give
$$A \cdot B = c\,(A \cdot A) = c\,|A|^2$$. That is not $$0$$ (:ref{#numbers/zero-product}), since
$$c \ne 0$$ and $$|A| > 0$$ (:ref{#coordinates/distance-zero}). So the lines are not parallel, and
by :ref{#line-intersection} they meet in exactly one point.

**Rule 4.** Let the directions be $$A_1$$, $$A_2$$ and $$A_3$$. Then $$A_2 \cdot A_3 = 0$$, and
$$A_2 \cdot A_1 = A_1 \cdot A_2 = 0$$ by :ref{#dot-rules}. By the second paragraph of the proof of
rule 1, with $$G = G_{90}(A_2)$$, both are nonzero multiples of $$G$$: $$A_1 = cG$$ and
$$A_3 = c'G$$. So $$A_3 = \frac{c'}{c}A_1$$ (:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/scale-twice}), with
$$\frac{c'}{c} \ne 0$$, and $$L_1 \parallel L_3$$.

:::

Rule 3 is what the picture's definition (:ref{#distance-and-angles/perpendicular}) asks for first:
perpendicular lines cross.

The Pythagoras theorem of Distance and angles was proved with areas. For perpendicular in the
formula sense it needs no area at all, because :ref{#difference-length} already has the right shape.

:::proposition[Pythagoras from the dot product]{#rules-proved-pythagoras}

For any points $$A$$ and $$B$$, $$A \cdot B = 0$$ exactly when $$|A - B|^2 = |A|^2 + |B|^2$$. So if
$$C$$, $$P$$ and $$Q$$ are points with $$P \ne C$$ and $$Q \ne C$$, and the lines $$CP$$ and $$CQ$$
are perpendicular, then

```math
d(P, Q)^2 = d(C, P)^2 + d(C, Q)^2
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_theorem#Inner_product_spaces"}

By :ref{#difference-length}, $$|A - B|^2 = |A|^2 + |B|^2 - 2\,A \cdot B$$. This equals
$$|A|^2 + |B|^2$$ exactly when $$2\,A \cdot B = 0$$, that is, when $$A \cdot B = 0$$.

For the triangle, the lines $$CP$$ and $$CQ$$ have directions $$A = P - C$$ and $$B = Q - C$$
(:ref{#line-rule-two-points}), so $$A \cdot B = 0$$. The two copies of $$C$$ cancel in
$$A - B = (P - C) - (Q - C) = P - Q$$ (:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/sum-rules}). By
:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/distance-norm}, $$|A - B| = d(P, Q)$$, $$|A| = d(C, P)$$ and
$$|B| = d(C, Q)$$, and the first part gives the result.

:::

For example, the lines from $$C = (1, 1)$$ to $$P = (4, 2)$$ and to $$Q = (0, 4)$$ have directions
$$(3, 1)$$ and $$(-1, 3)$$, which have dot product $$0$$. And $$d(C, P)^2 + d(C, Q)^2 = 10 + 10$$,
which is $$d(P, Q)^2 = 16 + 4 = 20$$.

## Moves send lines to lines

Slide a ruler across a page and its edge stays straight. A half turn, a zoom or a flip does the
same. With formulas this is a calculation: each move turns $$P + tB$$ into a new starting point plus
$$t$$ times a new direction. Take the slide by $$A$$: $$T_A(P + tB) = (P + tB) + A = (P + A) + tB$$,
so the line through $$P$$ with direction $$B$$ lands on the line through $$P + A$$ with the same
direction.

:::theorem[Moves send lines to lines]{#moves-keep-lines}

Let $$F$$ be one of the moves of
[Moves as formulas](../adding-and-scaling-points/moves-as-formulas.mdx): a slide $$T_A$$, a half
turn $$H_Q$$, a zoom $$Z_{r,Q}$$ with $$r > 0$$, a flip $$R_1$$, $$R_2$$ or $$S$$, or the quarter
turn $$G_{90}$$. For every point $$P$$ and direction $$B$$ there is a direction $$B'$$ with

```math
F(P + tB) = F(P) + tB' \quad \text{for every real } t
```

So $$F$$ sends:

- the line through $$P$$ with direction $$B$$ onto the line through $$F(P)$$ with direction $$B'$$
- the ray from $$P$$ in direction $$B$$ onto the ray from $$F(P)$$ in direction $$B'$$
- the segment $$PM$$, for $$P \ne M$$, onto the segment from $$F(P)$$ to $$F(M)$$

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_transformation#Properties_preserved"}

**Finding $$B'$$.** For the slide, half turn and zoom, the rules for adding and scaling
(:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/sum-rules}, :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/scale-sum-rules},
:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/scale-twice}) give

```math
T_A(P + tB) = (P + A) + tB \qquad H_Q(P + tB) = (2Q - P) + t(-B) \qquad Z_{r,Q}(P + tB) = \big(Q + r(P - Q)\big) + t(rB)
```

So $$B' = B$$, $$-B$$ and $$rB$$. For the flips and the quarter turn, write $$P = (p_1, p_2)$$ and
$$B = (b_1, b_2)$$, so $$P + tB = (p_1 + tb_1, p_2 + tb_2)$$. Then

```math
R_1(P + tB) = (p_1, -p_2) + t(b_1, -b_2) \qquad R_2(P + tB) = (-p_1, p_2) + t(-b_1, b_2)
```

```math
S(P + tB) = (p_2, p_1) + t(b_2, b_1) \qquad G_{90}(P + tB) = (-p_2, p_1) + t(-b_2, b_1)
```

In each case the first point is $$F(P)$$, the case $$t = 0$$. None of the seven points $$B'$$ is
$$O$$. The first is $$B$$ itself, and for $$-B$$ and $$rB$$ this is part 1 of
:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/scale-cancel}, since $$-1 \ne 0$$ and $$r > 0$$. The other four have
the coordinates of $$B$$, perhaps swapped and with signs changed, so both are $$0$$ exactly when
$$b_1 = b_2 = 0$$.

**Lines and rays.** Every image $$F(P + tB)$$ of a point of the line is $$F(P) + tB'$$, a point of
the line through $$F(P)$$ with direction $$B'$$. And every point $$F(P) + tB'$$ of that line is the
image of $$P + tB$$. So the first line goes onto the second. The same argument with $$t \ge 0$$
throughout covers rays.

**Segments.** Take $$B = M - P$$. Then $$F(M) = F(P + 1B) = F(P) + B'$$, so $$B' = F(M) - F(P)$$
(:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/difference-step}), and $$F(M) \ne F(P)$$ since $$B' \ne O$$. The
same argument with $$0 \le t \le 1$$ shows that $$F$$ sends the points $$P + t(M - P)$$ onto the
points $$F(P) + t\big(F(M) - F(P)\big)$$, which make up the segment from $$F(P)$$ to $$F(M)$$.

:::

For a zoom about the origin, $$Z_r(P) = rP$$, so the segment $$PM$$ goes onto the segment from
$$rP$$ to $$rM$$. That is rule 2 of :ref{#area-and-scaling/zoom-rules}, now proved for zooms written
as formulas, about the origin or any other centre. It also matches :ref{#isometries/isometry-lines},
which the Isometries chapter proved from the picture's rules for any isometry.

Rule 3 of the same remark, that a zoom keeps the size of every angle, is still taken as given. Angle
sizes in coordinates need more than right angles, and they wait for a later chapter on trigonometry.

## What is proved now

Every rule about lines and segments that Distance and angles took from the picture is now a theorem
in $$\mathbb{R}^2$$:

- **Distance**: the three rules of :ref{#distance-and-angles/distance-rules}, from
  [Geometry from numbers](../coordinates/geometry-from-numbers.mdx)
- **Segments**: the three rules of :ref{#distance-and-angles/segment-rules}, by
  :ref{#segment-rules-proved}
- **Lines**: both rules of :ref{#distance-and-angles/line-rules}, by :ref{#line-rule-two-points} and
  :ref{#line-rule-parallel}, along with crossing lines and parallel to a parallel
  (:ref{#rules-proved-crossing})
- **Perpendicular lines**: both rules of :ref{#distance-and-angles/perpendicular-rules}, by
  :ref{#perpendicular-rules-proved}
- **Zooms**: rules 1 and 2 of :ref{#area-and-scaling/zoom-rules}, for zooms written as formulas

Results that Distance and angles proved from these rules alone carry over to $$\mathbb{R}^2$$ too,
since their proofs used nothing else. The distance between parallel lines
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/parallel-distance}) was also taken as given, and the last exercise proves
it from :ref{#distance-to-line}.

Some facts still come from the picture. Measuring angles rests on the rules for disc area
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/disc-area}), and areas rest on :ref{#distance-and-angles/area-rules}.
Results built on them, such as the angles of a triangle adding up to $$180^\circ$$ and the link in
:ref{#perpendicular-dot} between a dot product of $$0$$ and the picture's right angle, still lean on
those rules.

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on the rules for lines, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-rules-proved-equality}

Take $$(a, b) = (3, -2)$$. For each step $$(s, t)$$ below, work out both sides of
:ref{#product-bound-equality} and say whether they are equal. Where they aren't, say which condition
of the lemma fails.

1. $$(s, t) = (4.5, -3)$$
2. $$(s, t) = (-6, 4)$$
3. $$(s, t) = (2, 3)$$

:::solution

Here $$\sqrt{a^2 + b^2} = \sqrt{13}$$.

1. $$as + bt = 13.5 + 6 = 19.5$$. The lengths are $$\sqrt{13}$$ and
   $$\sqrt{20.25 + 9} = \sqrt{29.25}$$, and their product is $$\sqrt{380.25} = 19.5$$. They are
   equal, and $$(4.5, -3) = 1.5\,(3, -2)$$ with $$1.5 \ge 0$$.
2. $$as + bt = -18 - 8 = -26$$, and $$\sqrt{13}\,\sqrt{52} = \sqrt{676} = 26$$. Not equal:
   $$(-6, 4) = -2\,(3, -2)$$ is a multiple, but by a negative number.
3. $$as + bt = 6 - 6 = 0$$, and $$\sqrt{13}\,\sqrt{13} = 13$$. Not equal: $$at - bs = 9 + 4 = 13$$,
   which is not $$0$$, so $$(2, 3)$$ is not a multiple of $$(3, -2)$$ at all. In fact the dot
   product is $$0$$, so the two steps are perpendicular.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-rules-proved-detour}

Let $$P = (-2, 3)$$ and $$M = (6, -3)$$.

1. For $$Q = (2, 0)$$, check that $$d(P, M) = d(P, Q) + d(Q, M)$$, and find the $$u$$ with
   $$Q = P + u(M - P)$$.
2. For $$Q' = (2, 1)$$, work out $$d(P, Q') + d(Q', M)$$, and use :ref{#triangle-equality} to say
   whether $$Q'$$ is on the segment $$PM$$.

:::solution

1. $$d(P, Q) = \sqrt{16 + 9} = 5$$, $$d(Q, M) = \sqrt{16 + 9} = 5$$ and
   $$d(P, M) = \sqrt{64 + 36} = 10$$, so the detour adds nothing. The steps are $$Q - P = (4, -3)$$
   and $$M - Q = (4, -3)$$, so $$c = 1$$ in the proof of :ref{#triangle-equality} and
   $$u = \frac{1}{1 + 1} = \frac{1}{2}$$. Indeed $$P + \frac{1}{2}(8, -6) = (2, 0)$$: $$Q$$ is the
   midpoint.
2. $$d(P, Q') = \sqrt{16 + 4} = \sqrt{20} \approx 4.47$$ and
   $$d(Q', M) = \sqrt{16 + 16} = \sqrt{32} \approx 5.66$$. The sum is about $$10.13$$, which is not
   $$10$$, so by :ref{#triangle-equality} the point $$Q'$$ is not on the segment.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-rules-proved-middle}

Show that $$X_1 = (7, -1)$$, $$X_2 = (-2, 5)$$ and $$X_3 = (1, 3)$$ lie on one line. Use
:ref{#rules-proved-parameters} to say which one lies between the other two, and check the answer
with distances.

:::solution

Take the line through $$X_2$$ with direction $$A = X_3 - X_2 = (3, -2)$$. Then $$X_2$$ has parameter
$$0$$ and $$X_3$$ has parameter $$1$$. Also $$X_2 + 3A = (-2 + 9, 5 - 6) = (7, -1) = X_1$$, so
$$X_1$$ is on the line, with parameter $$3$$.

The parameters are $$0$$, $$1$$ and $$3$$, and $$1$$ is strictly between the others, so $$X_3$$ lies
between $$X_2$$ and $$X_1$$. As a check, $$d(X_2, X_3) = \sqrt{13}$$,
$$d(X_3, X_1) = \sqrt{36 + 16} = 2\sqrt{13}$$ and $$d(X_2, X_1) = \sqrt{81 + 36} = 3\sqrt{13}$$, and
$$\sqrt{13} + 2\sqrt{13} = 3\sqrt{13}$$, as :ref{#triangle-equality} requires.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-rules-proved-ray}

Let $$P = (1, 2)$$ and $$Q = (3, 1)$$. For each point, decide whether it lies on the ray from $$P$$
through $$Q$$:

1. $$(7, -1)$$
2. $$(-3, 4)$$
3. $$(2, 1.5)$$
4. $$(4, 1)$$

For the point that is on the line but not on the ray, check the picture's description from
:ref{#ray-agrees}: $$P$$ lies between it and $$Q$$.

:::solution

The direction is $$A = Q - P = (2, -1)$$. A point $$X$$ is on the line when $$X - P$$ is a multiple
$$tA$$, and on the ray when also $$t \ge 0$$.

1. $$X - P = (6, -3) = 3A$$, so $$t = 3$$: on the ray.
2. $$X - P = (-4, 2) = -2A$$, so $$t = -2$$: on the line, not on the ray.
3. $$X - P = (1, -0.5) = 0.5A$$, so $$t = 0.5$$: on the ray, and on the segment $$PQ$$ too.
4. $$X - P = (3, -1)$$. A multiple of $$(2, -1)$$ with first coordinate $$3$$ is
   $$1.5A = (3, -1.5)$$, so $$X$$ is not on the line at all.

For $$X = (-3, 4)$$: $$Q - X = (6, -3)$$ and $$P - X = (4, -2) = \frac{2}{3}(6, -3)$$. So
$$P = X + \frac{2}{3}(Q - X)$$, and since $$0 < \frac{2}{3} < 1$$, $$P$$ lies between $$X$$ and
$$Q$$, so the picture leaves $$X$$ out of the ray too.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-rules-proved-perpendicular}

Let $$L$$ be the line through $$O$$ with direction $$A = (3, 4)$$, and let $$X = (-5, 10)$$.

1. Use the proof of :ref{#perpendicular-rules-proved} to write down the line $$K$$ through $$X$$
   perpendicular to $$L$$.
2. Find the point $$F$$ where $$K$$ meets $$L$$, and the distance $$d(X, F)$$.
3. Someone suggests the line through $$X$$ with direction $$(8, -6)$$. Explain why it is the same
   line as $$K$$.

:::solution

1. $$G_{90}(A) = (-4, 3)$$, and $$A \cdot (-4, 3) = -12 + 12 = 0$$. So $$K$$ is the line through
   $$X$$ with direction $$(-4, 3)$$: the points $$(-5 - 4s, 10 + 3s)$$.
2. A meeting point has $$t(3, 4) = (-5 - 4s, 10 + 3s)$$, so $$3t + 4s = -5$$ and $$4t - 3s = 10$$.
   Multiplying the first by $$3$$, the second by $$4$$ and adding gives $$25t = 25$$, so $$t = 1$$,
   and then $$s = -2$$. So $$F = (3, 4)$$. Then $$X - F = (-8, 6)$$, and
   $$d(X, F) = \sqrt{64 + 36} = 10$$.
3. $$(8, -6) \cdot (3, 4) = 24 - 24 = 0$$, so this line is perpendicular to $$L$$ too, and it passes
   through $$X$$. By rule 1 there is only one such line, so it is $$K$$. Directly,
   $$(8, -6) = -2\,(-4, 3)$$, and :ref{#line-descriptions} gives the same line.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-rules-proved-shear}

The **shear** $$F(x, y) = (x + y, y)$$ slides each point sideways by its height. It is not one of
the moves in :ref{#moves-keep-lines}. Prove that it still sends the line through $$P$$ with
direction $$B$$ onto a line, and find the image of the line through $$(0, 0)$$ with direction
$$(1, 1)$$.

:::solution

Write $$P = (p_1, p_2)$$ and $$B = (b_1, b_2)$$. Then

```math
F(P + tB) = \big(p_1 + tb_1 + p_2 + tb_2,\ p_2 + tb_2\big) = (p_1 + p_2, p_2) + t(b_1 + b_2, b_2)
```

So $$F(P + tB) = F(P) + tB'$$ with $$B' = (b_1 + b_2, b_2)$$. This $$B'$$ is not $$O$$: if
$$b_2 = 0$$ and $$b_1 + b_2 = 0$$, then $$b_1 = 0$$ too, but $$B \ne O$$. Now the argument of
:ref{#moves-keep-lines} applies word for word: every image lies on the line through $$F(P)$$ with
direction $$B'$$, and every point $$F(P) + tB'$$ of that line is the image of $$P + tB$$.

For $$P = (0, 0)$$ and $$B = (1, 1)$$, $$F(P) = (0, 0)$$ and $$B' = (2, 1)$$. So the diagonal
$$y = x$$ goes onto the line through $$O$$ with direction $$(2, 1)$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-rules-proved-half-turn}

Let $$L$$ be the line through $$P$$ with direction $$B$$, and let $$Q$$ be any point. Prove that the
half turn $$H_Q$$ sends $$L$$ onto a line parallel to $$L$$, and that this line is $$L$$ itself
exactly when $$Q$$ lies on $$L$$.

:::solution

By :ref{#moves-keep-lines}, $$H_Q$$ sends $$L$$ onto the line $$L'$$ through $$2Q - P$$ with
direction $$-B = (-1)B$$. Since $$-1 \ne 0$$, $$L' \parallel L$$.

**If $$Q$$ is on $$L$$**, say $$Q = P + t_0B$$, then $$2Q - P = P + 2t_0B$$, a point of $$L$$. By
part 2 of :ref{#line-descriptions} it can be the starting point of $$L$$, and by part 1 the
direction $$-B$$ gives the same line as $$B$$. So $$L' = L$$.

**If $$L' = L$$**, then $$2Q - P$$ is on $$L$$, so $$2Q - P = P + uB$$ for some $$u$$. Adding $$P$$
and halving gives $$Q = P + \frac{u}{2}B$$, so $$Q$$ is on $$L$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-rules-proved-parallel-gap}

Let $$L$$ be the line $$3x + 4y = 2$$ and $$L'$$ the line $$3x + 4y = 27$$.

1. Check that $$P = (2, -1)$$ and $$Q = (-2, 2)$$ lie on $$L$$. The lines through them perpendicular
   to $$L$$ meet $$L'$$ at $$P'$$ and $$Q'$$. Find $$P'$$ and $$Q'$$, and check that
   $$d(P, P') = d(Q, Q')$$.
2. Prove the rule of :ref{#distance-and-angles/parallel-distance} in $$\mathbb{R}^2$$: for parallel
   lines $$L$$ and $$L'$$ and points $$P$$ and $$Q$$ of $$L$$, with $$P'$$ and $$Q'$$ as in part 1,
   $$d(P, P') = d(Q, Q')$$.

:::solution

1. $$3 \cdot 2 + 4 \cdot (-1) = 2$$ and $$3 \cdot (-2) + 4 \cdot 2 = 2$$, so both are on $$L$$. By
   :ref{#normal-direction}, $$(3, 4)$$ is perpendicular to $$L$$, so the perpendicular through $$P$$
   is $$(2 + 3t, -1 + 4t)$$. Putting it into the equation of $$L'$$ gives
   $$3(2 + 3t) + 4(-1 + 4t) = 2 + 25t = 27$$, so $$t = 1$$ and $$P' = (5, 3)$$. For $$Q$$, in the
   same way, $$3(-2 + 3t) + 4(2 + 4t) = 2 + 25t = 27$$, so $$t = 1$$ and $$Q' = (1, 6)$$. Both
   distances are $$|1 \cdot (3, 4)| = 5$$.
2. Let $$A = (a_1, a_2)$$ be a direction of $$L'$$, and let $$D$$ be a direction of the line through
   $$P$$ perpendicular to $$L$$. By rule 2 of :ref{#perpendicular-rules-proved}, that line is
   perpendicular to $$L'$$ too, so $$D \cdot A = 0$$, and by rule 3 it meets $$L'$$, at $$P'$$.
   Since $$P'$$ is on it, $$P' = P + kD$$ for some $$k$$, so
   $$(P - P') \cdot A = -k\,(D \cdot A) = 0$$ (:ref{#dot-rules}). By :ref{#closest-point}, $$P'$$ is
   the closest point of $$L'$$ to $$P$$, so $$d(P, P')$$ is the distance from $$P$$ to $$L'$$. The
   same goes for $$Q$$ and $$Q'$$.

   Since $$L \parallel L'$$, the direction $$A$$ is a direction of $$L$$ too
   (:ref{#parallel-formula} and part 1 of :ref{#line-descriptions}). So by :ref{#line-equation},
   $$L'$$ is $$a_2x - a_1y = c'$$ and $$L$$ is $$a_2x - a_1y = c$$ for some numbers $$c$$ and
   $$c'$$. Every point $$(x_0, y_0)$$ of $$L$$ has $$a_2x_0 - a_1y_0 = c$$, so by
   :ref{#distance-to-line} its distance to $$L'$$ is

   ```math
   \frac{|c - c'|}{\sqrt{a_1^2 + a_2^2}}
   ```

   This doesn't depend on the point, so $$d(P, P') = d(Q, Q')$$. In part 1 it is
   $$\frac{|2 - 27|}{5} = 5$$.

:::
