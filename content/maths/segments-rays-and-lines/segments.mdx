Imagine watching a download bar fill up on a screen. At $$25\%$$ the coloured part reaches a quarter
of the way along the bar, and at $$50\%$$ it reaches the middle. Each percentage picks out one spot
on the bar, and as the number climbs from $$0$$ to $$100$$ that spot sweeps from one end to the
other, missing nothing.

The bar is a segment, and the percentage says how far along it you are. In this guide we'll describe
every point of a segment by one formula, with a number between $$0$$ and $$1$$ saying how far along
the point is, and then add a direction of travel to turn a segment into an arrow.

## The points of a segment

To get from a point $$P$$ to a point $$Q$$, you take the step $$Q - P$$
(:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/difference-step}). A quarter of that step takes you a quarter of
the way, and half of it takes you halfway. So each point of the segment is $$P$$ plus some fraction
of the step, and the fraction says how far along it is.

For example, take $$P = (1, 1)$$ and $$Q = (5, 4)$$. The step from $$P$$ to $$Q$$ is
$$Q - P = (4, 3)$$. A quarter of it is $$(1, 0.75)$$, which lands at $$(2, 1.75)$$. Half of it is
$$(2, 1.5)$$, which lands at $$(3, 2.5)$$, and three quarters of it lands at $$(4, 3.25)$$.

```plane
point P 1 1 se
point Q 5 4 se
point X 2 1.75 nw
point M 3 2.5 nw
point Y 4 3.25 nw
segment P Q color=blue
caption "The segment from P = (1, 1) to Q = (5, 4), with X = (2, 1.75), M = (3, 2.5) and Y = (4, 3.25) a quarter, a half and three quarters of the way along"
```

:::definition[Segment in coordinates]{#segment-formula}

Let $$P$$ and $$Q$$ be different points of $$\mathbb{R}^2$$. The **segment** $$PQ$$ is made of the
points

```math
P + t(Q - P) \qquad \text{for every number } t \text{ with } 0 \le t \le 1
```

The point for $$t = 0$$ is $$P$$, the point for $$t = 1$$ is $$Q$$, and the point for
$$t = \frac{1}{2}$$ is $$\frac{1}{2}(P + Q)$$, the midpoint of
:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/midpoint-formula}.

:::

The three named points follow from the rules for adding and scaling
(:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/sum-rules}, :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/scale-sum-rules},
:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/scale-twice}). For $$t = 0$$ the step is $$0(Q - P) = O$$, which
leaves $$P$$. For $$t = 1$$ it is the whole step, and $$P + (Q - P) = Q$$ by
:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/difference-step}.

The same rules rewrite the formula in a second way. Multiplying out,

```math
P + t(Q - P) = P + tQ - tP = (1 - t)P + tQ
```

so the point a fraction $$t$$ of the way along is $$(1 - t)P + tQ$$. With $$t = \frac{1}{2}$$ this
is $$\frac{1}{2}P + \frac{1}{2}Q = \frac{1}{2}(P + Q)$$, the midpoint.

Drag the slider for $$t$$ and watch the point $$X = P + t(Q - P)$$ move. Between $$0$$ and $$1$$ it
stays on the segment. Push $$t$$ below $$0$$ or above $$1$$, and the same formula carries on along
the line through $$P$$ and $$Q$$, past one end or the other.

## How far along

A point a quarter of the way from $$P$$ to $$Q$$ should be a quarter of the distance from $$P$$, and
three quarters of the distance from $$Q$$. The formula gives that, because the step from $$P$$ to
the point is $$t$$ times the whole step, and scaling a step scales its length
(:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/norm-scale}).

In the example, $$Q - P = (4, 3)$$ has length $$\sqrt{16 + 9} = 5$$. The point $$X = (2, 1.75)$$ is
a step of $$(1, 0.75)$$ from $$P$$, of length $$\sqrt{1 + 0.5625} = 1.25$$, which is
$$\frac{1}{4} \cdot 5$$. The step from $$X$$ to $$Q$$ is $$(3, 2.25)$$, of length
$$3.75 = \frac{3}{4} \cdot 5$$.

:::proposition[The point a fraction of the way along]{#fraction-along}

Let $$P$$ and $$Q$$ be different points, let $$0 \le t \le 1$$, and let $$X = P + t(Q - P)$$. Then

```math
d(P, X) = t\,d(P, Q) \qquad d(X, Q) = (1 - t)\,d(P, Q)
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Section_formula#Internal_divisions"}

By :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/difference-step}, $$X - P$$ is the only step that takes $$P$$ to
$$X$$, and $$t(Q - P)$$ is such a step, so $$X - P = t(Q - P)$$. Since $$t \ge 0$$, we have
$$|t| = t$$, so by :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/distance-norm} and
:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/norm-scale},

```math
d(P, X) = |t(Q - P)| = t\,|Q - P| = t\,d(P, Q)
```

For the other part, write $$X = (1 - t)P + tQ$$. By the rules for adding and scaling
(:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/sum-rules}, :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/scale-sum-rules},
:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/scale-twice}),

```math
Q - X = Q - tQ - (1 - t)P = (1 - t)Q - (1 - t)P = (1 - t)(Q - P)
```

Since $$t \le 1$$, the number $$1 - t$$ is not negative, so $$|1 - t| = 1 - t$$ and in the same way
$$d(X, Q) = (1 - t)\,d(P, Q)$$.

:::

Adding the two distances gives $$t\,d(P, Q) + (1 - t)\,d(P, Q) = d(P, Q)$$, so going from $$P$$ to
$$Q$$ by way of $$X$$ is exactly as long as going straight. The proposition also says that different
values of $$t$$ give different points, since they give different distances from $$P$$. So each point
of the segment has one value of $$t$$, and $$t = \frac{d(P, X)}{d(P, Q)}$$ says how far along it is.

### The formula and the picture

Distance and angles described the segment $$PQ$$ as the straight piece of a line from $$P$$ to $$Q$$
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/segment}), and took its rules from the picture
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/segment-rules}). Using those rules, the formula gives exactly the same
points as the picture. Each point $$X = P + t(Q - P)$$ has $$d(P, X) + d(X, Q) = d(P, Q)$$, so by
rule 1 it lies on the drawn segment.

The other way round, take any point $$Y$$ of the drawn segment, at distance $$c$$ from $$P$$. Then
$$0 \le c \le d(P, Q)$$, and rule 2 says $$Y$$ is the only point of the drawn segment at that
distance. With $$t = \frac{c}{d(P, Q)}$$, the point $$X = P + t(Q - P)$$ is on the drawn segment and
also at distance $$c$$ from $$P$$, by :ref{#fraction-along}. So $$X = Y$$, and every point of the
drawn segment comes from the formula.

That check leans on the picture. From now on, in $$\mathbb{R}^2$$, :ref{#segment-formula} is the
definition of a segment, in the same way that the Coordinates chapter made distance a formula
(:ref{#coordinates/coordinate-plane}). The rules for segments then become things to prove, and
:ref{#segment-rules-proved} proves them for the formula.

## Segments either way round

Walking from the bus stop to the shop covers the same stretch of pavement as walking back. A point a
quarter of the way along on the way there is three quarters of the way along on the way back. So the
segment from $$P$$ to $$Q$$ and the segment from $$Q$$ to $$P$$ should be the same points, with
$$t$$ and $$1 - t$$ trading places.

:::proposition[Segments either way round]{#segment-symmetric}

Let $$P$$ and $$Q$$ be different points. The segment $$PQ$$ and the segment $$QP$$ are made of the
same points. The point of $$PQ$$ for the number $$t$$ is the point of $$QP$$ for the number
$$1 - t$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_segment#In_real_or_complex_vector_spaces"}

By :ref{#segment-formula}, written in its second form, the point of $$PQ$$ for $$t$$ is
$$(1 - t)P + tQ$$, and the point of $$QP$$ for a number $$s$$ is $$(1 - s)Q + sP$$. Taking
$$s = 1 - t$$, the second is

```math
\big(1 - (1 - t)\big)Q + (1 - t)P = tQ + (1 - t)P = (1 - t)P + tQ
```

by :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/sum-rules}, which is the first. When $$t$$ runs from $$0$$ to
$$1$$, so does $$1 - t$$. So every point of $$PQ$$ is a point of $$QP$$, and every point of $$QP$$,
with $$s$$ between $$0$$ and $$1$$, is the point of $$PQ$$ for $$t = 1 - s$$.

:::

For example, with $$P = (1, 1)$$ and $$Q = (5, 4)$$, the point a quarter of the way from $$P$$ is
$$(2, 1.75)$$. From $$Q$$, the step to $$P$$ is $$(-4, -3)$$, and three quarters of it is
$$(-3, -2.25)$$, which lands at $$(5, 4) + (-3, -2.25) = (2, 1.75)$$, the same point.

## Dividing a segment into equal pieces

To cut a segment into five equal pieces, mark the points a fifth, two fifths, three fifths and four
fifths of the way along. Each piece goes from one value of $$t$$ to the next, a fifth more, so by
:ref{#fraction-along} each piece has a fifth of the whole length.

:::example[Points a third and two thirds of the way]

Take $$P = (-2, 1)$$ and $$Q = (4, 10)$$. The step is $$Q - P = (6, 9)$$, and a third of it is
$$(2, 3)$$. So the point a third of the way along is $$(-2, 1) + (2, 3) = (0, 4)$$, and the point
two thirds of the way along is $$(-2, 1) + (4, 6) = (2, 7)$$.

The whole segment has length $$\sqrt{36 + 81} = \sqrt{117} = 3\sqrt{13}$$. The first point is
$$|(2, 3)| = \sqrt{13}$$ from $$P$$, a third of that, as :ref{#fraction-along} says.

:::

:::example[Fence posts]

A straight fence runs from a post at $$P = (2, 1)$$ to a post at $$Q = (12, 6)$$, in metres, and
four more posts go in between to split it into five equal lengths. The step is $$(10, 5)$$, and a
fifth of it is $$(2, 1)$$. So the posts stand at the points for $$t = 0$$, $$t = \frac{1}{5}$$ and
so on up to $$t = 1$$, which are

```math
(2, 1) \quad (4, 2) \quad (6, 3) \quad (8, 4) \quad (10, 5) \quad (12, 6)
```

Each gap is a step of $$(2, 1)$$, of length $$\sqrt{5} \approx 2.24$$ m, and five gaps make the
whole length $$\sqrt{125} = 5\sqrt{5} \approx 11.18$$ m.

:::

```plane
point P 2 1 se
point F1 4 2 se
point F2 6 3 se
point F3 8 4 se
point F4 10 5 se
point Q 12 6 se
segment P Q color=blue
ticks P F1 1
ticks F1 F2 1
ticks F2 F3 1
ticks F3 F4 1
ticks F4 Q 1
caption "Six fence posts from P = (2, 1) to Q = (12, 6), at t = 0, 1/5, 2/5, 3/5, 4/5 and 1, split the fence into five gaps of √5 m"
```

## Blending between two positions

Animation software fills in movement with the formula $$(1 - t)P + tQ$$. An animator draws a ball at
its start position in one keyframe and at its end position in a later one, and the software fills in
the frames between. Over ten frames, frame $$k$$ shows the ball at $$(1 - t)P + tQ$$ with
$$t = \frac{k}{10}$$.

Read the formula as a blend: $$1 - t$$ of the start mixed with $$t$$ of the end. Say the ball starts
at $$P = (40, 300)$$ and ends at $$Q = (440, 100)$$, in pixels from the bottom left corner of the
screen. Frame $$3$$ is a $$70\%$$ to $$30\%$$ blend, so the ball is at

```math
0.7(40, 300) + 0.3(440, 100) = (28, 210) + (132, 30) = (160, 240)
```

Each frame is a step of $$\frac{1}{10}(Q - P)$$ on from the one before, so by
:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/norm-scale} consecutive frames are a tenth of the whole path apart,
and the ball moves at a steady speed. Animators call this filling in "tweening", short for
in-betweening.

## Arrows

A segment has no start and no end, since $$PQ$$ and $$QP$$ are the same points
(:ref{#segment-symmetric}). A step does have a way of going: the step from $$(1, 1)$$ to $$(5, 4)$$
is $$(4, 3)$$, and the step back is $$(-4, -3)$$. An arrow keeps track of which end comes first.

Two arrows can also have the same step without sharing any points. The arrow from $$(1, 1)$$ to
$$(5, 4)$$ and the arrow from $$(-4, 2)$$ to $$(0, 5)$$ both have step $$(4, 3)$$: they are the same
move, made from two different places.

:::definition[Arrow]{#arrow}

Let $$P$$ and $$Q$$ be points. The **arrow** $$\overrightarrow{PQ}$$, read "the arrow from $$P$$ to
$$Q$$", is the pair of points $$P$$ and $$Q$$ in that order, drawn from its **start** $$P$$ to its
**end** $$Q$$. Its **step** is $$Q - P$$.

Two arrows are **equivalent** when they have the same step.

:::

Order matters for arrows. The arrow $$\overrightarrow{QP}$$ has step $$P - Q = -(Q - P)$$, so it is
not equivalent to $$\overrightarrow{PQ}$$ unless $$P = Q$$.

Arrows are what slides move by. :ref{#isometries/translation} defines the translation by the arrow
from $$O$$ to $$M$$. In coordinates that arrow has step $$M - O = M$$, and adding $$M$$ to every
point is the slide $$T_M$$ of :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/slide-formula}. The proof that $$T_M$$
moves each point in the direction of the arrow is :ref{#slide-direction}, in
[Rays and direction](./rays.mdx).

### Equivalent arrows and parallelograms

Draw two equivalent arrows and join their starts and their ends. The four points make a
parallelogram, since the two arrows are opposite sides that point the same way and have the same
length. In a parallelogram the two diagonals cross at their midpoints, and that gives a test with no
subtraction in it.

:::proposition[Equivalent arrows make a parallelogram]{#arrow-equivalent}

For points $$P$$, $$Q$$, $$M$$ and $$N$$, the following say the same thing:

1. $$\overrightarrow{PQ}$$ and $$\overrightarrow{MN}$$ are equivalent
2. $$N = M + (Q - P)$$
3. $$P + N = Q + M$$

When $$P \ne N$$ and $$Q \ne M$$, part 3 says that the segments $$PN$$ and $$QM$$ have the same
midpoint.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equipollence_(geometry)#Parallelogram_property"}

**Parts 1 and 2.** The arrows are equivalent when $$N - M = Q - P$$. By
:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/difference-step}, $$N - M$$ is the only point $$X$$ with
$$M + X = N$$. So $$N - M = Q - P$$ exactly when $$M + (Q - P) = N$$.

**Parts 2 and 3.** If $$N = M + (Q - P)$$, adding $$P$$ to both sides gives
$$P + N = M + Q = Q + M$$, by :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/sum-rules}. If $$P + N = Q + M$$,
subtracting $$P$$ from both sides gives $$N = Q + M - P = M + (Q - P)$$.

**The midpoints.** By :ref{#segment-formula}, the midpoints of $$PN$$ and $$QM$$ are
$$\frac{1}{2}(P + N)$$ and $$\frac{1}{2}(Q + M)$$. If $$P + N = Q + M$$, halving both sides makes
the midpoints equal. If the midpoints are equal, doubling both sides
(:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/scale-twice}) gives $$P + N = Q + M$$.

:::

```plane
point P 1 1 se
point Q 5 4 se
point M -4 2 sw
point N 0 5 nw
point C 0.5 3 s
segment P Q color=blue
segment M N color=blue
segment P M dashed color=muted
segment Q N dashed color=muted
segment P N dashed color=orange
segment Q M dashed color=orange
caption "The arrows from P = (1, 1) to Q = (5, 4) and from M = (−4, 2) to N = (0, 5) both have step (4, 3). The diagonals PN and QM cross at their common midpoint C = (0.5, 3)."
```

:::example[Finding the end of an arrow]

Find the point $$N$$ that makes $$\overrightarrow{MN}$$ equivalent to $$\overrightarrow{PQ}$$, for
$$P = (1, 1)$$, $$Q = (5, 4)$$ and $$M = (-4, 2)$$.

By :ref{#arrow-equivalent}, $$N = M + (Q - P) = (-4, 2) + (4, 3) = (0, 5)$$. As a check,
$$P + N = (1, 6)$$ and $$Q + M = (1, 6)$$, so the diagonals $$PN$$ and $$QM$$ share the midpoint
$$(0.5, 3)$$.

:::

## Sliding and zooming a segment

Slide a ruler across a table and every mark on it moves by the same step. The two ends land in new
places, and the mark a quarter of the way along is still a quarter of the way along. So the slide of
a segment is the segment between the slid ends, and each point keeps its value of $$t$$. A zoom
about the origin stretches every step by the same factor, and a segment goes to a segment in the
same way.

For example, slide the segment from $$P = (1, 1)$$ to $$Q = (5, 4)$$ by $$A = (2, -3)$$. Its ends go
to $$(3, -2)$$ and $$(7, 1)$$. The point $$(2, 1.75)$$ a quarter of the way along goes to
$$(4, -1.25)$$, and a quarter of the step $$(4, 3)$$ from $$(3, -2)$$ lands there too.

:::proposition[Slides move segments]{#segment-slide}

Let $$P$$ and $$Q$$ be different points, let $$A$$ be a point and let $$r > 0$$.

1. **Slides**: $$T_A$$ sends the segment $$PQ$$ onto the segment from $$P + A$$ to $$Q + A$$, and it
   sends the point for $$t$$ to the point for $$t$$
2. **Arrows**: $$T_A$$ sends each arrow $$\overrightarrow{PQ}$$ to the equivalent arrow from
   $$P + A$$ to $$Q + A$$
3. **Zooms**: the zoom $$Z_r(X) = rX$$ about the origin sends the segment $$PQ$$ onto the segment
   from $$rP$$ to $$rQ$$, and the point for $$t$$ to the point for $$t$$

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_transformation#Properties_preserved"}

**Slides.** First, $$P + A \ne Q + A$$, since adding $$-A$$ to both sides of $$P + A = Q + A$$ would
give $$P = Q$$. Working coordinate by coordinate, $$(Q + A) - (P + A) = Q - P$$. So for
$$0 \le t \le 1$$,

```math
T_A\big(P + t(Q - P)\big) = (P + A) + t(Q - P) = (P + A) + t\big((Q + A) - (P + A)\big)
```

by :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/sum-rules}. The right side is the point for $$t$$ of the segment
from $$P + A$$ to $$Q + A$$ (:ref{#segment-formula}). So every point of $$PQ$$ lands on the new
segment, and every point of the new segment, with its own $$t$$, is the image of the point of $$PQ$$
for that $$t$$.

**Arrows.** The image arrow has step $$(Q + A) - (P + A) = Q - P$$, the step of
$$\overrightarrow{PQ}$$, so the two arrows are equivalent.

**Zooms.** First, $$rP \ne rQ$$ by :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/scale-cancel}, since $$r \ne 0$$.
By :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/scale-sum-rules} and
:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/scale-twice},

```math
r\big(P + t(Q - P)\big) = rP + (rt)(Q - P) = rP + t(rQ - rP)
```

which is the point for $$t$$ of the segment from $$rP$$ to $$rQ$$. As for slides, every point of
each segment is matched with the point of the other for the same $$t$$.

:::

Part 3 proves the half of rule 2 of :ref{#area-and-scaling/zoom-rules} that is about segments, for
zooms about the origin written as formulas. :ref{#moves-keep-lines} in
[The rules for lines, proved](./rules-proved.mdx) does the same for lines, and for the other moves
of the Adding and scaling points chapter. With $$r = 2$$, the zoom sends the segment from $$(1, 1)$$
to $$(5, 4)$$ onto the segment from $$(2, 2)$$ to $$(10, 8)$$, and the quarter point $$(2, 1.75)$$
onto $$(4, 3.5)$$, which is $$(2, 2) + \frac{1}{4}(8, 6)$$.

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on segments, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-segments-points}

Let $$P = (-3, 2)$$ and $$Q = (5, -2)$$.

1. Find the points a quarter, a half and three quarters of the way from $$P$$ to $$Q$$.
2. Find $$d(P, Q)$$, and check :ref{#fraction-along} for the point a quarter of the way along.

:::solution

1. The step is $$Q - P = (8, -4)$$, and a quarter of it is $$(2, -1)$$. So by :ref{#segment-formula}
   the three points are $$(-3, 2) + (2, -1) = (-1, 1)$$, $$(-3, 2) + (4, -2) = (1, 0)$$ and
   $$(-3, 2) + (6, -3) = (3, -1)$$.
2. $$d(P, Q) = \sqrt{64 + 16} = \sqrt{80} = 4\sqrt{5}$$. The quarter point is a step of $$(2, -1)$$
   from $$P$$, of length $$\sqrt{5}$$, which is $$\frac{1}{4} \cdot 4\sqrt{5}$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-segments-on-or-off}

Let $$P = (1, 1)$$ and $$Q = (9, 5)$$. For each point, decide whether it lies on the segment $$PQ$$,
and if it does, find its value of $$t$$.

1. $$(7, 4)$$
2. $$(5, 4)$$
3. $$(13, 7)$$

:::solution

The step is $$Q - P = (8, 4)$$. A point $$X$$ is on the segment exactly when $$X - P = t(8, 4)$$ for
some $$t$$ with $$0 \le t \le 1$$ (:ref{#segment-formula},
:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/difference-step}).

1. $$(7, 4) - P = (6, 3)$$. The first coordinate gives $$t = \frac{6}{8} = \frac{3}{4}$$ and the
   second gives $$t = \frac{3}{4}$$ too, so the point is on the segment, three quarters of the way
   along.
2. $$(5, 4) - P = (4, 3)$$. The first coordinate needs $$t = \frac{1}{2}$$ and the second needs
   $$t = \frac{3}{4}$$, so no single $$t$$ works and the point is not on the segment.
3. $$(13, 7) - P = (12, 6) = 1.5(8, 4)$$. So $$t = 1.5$$, which is more than $$1$$: the point is on
   the line through $$P$$ and $$Q$$, past $$Q$$, but not on the segment.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-segments-thirds}

Split the segment from $$(-1, 4)$$ to $$(11, -5)$$ into three equal pieces. Find the two points
where it is cut, and the length of each piece.

:::solution

The step is $$(12, -9)$$, and a third of it is $$(4, -3)$$. So the cuts are at
$$(-1, 4) + (4, -3) = (3, 1)$$ and $$(-1, 4) + (8, -6) = (7, -2)$$. The whole segment has length
$$\sqrt{144 + 81} = 15$$, so by :ref{#fraction-along} each piece has length $$5$$, which is
$$|(4, -3)|$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-segments-find-end}

The point $$M = (2, 3)$$ is two fifths of the way from $$P = (-2, 1)$$ to a point $$Q$$. Find $$Q$$.

:::solution

By :ref{#segment-formula}, $$M = P + \frac{2}{5}(Q - P)$$, so
$$\frac{2}{5}(Q - P) = M - P = (4, 2)$$ (:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/difference-step}).
Multiplying by $$\frac{5}{2}$$ (:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/scale-twice}) gives
$$Q - P = (10, 5)$$, so $$Q = (-2, 1) + (10, 5) = (8, 6)$$.

Check: $$(-2, 1) + \frac{2}{5}(10, 5) = (-2, 1) + (4, 2) = (2, 3)$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-segments-arrows}

Let $$P = (2, -1)$$, $$Q = (5, 3)$$ and $$M = (-1, 0)$$.

1. Find $$N$$ so that $$\overrightarrow{MN}$$ is equivalent to $$\overrightarrow{PQ}$$, and check
   that $$P + N = Q + M$$.
2. Find the step of $$\overrightarrow{QP}$$, and explain why it is not equivalent to
   $$\overrightarrow{PQ}$$.
3. Show that whenever $$\overrightarrow{PQ}$$ and $$\overrightarrow{MN}$$ are equivalent, so are
   $$\overrightarrow{PM}$$ and $$\overrightarrow{QN}$$, for any four points.

:::solution

1. By :ref{#arrow-equivalent}, $$N = (-1, 0) + (3, 4) = (2, 4)$$. Then $$P + N = (4, 3)$$ and
   $$Q + M = (4, 3)$$.
2. Its step is $$P - Q = (-3, -4)$$, while the step of $$\overrightarrow{PQ}$$ is $$(3, 4)$$. The
   steps differ, so the arrows are not equivalent (:ref{#arrow}). They have the same length, $$5$$,
   but point opposite ways.
3. By :ref{#arrow-equivalent}, $$P + N = Q + M$$. By :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/sum-rules} this
   is the same as $$P + N = M + Q$$, which is part 3 of :ref{#arrow-equivalent} for the arrows
   $$\overrightarrow{PM}$$ and $$\overrightarrow{QN}$$. So they are equivalent too. In the
   parallelogram, these are the other pair of opposite sides.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-segments-moves}

Let $$P = (1, 0)$$ and $$Q = (4, 3)$$, and let $$X$$ be the point a third of the way from $$P$$ to
$$Q$$.

1. Find $$X$$.
2. Slide the segment by $$A = (-2, 5)$$. Find the image of $$X$$, and check that it is a third of
   the way along the image segment.
3. Do the same for the zoom $$Z_2$$ about the origin.

:::solution

1. The step is $$(3, 3)$$, so $$X = (1, 0) + (1, 1) = (2, 1)$$.
2. The ends go to $$(-1, 5)$$ and $$(2, 8)$$, and $$X$$ goes to $$(0, 6)$$. A third of the way from
   $$(-1, 5)$$ to $$(2, 8)$$ is $$(-1, 5) + (1, 1) = (0, 6)$$, the same point, as
   :ref{#segment-slide} says.
3. The ends go to $$(2, 0)$$ and $$(8, 6)$$, and $$X$$ goes to $$(4, 2)$$. A third of the way from
   $$(2, 0)$$ to $$(8, 6)$$ is $$(2, 0) + (2, 2) = (4, 2)$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-segments-midpoint-distance}

Let $$P \ne Q$$, let $$C = \frac{1}{2}(P + Q)$$ be the midpoint of $$PQ$$, and let
$$X = P + t(Q - P)$$ with $$0 \le t \le 1$$. Show that

```math
d(X, C) = \left|t - \tfrac{1}{2}\right|\,d(P, Q)
```

and deduce that the points for $$t$$ and $$1 - t$$ are the same distance from the midpoint.

:::solution

The midpoint is the point for $$\frac{1}{2}$$, so $$C = P + \frac{1}{2}(Q - P)$$
(:ref{#segment-formula}). By the rules for adding and scaling
(:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/sum-rules}, :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/scale-sum-rules}),

```math
X - C = t(Q - P) - \tfrac{1}{2}(Q - P) = \left(t - \tfrac{1}{2}\right)(Q - P)
```

So by :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/distance-norm} and
:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/norm-scale}, $$d(X, C) = \left|t - \frac{1}{2}\right|\,d(P, Q)$$.

For $$1 - t$$ the factor is $$\left|\frac{1}{2} - t\right|$$, which is the same number, since a
number and its negative have the same absolute value. For example, the points a quarter and three
quarters of the way along are both a quarter of the length from the midpoint.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-segments-tween}

In an animation, a ball moves from $$(40, 300)$$ to $$(440, 100)$$ over $$8$$ frames, filled in with
$$(1 - t)P + tQ$$ and $$t = \frac{k}{8}$$ for frame $$k$$.

1. Where is the ball in frame $$3$$?
2. How far does it move from one frame to the next?

:::solution

1. With $$t = \frac{3}{8}$$, the ball is at
   $$\frac{5}{8}(40, 300) + \frac{3}{8}(440, 100) = (25, 187.5) + (165, 37.5) = (190, 225)$$.
2. The whole path has length $$|(400, -200)| = \sqrt{200000} = 200\sqrt{5} \approx 447.2$$ pixels.
   Frame $$k$$ and frame $$k + 1$$ are the points for $$\frac{k}{8}$$ and $$\frac{k + 1}{8}$$, and
   their difference is $$\frac{1}{8}(Q - P)$$ by the rules for adding and scaling
   (:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/scale-sum-rules}). So by
   :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/norm-scale} each frame moves the ball
   $$\frac{1}{8} \cdot 200\sqrt{5} = 25\sqrt{5} \approx 55.9$$ pixels.
