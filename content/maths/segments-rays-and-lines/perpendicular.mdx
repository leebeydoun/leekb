Imagine a builder checking that the corner of a new patio is square. Her set square is far too small
for the job, so she uses the knotted rope from
[Whole-number right triangles](../coordinates/pythagorean-triples.mdx). She pegs out $$3$$ lengths
along one edge and $$4$$ along the other, and if the far ends are $$5$$ lengths apart, the corner is
square. Now suppose the patio exists only on a site plan, with each corner written as a pair of
coordinates. There is no rope to stretch, but the numbers alone should still settle the question.

They do, through a number called the **dot product**, worked out from the two edges. Two lines are
perpendicular exactly when the dot product of their directions is $$0$$. In this guide we'll find
that test from the Pythagoras theorem, and then use it to find the closest point of a line and the
tangent to a circle.

## The number behind the rope test

On the site plan, put the corner of the patio at $$Q = (2, 1)$$, in metres, with the edges running
to $$P = (6, 4)$$ and $$M = (-1, 5)$$. The rope test compares the squares of the two edges with the
square of the distance between their far ends. Here the edges are the steps $$P - Q = (4, 3)$$ and
$$M - Q = (-3, 4)$$, each of length $$5$$, and the far ends are $$P - M = (7, -1)$$ apart. So the
two squares add up to $$25 + 25 = 50$$, the far ends are $$\sqrt{49 + 1} = \sqrt{50}$$ apart, and
the corner is square.

```plane
point Q 2 1 s
point P 6 4 e
point M -1 5 w
segment Q P color=blue label="5" side=right
segment Q M color=orange label="5"
segment P M dashed label="√50"
right P Q M
caption "The corner Q = (2, 1) with edges to P = (6, 4) and M = (−1, 5): 5² + 5² = 50, so the corner is square"
```

Write the two steps as $$A = (a_1, a_2)$$ and $$B = (b_1, b_2)$$. The step between their far ends is
$$A - B$$, and multiplying out its length squared gives

```math
(a_1 - b_1)^2 + (a_2 - b_2)^2 = \left(a_1^2 + a_2^2\right) + \left(b_1^2 + b_2^2\right) - 2\left(a_1b_1 + a_2b_2\right)
```

The first two brackets are $$|A|^2$$ and $$|B|^2$$. So the rope test passes exactly when the last
bracket, $$a_1b_1 + a_2b_2$$, is $$0$$. For the patio it is $$4 \cdot (-3) + 3 \cdot 4 = 0$$.

:::definition[Dot product]{#dot-product}

The **dot product** of two points $$A = (a_1, a_2)$$ and $$B = (b_1, b_2)$$ is the number

```math
A \cdot B = a_1b_1 + a_2b_2
```

read "$$A$$ dot $$B$$". It is also called the **scalar product**.

:::

The dot product of two points is a number, not a point, and it can be negative. For example,
$$(3, 1) \cdot (-2, 5) = -6 + 5 = -1$$, and $$(4, 3) \cdot (4, 3) = 16 + 9 = 25$$. You have met this
number before without its name: :ref{#coordinates/product-bound} showed that
$$A \cdot B \le \lvert A \rvert\,\lvert B \rvert$$, which is how the triangle inequality was proved.

## How the dot product works

The dot product multiplies matching coordinates and adds. So it follows the same rules as ordinary
multiplication of numbers: the order doesn't matter, it shares out over a sum, and a number can be
moved out in front. A point dotted with itself gives the sum of the squares of its coordinates,
which is its length squared.

:::proposition[How the dot product works]{#dot-rules}

For all points $$A$$, $$B$$ and $$C$$ and every number $$c$$:

1. **Order**: $$A \cdot B = B \cdot A$$
2. **Sums**: $$A \cdot (B + C) = A \cdot B + A \cdot C$$
3. **Multiples**: $$(cA) \cdot B = c\,(A \cdot B)$$
4. **Length**: $$A \cdot A = |A|^2$$

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dot_product#Properties"}

Write $$A = (a_1, a_2)$$, $$B = (b_1, b_2)$$ and $$C = (c_1, c_2)$$. Each part is the matching rule
for numbers, applied to each coordinate.

1. $$A \cdot B = a_1b_1 + a_2b_2 = b_1a_1 + b_2a_2 = B \cdot A$$
2. $$B + C = (b_1 + c_1, b_2 + c_2)$$ by :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/point-sum}, so
   $$A \cdot (B + C) = a_1b_1 + a_1c_1 + a_2b_2 + a_2c_2 = A \cdot B + A \cdot C$$
3. $$cA = (ca_1, ca_2)$$ by :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/scalar-multiple}, so
   $$(cA) \cdot B = ca_1b_1 + ca_2b_2 = c\,(a_1b_1 + a_2b_2)$$
4. $$A \cdot A = a_1^2 + a_2^2$$. By :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/norm},
   $$|A| = \sqrt{a_1^2 + a_2^2}$$, and the square of the square root of a number that is not
   negative is that number. So $$|A|^2 = a_1^2 + a_2^2$$.

:::

Parts 1 and 3 together move a number out of either side: $$A \cdot (cB) = c\,(A \cdot B)$$. With
these rules, the calculation behind the rope test fits in one line, written with points instead of
coordinates.

:::proposition[The length of a difference]{#difference-length}

For all points $$A$$ and $$B$$,

```math
|A - B|^2 = |A|^2 + |B|^2 - 2\,A \cdot B
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dot_product#Application_to_the_law_of_cosines"}

By part 4 of :ref{#dot-rules}, $$|A - B|^2 = (A - B) \cdot (A - B)$$. Write $$A - B$$ as
$$A + (-1)B$$ and share out with parts 1, 2 and 3:

```math
(A - B) \cdot (A - B) = A \cdot A - A \cdot B - B \cdot A + B \cdot B = |A|^2 - 2\,A \cdot B + |B|^2
```

using part 1 to see that $$B \cdot A = A \cdot B$$, and part 4 for the two squares.

:::

## A right angle from the dot product

The rope test works on a triangle, with two edges leaving one corner. Put that corner at the origin,
so the edges are the steps $$A$$ and $$B$$. They make a triangle as long as the second step doesn't
point along the line of the first, forwards or backwards. When it does, $$B$$ is a multiple of
$$A$$, and the dot product is never $$0$$: for example $$(2, 1) \cdot (6, 3) = 12 + 3 = 15$$.

:::theorem[A right angle from the dot product]{#perpendicular-dot}

Let $$A$$ and $$B$$ be points other than $$O$$.

1. If $$B = cA$$ for some number $$c$$, then $$A \cdot B \ne 0$$.
2. If $$O$$, $$A$$ and $$B$$ don't lie on one line, then the angle at $$O$$ in the triangle with
   corners $$O$$, $$A$$ and $$B$$ is a right angle exactly when $$A \cdot B = 0$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Dot_Product_of_Perpendicular_Vectors"}

**Part 1.** Since $$B \ne O$$, the number $$c$$ is not $$0$$, because $$0A = O$$. By parts 3 and 4
of :ref{#dot-rules}, $$A \cdot B = A \cdot (cA) = c\,|A|^2$$. Since $$A \ne O$$, $$|A|^2 > 0$$
(:ref{#coordinates/distance-zero}), so $$c\,|A|^2 \ne 0$$.

**Part 2.** By :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/distance-norm}, the sides of the triangle have
lengths $$d(O, A) = |A|$$, $$d(O, B) = |B|$$ and $$d(A, B) = |A - B|$$. By :ref{#difference-length},

```math
|A|^2 + |B|^2 = |A - B|^2 \quad\text{exactly when}\quad A \cdot B = 0
```

If the angle at $$O$$ is a right angle, the sides $$OA$$ and $$OB$$ are the legs and $$AB$$ is the
hypotenuse. By :ref{#distance-and-angles/pythagoras}, $$|A|^2 + |B|^2 = |A - B|^2$$, so
$$A \cdot B = 0$$. Conversely, if $$A \cdot B = 0$$, then $$|A|^2 + |B|^2 = |A - B|^2$$, and by
:ref{#coordinates/pythagoras-converse}, with $$O$$, $$A$$ and $$B$$ in place of $$Q$$, $$P$$ and
$$M$$, the angle at $$O$$ is a right angle.

:::

The proof uses only the three distances between the corners. At a corner $$Q$$ other than the
origin, with the other corners at $$P$$ and $$M$$, take $$A = P - Q$$ and $$B = M - Q$$. Then
$$d(Q, P) = |A|$$, $$d(Q, M) = |B|$$ and $$d(P, M) = |A - B|$$, since $$(P - Q) - (M - Q) = P - M$$.
So the same argument shows that the angle at $$Q$$ is a right angle exactly when
$$(P - Q) \cdot (M - Q) = 0$$.

:::example[Checking the patio corner]

For the patio, $$(P - Q) \cdot (M - Q) = (4, 3) \cdot (-3, 4) = 0$$, so the corner at $$Q$$ is
square. Suppose instead the second edge ran to $$M' = (-1, 4)$$. Then
$$(4, 3) \cdot (-3, 3) = -12 + 9 = -3$$, so that corner is not square. The rope agrees: the two
edges squared add up to $$25 + 18 = 43$$, but the far ends are $$\sqrt{49 + 0} = 7$$ apart, and
$$43 \ne 49$$.

:::

A quarter turn should give a right angle, and the dot product confirms it. For $$A = (a_1, a_2)$$,
the quarter turn gives $$G_{90}(A) = (-a_2, a_1)$$ (:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/quarter-turn}),
and

```math
G_{90}(A) \cdot A = -a_2a_1 + a_1a_2 = 0
```

So for $$A \ne O$$, the point $$G_{90}(A)$$ is not a multiple of $$A$$, by part 1 of
:ref{#perpendicular-dot}, and the angle at $$O$$ between $$OA$$ and $$OG_{90}(A)$$ is a right angle,
by part 2. That proves part 2 of :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/quarter-turn} a second way.

## Perpendicular lines in coordinates

Two lines are perpendicular when they cross square on. A line in $$\mathbb{R}^2$$ is given by a
point and a direction (:ref{#line-formula}), and the direction says which way the line runs. So
whether two lines cross square on depends only on their two directions, and the dot product of the
directions is the test.

:::definition[Perpendicular in coordinates]{#perpendicular-formula}

Two lines with directions $$A$$ and $$B$$ are **perpendicular** when $$A \cdot B = 0$$. You write
$$L \perp K$$ when the lines $$L$$ and $$K$$ are perpendicular.

:::

A line has many directions, so the definition needs a check: the answer shouldn't depend on which
directions you pick. By part 3 of :ref{#line-descriptions}, every direction of a line with direction
$$A$$ is a multiple $$cA$$ with $$c \ne 0$$. In the same way every direction of the second line is a
multiple $$dB$$ with $$d \ne 0$$. By parts 1 and 3 of :ref{#dot-rules},
$$(cA) \cdot (dB) = cd\,(A \cdot B)$$, which is $$0$$ exactly when $$A \cdot B = 0$$.

Perpendicular lines are never parallel. If $$L \parallel K$$, then $$B = cA$$
(:ref{#parallel-formula}), and $$A \cdot B \ne 0$$ by part 1 of :ref{#perpendicular-dot}. So two
perpendicular lines meet in exactly one point, by :ref{#line-intersection}. In particular, no line
is perpendicular to itself, since $$A \cdot A = |A|^2 > 0$$.

The definition agrees with the picture's perpendicular lines
(:ref{#distance-and-angles/perpendicular}): take the lines through $$O$$ with directions $$A$$ and
$$B$$, where $$A \cdot B = 0$$. By part 1 of :ref{#perpendicular-dot}, $$B$$ is not a multiple of
$$A$$, so it is not on the line through $$O$$ and $$A$$ (:ref{#multiples-line}). So none of the
three points lies on the segment joining the other two, and by :ref{#triangle-equality} no distance
between them is the sum of the other two: they make a true triangle. By part 2, the triangle $$O$$,
$$A$$, $$B$$ has a right angle at $$O$$, so the two lines cross at $$O$$ square on. The guide
[The rules for lines, proved](./rules-proved.mdx) shows that the rules for perpendicular lines hold
for this definition (:ref{#perpendicular-rules-proved}).

For example, the horizontal line $$y = 2$$ has direction $$(1, 0)$$ and the vertical line $$x = 5$$
has direction $$(0, 1)$$, and $$(1, 0) \cdot (0, 1) = 0$$. So every horizontal line is perpendicular
to every vertical line, as rule 3 of :ref{#coordinates/coordinate-rules} takes from the picture. The
quarter turn gives a perpendicular to any line: the line $$P + tA$$ is perpendicular to every line
with direction $$G_{90}(A)$$.

## Perpendicular lines from their equations

In an equation $$ax + by = c$$, the pair $$(a, b)$$ points straight across the line. The line runs
in the direction $$(-b, a)$$ (:ref{#equation-line}), and swapping the coordinates and changing one
sign is a quarter turn, which makes a right angle. So two lines are perpendicular exactly when their
pairs $$(a, b)$$ have dot product $$0$$.

:::proposition[A perpendicular to an equation]{#normal-direction}

Let $$a$$ and $$b$$ be numbers, not both $$0$$. Every line with direction $$(a, b)$$ is
perpendicular to the line $$ax + by = c$$. Two lines $$ax + by = c$$ and $$a'x + b'y = c'$$ are
perpendicular exactly when

```math
aa' + bb' = 0
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Condition_for_Straight_Lines_in_Plane_to_be_Perpendicular#General_Equation"}

By :ref{#equation-line}, the line $$ax + by = c$$ has direction $$(-b, a)$$, and
$$(a, b) \cdot (-b, a) = -ab + ba = 0$$. So a line with direction $$(a, b)$$ is perpendicular to it
(:ref{#perpendicular-formula}).

For the second part, the two lines have directions $$(-b, a)$$ and $$(-b', a')$$, and

```math
(-b, a) \cdot (-b', a') = bb' + aa'
```

So the lines are perpendicular exactly when $$aa' + bb' = 0$$.

:::

The pair $$(a, b)$$ is called a **normal** to the line $$ax + by = c$$. For example, the lines
$$2x - 3y = 1$$ and $$3x + 2y = 7$$ are perpendicular, since $$2 \cdot 3 + (-3) \cdot 2 = 0$$.

For lines written with slopes, the test becomes a product. The line $$y = mx + k$$ is
$$-mx + y = k$$, with $$a = -m$$ and $$b = 1$$. A second line $$y = m'x + k'$$ gives $$a' = -m'$$
and $$b' = 1$$, so $$aa' + bb' = mm' + 1$$.

:::corollary[Slopes of perpendicular lines]{#perpendicular-slopes}

Two lines that are not vertical, with slopes $$m$$ and $$m'$$, are perpendicular exactly when

```math
mm' = -1
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slope#Algebra_and_geometry"}

By :ref{#linear-equations/slope}, the lines are $$y = mx + k$$ and $$y = m'x + k'$$ for some numbers
$$k$$ and $$k'$$, that is, $$-mx + y = k$$ and $$-m'x + y = k'$$. By :ref{#normal-direction}, they
are perpendicular exactly when $$(-m)(-m') + 1 \cdot 1 = 0$$, that is, when $$mm' = -1$$.

:::

So turning a line square on replaces its slope $$m$$ by $$-\frac{1}{m}$$. The line $$y = 2x + 1$$,
which climbs $$2$$ for each step right, is perpendicular to $$y = -\frac{1}{2}x + 3$$, which drops
$$\frac{1}{2}$$ for each step right: $$2 \cdot \left(-\frac{1}{2}\right) = -1$$. A horizontal line
has slope $$0$$, and no number times $$0$$ is $$-1$$, so its perpendiculars have no slope: they are
the vertical lines.

## The closest point on a line

Stand next to a long straight fence and walk to it by the shortest route. You walk at it square on,
and you reach it at the foot of the perpendicular, as :ref{#distance-and-angles/shortest-distance}
proves. In coordinates, that gives a way to find the foot: it is the point $$F$$ of the line for
which the step from $$F$$ to you has dot product $$0$$ with the direction of the fence.

Take the line through $$P = (1, 2)$$ with direction $$A = (2, 1)$$, and the point $$X = (2, 5)$$. A
point $$P + tA$$ of the line is $$(1 + 2t, 2 + t)$$, and the step from it to $$X$$ is
$$(1 - 2t, 3 - t)$$. Its dot product with $$A$$ is $$2 - 4t + 3 - t = 5 - 5t$$, which is $$0$$
exactly when $$t = 1$$. So the foot is $$F = (3, 3)$$, and $$X$$ is $$|(-1, 2)| = \sqrt{5}$$ from
the line. Move $$X$$ below and watch the foot follow it.

<PerpendicularFoot />

The same calculation works for every line and every point. The dot product of $$X - (P + tA)$$ with
$$A$$ is $$(X - P) \cdot A - t\,|A|^2$$, and that is $$0$$ for exactly one value of $$t$$.

:::theorem[The closest point on a line]{#closest-point}

Let $$L$$ be the line $$P + tA$$, and $$X$$ a point. Let

```math
t_0 = \frac{(X - P) \cdot A}{|A|^2} \qquad F = P + t_0A
```

Then $$F$$ is the only point of $$L$$ with $$(X - F) \cdot A = 0$$. For every point $$Y$$ of $$L$$,

```math
|X - Y|^2 = |X - F|^2 + |F - Y|^2
```

So every point $$Y$$ of $$L$$ other than $$F$$ has $$d(X, Y) > d(X, F)$$: $$F$$ is the closest point
of $$L$$ to $$X$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_projection#Vector_projection_2"}

**Only one point.** For a point $$P + tA$$ of $$L$$, by :ref{#dot-rules},

```math
\big(X - (P + tA)\big) \cdot A = (X - P) \cdot A - t\,(A \cdot A) = (X - P) \cdot A - t\,|A|^2
```

Since $$A \ne O$$, $$|A|^2 > 0$$, so this is $$0$$ exactly when $$t = t_0$$, that is, at the point
$$F$$.

**The sum of squares.** Let $$Y = P + tA$$ be a point of $$L$$, and write $$U = X - F$$ and
$$V = F - Y = (t_0 - t)A$$. Then $$X - Y = U + V$$, and by :ref{#dot-rules},

```math
|U + V|^2 = (U + V) \cdot (U + V) = |U|^2 + 2\,U \cdot V + |V|^2
```

Here $$U \cdot V = (t_0 - t)\,(X - F) \cdot A = 0$$, by the first part. So
$$|X - Y|^2 = |X - F|^2 + |F - Y|^2$$.

**The closest point.** If $$Y \ne F$$, then $$|F - Y| > 0$$, so $$|X - Y|^2 > |X - F|^2$$. Both
lengths are positive or zero, so $$|X - Y| > |X - F|$$, and these are the distances $$d(X, Y)$$ and
$$d(X, F)$$ (:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/distance-norm}).

:::

If $$X$$ is on $$L$$, then $$F = X$$, since $$(X - X) \cdot A = 0$$. If $$X$$ is off $$L$$, the line
through $$X$$ and $$F$$ has direction $$X - F$$ (:ref{#two-point-line}), and $$(X - F) \cdot A = 0$$
says it is perpendicular to $$L$$. So $$F$$ is the foot of the perpendicular from $$X$$, and the
theorem is :ref{#distance-and-angles/shortest-distance} worked out in coordinates. The distance from
$$X$$ to $$L$$ (:ref{#distance-and-angles/point-line-distance}) is $$|X - F|$$.

In the example, with $$Y = P = (1, 2)$$, the theorem says $$|X - P|^2 = 5 + 5$$. The step from $$P$$
to $$X$$ is $$(1, 3)$$, and $$1 + 9 = 10$$.

### Distance from a point to an equation

When the line comes as an equation $$ax + by = c$$, its normal $$(a, b)$$ points straight across it.
So the step from the foot to the point is a multiple of $$(a, b)$$, and finding the distance comes
down to finding that multiple.

:::corollary[Distance from a point to a line]{#distance-to-line}

Let $$a$$ and $$b$$ be numbers, not both $$0$$. The distance from the point $$(x_0, y_0)$$ to the
line $$ax + by = c$$ is

```math
\frac{|ax_0 + by_0 - c|}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}}
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distance_from_a_point_to_a_line#Proofs"}

Write $$X = (x_0, y_0)$$ and $$N = (a, b)$$, so that a point $$Z$$ is on the line exactly when
$$N \cdot Z = c$$. Let $$F$$ be the closest point of the line to $$X$$, from :ref{#closest-point}.
The line has direction $$(-b, a)$$ (:ref{#equation-line}).

**The step from F to X is a multiple of N.** If $$X$$ is on the line, then $$F = X$$, and
$$X - F = O = 0N$$. Otherwise write $$X - F = (v_1, v_2)$$. By :ref{#closest-point},
$$(v_1, v_2) \cdot (-b, a) = 0$$, that is, $$av_2 - bv_1 = 0$$. That is the determinant of $$N$$ and
$$X - F$$, so by :ref{#parallel-determinant}, $$X - F = sN$$ for some number $$s$$, since
$$N \ne O$$.

**Finding s.** Since $$F$$ is on the line, $$N \cdot F = c$$. By :ref{#dot-rules},

```math
N \cdot X - c = N \cdot X - N \cdot F = N \cdot (X - F) = N \cdot (sN) = s\,|N|^2
```

So $$s = \dfrac{ax_0 + by_0 - c}{a^2 + b^2}$$. By :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/norm-scale},

```math
|X - F| = |s|\,|N| = \frac{|ax_0 + by_0 - c|}{a^2 + b^2}\,\sqrt{a^2 + b^2} = \frac{|ax_0 + by_0 - c|}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}}
```

and $$|X - F|$$ is the distance from $$X$$ to the line.

:::

The top of the fraction is what you get by putting the point into the equation and seeing how far it
misses. Points on the line miss by $$0$$, and points further away miss by more.

:::example[How far is the house from the road]

On a map with a grid of $$100$$ m squares, a straight road runs along the line $$3x + 4y = 7$$, and
a house stands at $$(7, 9)$$. Putting the house into the equation gives
$$3 \cdot 7 + 4 \cdot 9 - 7 = 50$$, and $$\sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5$$. So the house is
$$\frac{50}{5} = 10$$ squares from the road, which is $$1$$ km.

The shortest path to the road leaves the house in the direction of the normal $$(3, 4)$$. From the
proof, $$s = \frac{50}{25} = 2$$, so the path meets the road at $$(7, 9) - 2(3, 4) = (1, 1)$$. As a
check, $$3 \cdot 1 + 4 \cdot 1 = 7$$, so $$(1, 1)$$ is on the road, and it is $$|(6, 8)| = 10$$
squares from the house.

:::

## Tangents meet the radius square on

Swing a stone round on a string and let go. The stone flies off along the tangent to its circle,
square on to the string at the moment you let go. The string runs along a radius, so the tangent at
each point of a circle should be perpendicular to the radius there. The dot product turns that into
a short calculation.

The guide [Lines and circles](./lines-and-circles.mdx) found where the line $$Q + tA$$ meets the
circle with centre $$C$$ and radius $$r$$ (:ref{#line-circle}). The number $$k$$ there is
$$a_1(q_1 - c_1) + a_2(q_2 - c_2)$$, which is the dot product $$A \cdot (Q - C)$$. When $$Q$$ is on
the circle, :ref{#on-circle-line} says the line is tangent exactly when $$k = 0$$.

:::proposition[Tangents meet the radius square on]{#tangent-perpendicular}

Let $$Q$$ be a point of the circle with centre $$C$$ and radius $$r > 0$$. A line through $$Q$$ is
tangent to the circle exactly when it is perpendicular to the line $$CQ$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Radius_at_Right_Angle_to_Tangent"}

Write the line as $$Q + tA$$ (:ref{#line-descriptions}). By :ref{#on-circle-line}, it is tangent
exactly when $$k = 0$$, where $$k = a_1(q_1 - c_1) + a_2(q_2 - c_2) = A \cdot (Q - C)$$.

Since $$d(C, Q) = r > 0$$, the points are different, and the line $$CQ$$ has direction $$Q - C$$
(:ref{#two-point-line}). By :ref{#perpendicular-formula}, the line $$Q + tA$$ is perpendicular to
$$CQ$$ exactly when $$A \cdot (Q - C) = 0$$, which is $$k = 0$$.

:::

:::example[A tangent and a line that cuts]

Take the circle with centre $$C = (1, 2)$$ through $$Q = (4, 3)$$. Its radius is
$$|Q - C| = |(3, 1)| = \sqrt{10}$$. The tangent at $$Q$$ has normal $$Q - C = (3, 1)$$, so by
:ref{#normal-direction} its equation is $$3x + y = 3 \cdot 4 + 3 = 15$$, with direction $$(-1, 3)$$.

A line through $$Q$$ in any other direction cuts the circle a second time. With direction
$$(1, 1)$$, $$k = (1, 1) \cdot (3, 1) = 4$$, and :ref{#on-circle-line} puts the second point at
$$t = -\frac{2k}{|A|^2} = -\frac{8}{2} = -4$$, which is $$(4, 3) - 4(1, 1) = (0, -1)$$. As a check,
$$(0 - 1)^2 + (-1 - 2)^2 = 10$$.

:::

1. $$(3, -2)$$ and $$(4, 6)$$
2. $$(5, 1)$$ and $$(-1, 4)$$
3. $$(-2, 7)$$ and $$(7, 2)$$

:::solution

By :ref{#dot-product}:

1. $$3 \cdot 4 + (-2) \cdot 6 = 12 - 12 = 0$$, so these are perpendicular.
2. $$5 \cdot (-1) + 1 \cdot 4 = -5 + 4 = -1$$, so these are not.
3. $$(-2) \cdot 7 + 7 \cdot 2 = -14 + 14 = 0$$, so these are perpendicular. In fact
   $$(-2, 7) = G_{90}(7, 2)$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-perpendicular-right-corner}

The triangle with corners $$(1, 0)$$, $$(5, 2)$$ and $$(0, 2)$$ has a right angle at one corner.
Find which one with dot products, and check your answer with the Pythagoras theorem.

:::solution

At each corner, take the steps to the other two corners and their dot product, as in the paragraph
after :ref{#perpendicular-dot}:

- **At (1, 0)**: the steps are $$(4, 2)$$ and $$(-1, 2)$$, with dot product $$-4 + 4 = 0$$
- **At (5, 2)**: the steps are $$(-4, -2)$$ and $$(-5, 0)$$, with dot product $$20$$
- **At (0, 2)**: the steps are $$(1, -2)$$ and $$(5, 0)$$, with dot product $$5$$

So the right angle is at $$(1, 0)$$. The sides from that corner have squared lengths $$16 + 4 = 20$$
and $$1 + 4 = 5$$, and the third side, from $$(5, 2)$$ to $$(0, 2)$$, has length $$5$$. Indeed
$$20 + 5 = 25 = 5^2$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-perpendicular-line-through}

Find an equation of the line through $$(2, -1)$$ perpendicular to $$2x - 5y = 4$$, and check your
answer with slopes.

:::solution

The line $$2x - 5y = 4$$ has direction $$(5, 2)$$ (:ref{#equation-line}). The line we want has that
pair as its normal, so it is $$5x + 2y = c$$ (:ref{#normal-direction}). It passes through
$$(2, -1)$$, so $$c = 10 - 2 = 8$$, and the line is $$5x + 2y = 8$$. As a check,
$$2 \cdot 5 + (-5) \cdot 2 = 0$$.

With slopes, $$2x - 5y = 4$$ is $$y = \frac{2}{5}x - \frac{4}{5}$$, and $$5x + 2y = 8$$ is
$$y = -\frac{5}{2}x + 4$$. The product of the slopes is $$-1$$, as :ref{#perpendicular-slopes} says
it should be.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-perpendicular-distance}

Find the distance from $$(-3, 4)$$ to the line $$5x - 12y = 2$$, and the point of the line closest
to $$(-3, 4)$$.

:::solution

By :ref{#distance-to-line}, the distance is

```math
\frac{|5 \cdot (-3) - 12 \cdot 4 - 2|}{\sqrt{25 + 144}} = \frac{|-65|}{13} = 5
```

From the proof, the step from the closest point $$F$$ to $$X = (-3, 4)$$ is $$sN$$, with
$$N = (5, -12)$$ and $$s = \frac{-65}{169} = -\frac{5}{13}$$. So

```math
F = X - sN = (-3, 4) + \tfrac{5}{13}(5, -12) = \left(-\tfrac{14}{13}, -\tfrac{8}{13}\right)
```

As a check, putting $$F$$ into the equation gives

```math
5 \cdot \left(-\tfrac{14}{13}\right) - 12 \cdot \left(-\tfrac{8}{13}\right) = \frac{-70 + 96}{13} = 2
```

so $$F$$ is on the line.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-perpendicular-closest}

Find the point of the line $$(0, 3) + t(1, -1)$$ closest to $$X = (5, 4)$$, and the distance from
$$X$$ to the line. Then check the distance with :ref{#distance-to-line}, using the equation
$$x + y = 3$$ of the same line.

:::solution

By :ref{#closest-point}, with $$P = (0, 3)$$ and $$A = (1, -1)$$, the step is $$X - P = (5, 1)$$,
and

```math
t_0 = \frac{(5, 1) \cdot (1, -1)}{|(1, -1)|^2} = \frac{5 - 1}{2} = 2 \qquad F = (0, 3) + 2(1, -1) = (2, 1)
```

The step from $$F$$ to $$X$$ is $$(3, 3)$$, and $$(3, 3) \cdot (1, -1) = 0$$, as it should be. The
distance is $$|(3, 3)| = \sqrt{18} = 3\sqrt{2}$$.

Every point $$(t, 3 - t)$$ of the line has $$x + y = 3$$. By :ref{#distance-to-line}, the distance
is $$\frac{|5 + 4 - 3|}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{6}{\sqrt{2}} = 3\sqrt{2}$$, the same.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-perpendicular-rhombus}

Let $$A$$ and $$B$$ be points other than $$O$$, with $$B$$ not a multiple of $$A$$, so that $$O$$,
$$A$$, $$A + B$$ and $$B$$ are the corners of a parallelogram. Its diagonals run from $$O$$ to
$$A + B$$ and from $$A$$ to $$B$$. Show that the diagonals are perpendicular exactly when
$$|A| = |B|$$, that is, exactly when the parallelogram is a rhombus.

:::solution

The diagonals have directions $$A + B$$ and $$B - A$$. Neither is $$O$$: $$A + B = O$$ would make
$$B = -A$$, and $$B - A = O$$ would make $$B = A$$, both multiples of $$A$$. By :ref{#dot-rules},

```math
(A + B) \cdot (B - A) = A \cdot B - A \cdot A + B \cdot B - B \cdot A = |B|^2 - |A|^2
```

since $$A \cdot B = B \cdot A$$. By :ref{#perpendicular-formula}, the diagonals are perpendicular
exactly when $$|B|^2 = |A|^2$$. Lengths are never negative, so that happens exactly when
$$|A| = |B|$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-perpendicular-tangent}

The circle with centre $$C = (2, -1)$$ and radius $$5$$ passes through $$Q = (5, 3)$$. Find the
equation of its tangent at $$Q$$, and use :ref{#distance-to-line} to check that the tangent is $$5$$
from the centre.

:::solution

First, $$|Q - C| = |(3, 4)| = 5$$, so $$Q$$ is on the circle. By :ref{#tangent-perpendicular}, the
tangent is the line through $$Q$$ perpendicular to $$CQ$$, so $$Q - C = (3, 4)$$ is its normal
(:ref{#normal-direction}). Its equation is $$3x + 4y = c$$ with $$c = 15 + 12 = 27$$, so the tangent
is $$3x + 4y = 27$$.

The distance from $$C$$ to it is

```math
\frac{|3 \cdot 2 + 4 \cdot (-1) - 27|}{\sqrt{9 + 16}} = \frac{|-25|}{5} = 5
```

which is the radius. So the closest point of the tangent to $$C$$ is on the circle, and every other
point of the tangent is further than $$5$$ from $$C$$, outside the circle.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-perpendicular-semicircle}

Let $$M$$ and $$N$$ be the ends of a diameter of a circle with centre $$C$$ and radius $$r$$, so
$$M = C - R$$ and $$N = C + R$$ with $$|R| = r$$. Let $$Q$$ be any other point of the circle. Show
that $$(M - Q) \cdot (N - Q) = 0$$, so the angle at $$Q$$ in the triangle $$MQN$$ is a right angle.

:::solution

Write $$Q = C + V$$, with $$|V| = r$$ since $$Q$$ is on the circle. Then $$M - Q = -R - V$$ and
$$N - Q = R - V$$. By :ref{#dot-rules},

```math
(-R - V) \cdot (R - V) = -R \cdot R + R \cdot V - V \cdot R + V \cdot V = |V|^2 - |R|^2 = r^2 - r^2 = 0
```

since $$R \cdot V = V \cdot R$$. Neither step is $$O$$, since $$Q$$ is not $$M$$ or $$N$$. The dot
product is $$0$$, so by part 1 of :ref{#perpendicular-dot}, $$N - Q$$ is not a multiple of
$$M - Q$$, and $$N$$ is not on the line through $$Q$$ and $$M$$ (:ref{#line-formula}). So $$M$$,
$$Q$$ and $$N$$ make a triangle, and by part 2, taken at the corner $$Q$$ as in the paragraph after
the theorem, its angle at $$Q$$ is a right angle.

:::
