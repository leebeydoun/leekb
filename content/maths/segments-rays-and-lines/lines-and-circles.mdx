  Where a line meets a circle, found by solving one quadratic, and what its discriminant says about
  missing, touching and crossing.

Imagine a straight road that runs past a round lake. Depending on where the road was built, it
passes the lake without coming near the water, or it runs right up to the shore at one spot and
carries on, or it needs a bridge that leaves one bank and lands on the other. You won't find a
straight road that meets the shore at three places.

In the language of this chapter the road is a line and the shore is a circle. A line that touches a
circle at exactly one point is called a **tangent**. In this guide we'll find where any line meets
any circle by solving one quadratic, and read the three cases off its discriminant.

## Putting a line into the circle's equation

Each point of a line is $$P + tA$$ for one number $$t$$, and a point is on a circle exactly when its
distance from the centre is the radius. So asking which points of the line are on the circle is the
same as asking which numbers $$t$$ pass that distance test. Writing the test out leaves a single
equation in $$t$$, and squaring the distance makes it a quadratic.

Take a lake whose shore is the circle with centre $$C = (1, -1)$$ and radius $$5$$, on a map marked
in kilometres. A road runs along the line through $$P = (-7, 5)$$ with direction $$A = (4, -3)$$
(:ref{#line-formula}). Its point with parameter $$t$$ is

```math
X = P + tA = (-7 + 4t,\ 5 - 3t)
```

The step from the centre to $$X$$ is $$X - C = (-8 + 4t,\ 6 - 3t)$$, so by the equation of a circle
(:ref{#coordinates/circle-equation}) the point $$X$$ is on the shore exactly when

```math
(-8 + 4t)^2 + (6 - 3t)^2 = 25
```

Multiplying out, the left side is $$64 - 64t + 16t^2 + 36 - 36t + 9t^2 = 25t^2 - 100t + 100$$. So
the equation is $$25t^2 - 100t + 75 = 0$$, and dividing by $$25$$ gives

```math
t^2 - 4t + 3 = (t - 1)(t - 3) = 0
```

The solutions are $$t = 1$$ and $$t = 3$$. They give the points $$(-3, 2)$$ and $$(5, -4)$$, where
the bridge leaves the shore and lands again. As a check, $$(-3 - 1)^2 + (2 + 1)^2 = 16 + 9 = 25$$,
and $$(5 - 1)^2 + (-4 + 1)^2 = 16 + 9 = 25$$.

The same steps work for every line and every circle. The square of the length $$|A|$$
(:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/norm}) ends up in front of $$t^2$$, and one more number, which
we'll call $$k$$, sits in front of $$2t$$.

:::proposition[Where a line meets a circle]{#line-circle}

Let $$C = (c_1, c_2)$$ and $$r > 0$$, and let $$L$$ be the line through $$P = (p_1, p_2)$$ with
direction $$A = (a_1, a_2)$$. Write

```math
k = a_1(p_1 - c_1) + a_2(p_2 - c_2)
```

Then the point $$P + tA$$ is on the circle with centre $$C$$ and radius $$r$$ exactly when

```math
|A|^2t^2 + 2kt + \left(|P - C|^2 - r^2\right) = 0
```

This is a quadratic in $$t$$, with discriminant
$$4\left(k^2 - |A|^2\left(|P - C|^2 - r^2\right)\right)$$, and different solutions give different
points. So the line meets the circle:

1. in exactly two points, when the discriminant is positive
2. in exactly one point, when the discriminant is $$0$$
3. nowhere, when the discriminant is negative

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line%E2%80%93sphere_intersection"}

By :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/scalar-multiple} and :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/point-sum},
$$P + tA = (p_1 + ta_1,\ p_2 + ta_2)$$. Write $$u_1 = p_1 - c_1$$ and $$u_2 = p_2 - c_2$$, so that
$$P - C = (u_1, u_2)$$ (:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/point-difference}). By
:ref{#coordinates/circle-equation}, the point $$P + tA$$ is on the circle exactly when

```math
(u_1 + a_1t)^2 + (u_2 + a_2t)^2 = r^2
```

By the square of a sum (:ref{#numbers/square-formulas}),

```math
(u_1 + a_1t)^2 = u_1^2 + 2a_1u_1t + a_1^2t^2
```

and the same holds with $$u_2$$ and $$a_2$$. Adding the two and gathering the terms, the left side
is

```math
\left(a_1^2 + a_2^2\right)t^2 + 2\left(a_1u_1 + a_2u_2\right)t + \left(u_1^2 + u_2^2\right) = |A|^2t^2 + 2kt + |P - C|^2
```

by :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/norm}. Taking $$r^2$$ from both sides gives the equation in the
statement.

Since $$A \ne O$$, its length is positive (:ref{#coordinates/distance-zero}), so $$|A|^2 \ne 0$$ and
the equation is a quadratic. Its discriminant (:ref{#quadratics/discriminant}) is

```math
(2k)^2 - 4|A|^2\left(|P - C|^2 - r^2\right) = 4\left(k^2 - |A|^2\left(|P - C|^2 - r^2\right)\right)
```

Different solutions give different points. Suppose $$P + tA = P + sA$$. Comparing first coordinates
gives $$ta_1 = sa_1$$, so $$(t - s)a_1 = 0$$, and in the same way $$(t - s)a_2 = 0$$. Since
$$A \ne O$$, one of $$a_1$$ and $$a_2$$ is not $$0$$, so $$t - s = 0$$
(:ref{#numbers/zero-product}). So the line meets the circle in as many points as the quadratic has
solutions, and :ref{#quadratics/root-count} counts them: two, one or none as the discriminant is
positive, $$0$$ or negative.

:::

The number in brackets has a meaning of its own. By :ref{#coordinates/disc-inequality},
$$|P - C|^2 - r^2$$ is negative, $$0$$ or positive as the starting point $$P$$ is inside, on or
outside the circle. The number $$k$$ is built from the direction $$A$$ and the step $$P - C$$, and
[Lines through a point on the circle](#lines-through-a-point-on-the-circle) shows what $$k = 0$$
means.

For the road, $$|A|^2 = 16 + 9 = 25$$ and $$|P - C|^2 = 64 + 36 = 100$$. Also
$$k = 4 \cdot (-8) + (-3) \cdot 6 = -50$$, so the proposition gives
$$25t^2 - 100t + (100 - 25) = 0$$, the equation we found by hand.

The Coordinates chapter could only handle horizontal and vertical lines, in
:ref{#coordinates/circle-horizontal-line}. The proposition covers every line, including those, and
one of the exercises recovers the horizontal case from it.

## Two points, one point or none

Picture the road being moved sideways across the map while it keeps its direction, so that each new
road is parallel to the last. Far from the lake it misses the water. As it moves in, it first
touches the shore at one spot, then cuts across the lake, then touches the shore again on the far
side, and finally misses once more.

Keep the lake and the direction $$A = (4, -3)$$, and move the starting point. The road through
$$(-7, 5)$$ gave $$25t^2 - 100t + 75 = 0$$, with discriminant
$$100^2 - 4 \cdot 25 \cdot 75 = 2500$$, which is positive. That matches its two meeting points.

Now take the road through $$P = (0, 6)$$. Here $$P - C = (-1, 7)$$, so $$|P - C|^2 = 50$$ and
$$k = 4 \cdot (-1) + (-3) \cdot 7 = -25$$. The quadratic is

```math
25t^2 - 50t + 25 = 25(t - 1)^2 = 0
```

Its discriminant is $$50^2 - 4 \cdot 25 \cdot 25 = 0$$, and the only solution is $$t = 1$$. So this
road meets the shore at the one point $$(0, 6) + (4, -3) = (4, 3)$$.

Move a little further, to the road through $$P = (0, 8)$$. Then $$P - C = (-1, 9)$$, so
$$|P - C|^2 = 82$$ and $$k = -4 - 27 = -31$$. The quadratic is $$25t^2 - 62t + 57 = 0$$, with
discriminant $$62^2 - 4 \cdot 25 \cdot 57 = 3844 - 5700 = -1856$$. That is negative, so this road
misses the lake.

By :ref{#line-equation}, the three roads are the lines $$3x + 4y = -1$$, $$3x + 4y = 24$$ and
$$3x + 4y = 32$$.

:::definition[Tangent line]{#tangent-line}

A line that meets a circle in exactly one point is **tangent** to the circle at that point. The line
is called a **tangent** to the circle, and the point is the **point of contact**.

:::

So the line $$3x + 4y = 24$$ is tangent to the lake's circle at $$(4, 3)$$. The same family has a
second tangent, on the other side of the lake: the road through $$(0, -6.5)$$ touches the shore at
$$(-2, -5)$$. Slide the road below to find both, and watch the sign of the discriminant change each
time the road passes one of them.

## Lines through a point inside

Now put yourself in a rowing boat out on the lake. Row in a straight line in any direction you like,
and you reach the shore ahead of you. Had you rowed the opposite way instead, you'd have reached the
shore behind you. So every line through a point inside a circle crosses it twice, with one meeting
point on each side, and no such line is a tangent.

:::theorem[Lines through a point inside]{#inside-point-line}

Let $$P$$ be inside the circle with centre $$C$$ and radius $$r$$, that is, $$|P - C| < r$$. Then
every line through $$P$$ meets the circle in exactly two points. Written as $$P + tA$$, the line
meets the circle once with $$t > 0$$ and once with $$t < 0$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_of_a_point"}

By part 2 of :ref{#line-descriptions}, any point of a line can serve as its starting point, so the
line can be written as $$P + tA$$ for some direction $$A$$. Since $$0 \le |P - C| < r$$,
:ref{#real-numbers/power-order} gives $$|P - C|^2 < r^2$$. So the number $$m = |P - C|^2 - r^2$$ in
:ref{#line-circle} is negative.

The discriminant is $$4\left(k^2 - |A|^2m\right)$$. Here $$k^2 \ge 0$$
(:ref{#real-numbers/squares-positive}), and $$-|A|^2m > 0$$, because $$|A|^2 > 0$$ and $$m < 0$$
(:ref{#real-numbers/sign-rules}). So the discriminant is positive, and the line meets the circle in
exactly two points, with parameters $$t_1$$ and $$t_2$$ (:ref{#line-circle}).

By :ref{#quadratics/vieta}, $$t_1t_2 = \frac{m}{|A|^2}$$, which is negative. A product of two
numbers is negative exactly when one is positive and the other negative
(:ref{#real-numbers/product-sign}), so one of $$t_1$$ and $$t_2$$ is positive and the other is
negative.

:::

The point with $$t > 0$$ is on the ray from $$P$$ in direction $$A$$ (:ref{#ray-formula}). The point
with $$t < 0$$ is $$P + (-t)(-A)$$ with $$-t > 0$$, so it is on the ray from $$P$$ in the opposite
direction $$-A$$. So the two meeting points lie one ahead of the boat and one behind it, along the
line it rows.

For example, take $$P = (2, 1)$$ and the direction $$A = (1, 1)$$, with the lake as before. Then
$$P - C = (1, 2)$$, so $$|P - C|^2 = 5$$, which is less than $$25$$, and $$P$$ is inside. Also
$$k = 1 + 2 = 3$$ and $$|A|^2 = 2$$, so the quadratic is $$2t^2 + 6t - 20 = 0$$, that is,

```math
t^2 + 3t - 10 = (t + 5)(t - 2) = 0
```

The solutions $$t = 2$$ and $$t = -5$$ have opposite signs, as the theorem says. They give the
points $$(4, 3)$$, ahead of $$P$$, and $$(-3, -4)$$, behind it.

## Lines through a point on the circle

Now stand on the shore, at a point $$P$$ of the circle, and set off in a straight line. Most
directions take you across the lake to a second point on the far shore. There is one line through
$$P$$ that only grazes the lake, touching the shore at $$P$$ and leaving straight away. In the
quadratic, $$t = 0$$ is always a solution, because $$P$$ itself is on the circle.

:::proposition[Lines through a point on the circle]{#on-circle-line}

Let $$P$$ be on the circle with centre $$C$$ and radius $$r$$, and let $$L$$ be the line $$P + tA$$,
with $$k$$ as in :ref{#line-circle}. Then $$L$$ meets the circle at $$P$$, where $$t = 0$$, and at
the point with

```math
t = -\frac{2k}{|A|^2}
```

These are two different points exactly when $$k \ne 0$$. So $$L$$ meets the circle a second time
when $$k \ne 0$$, and $$L$$ is tangent to the circle at $$P$$ when $$k = 0$$.

:::

:::proof{source="https://mathworld.wolfram.com/Circle-LineIntersection.html"}

Since $$P$$ is on the circle, $$|P - C| = r$$ (:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/distance-norm}), so
$$|P - C|^2 - r^2 = 0$$. The quadratic of :ref{#line-circle} becomes

```math
|A|^2t^2 + 2kt = t\left(|A|^2t + 2k\right) = 0
```

By :ref{#numbers/zero-product}, either $$t = 0$$ or $$|A|^2t + 2k = 0$$, and the second gives
$$t = -\frac{2k}{|A|^2}$$, since $$|A|^2 \ne 0$$. The two values of $$t$$ are equal exactly when
$$k = 0$$. Different values of $$t$$ give different points (:ref{#line-circle}), so the line meets
the circle twice when $$k \ne 0$$ and once when $$k = 0$$, and then it is tangent by
:ref{#tangent-line}.

:::

The point $$P = (4, 3)$$ is on the lake's circle, since $$P - C = (3, 4)$$ has length $$5$$. Here
are three lines through it:

- **Direction $$(1, 1)$$**: $$k = 3 + 4 = 7$$ and $$|A|^2 = 2$$, so the second point has $$t = -7$$.
  It is $$(4, 3) - 7(1, 1) = (-3, -4)$$, the far end of the boat's line.
- **Direction $$(3, 4)$$**: $$k = 9 + 16 = 25$$ and $$|A|^2 = 25$$, so $$t = -2$$ and the second
  point is $$(4, 3) - 2(3, 4) = (-2, -5)$$. This line goes through the centre, since
  $$P - C = (3, 4)$$ is its direction, and $$(-2, -5)$$ is the opposite end of a diameter.
- **Direction $$(4, -3)$$**: $$k = 4 \cdot 3 + (-3) \cdot 4 = 0$$, so the line is tangent at
  $$(4, 3)$$. It is the road $$3x + 4y = 24$$ in the figure under
  [Two points, one point or none](#two-points-one-point-or-none).

Draw the radius from $$C$$ to $$(4, 3)$$ in that figure, and it looks square to the tangent line.
That is true for every circle: $$k = 0$$ says exactly that the line is perpendicular to the radius
$$CP$$. Proving it needs perpendicular lines described with numbers, and
:ref{#tangent-perpendicular} does it.

## Does a flight path pass within range

A radar station can see a plane while the plane is within a fixed distance of it. So the region it
covers is a disc, with the station at the centre. A plane flying a straight course at a steady speed
has a position $$P + tA$$ after $$t$$ minutes. So working out when the plane is in range means
finding the numbers $$t$$ for which $$P + tA$$ is in the disc.

Mark a map in kilometres, with an airport at the origin. A plane leaves the airport $$P = O$$ at
$$t = 0$$ and flies with step $$A = (8, 6)$$ each minute, so its speed is $$|A| = 10$$ km a minute,
or $$600$$ km an hour. A radar station at $$C = (80, 10)$$ can see $$50$$ km. Then $$|A|^2 = 100$$,
$$k = 8 \cdot (-80) + 6 \cdot (-10) = -700$$ and $$|P - C|^2 = 6400 + 100 = 6500$$.

By :ref{#coordinates/disc-inequality}, the plane is in range exactly when
$$|P + tA - C|^2 \le 50^2$$. The proof of :ref{#line-circle} shows that $$|P + tA - C|^2 - 50^2$$ is
the left side of its quadratic, so the plane is in range exactly when

```math
100t^2 - 1400t + 4000 \le 0
```

Dividing by $$100$$, this is $$t^2 - 14t + 40 \le 0$$, and $$t^2 - 14t + 40 = (t - 4)(t - 10)$$
(:ref{#quadratics/vieta}). Between $$t = 4$$ and $$t = 10$$ the first bracket is positive and the
second negative, so the product is negative. Before $$t = 4$$ both brackets are negative, and after
$$t = 10$$ both are positive, so there the product is positive (:ref{#real-numbers/product-sign}).

So the plane comes into range $$4$$ minutes after take-off, at $$4A = (32, 24)$$, and leaves it
after $$10$$ minutes, at $$10A = (80, 60)$$. It is seen for $$6$$ minutes, which is $$60$$ km of its
flight. Halfway through, at $$t = 7$$, it is at $$(56, 42)$$, which is $$\sqrt{24^2 + 32^2} = 40$$
km from the station.

A second station at $$(100, 0)$$, also with a range of $$50$$ km, gives $$k = -800$$ and
$$|P - C|^2 = 10\,000$$. Its quadratic is $$100t^2 - 1600t + 7500 = 0$$, with discriminant
$$1600^2 - 4 \cdot 100 \cdot 7500 = -440\,000$$. That is negative, so by :ref{#line-circle} the
flight path never meets the edge of that station's range. Completing the square
(:ref{#quadratics/complete-square}) shows which side it stays on:

```math
100t^2 - 1600t + 7500 = 100\left((t - 8)^2 + 11\right)
```

which is positive for every $$t$$ (:ref{#real-numbers/squares-positive}). So the plane stays outside
the disc, and that station never sees it.

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on lines and circles, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-lines-and-circles-count}

The circle $$(x + 1)^2 + (y - 2)^2 = 10$$ has centre $$C = (-1, 2)$$. Take the point $$P = (3, 0)$$.
For each direction, say whether the line through $$P$$ with that direction crosses the circle,
touches it or misses it, and find any meeting points.

1. $$A = (1, 1)$$
2. $$A = (1, -1)$$
3. $$A = (-1, 3)$$

:::solution

Here $$P - C = (4, -2)$$, so $$|P - C|^2 = 20$$ and $$|P - C|^2 - r^2 = 20 - 10 = 10$$. By
:ref{#line-circle}, the discriminant is $$4\left(k^2 - 10|A|^2\right)$$.

1. $$k = 4 - 2 = 2$$ and $$|A|^2 = 2$$, so the discriminant is $$4(4 - 20) = -64$$. The line misses
   the circle.
2. $$k = 4 + 2 = 6$$ and $$|A|^2 = 2$$, so the discriminant is $$4(36 - 20) = 64$$, and the line
   crosses the circle twice. The quadratic is $$2t^2 + 12t + 10 = 0$$, that is,
   $$t^2 + 6t + 5 = (t + 1)(t + 5) = 0$$. So $$t = -1$$ or $$t = -5$$, giving $$(2, 1)$$ and
   $$(-2, 5)$$.
3. $$k = -4 - 6 = -10$$ and $$|A|^2 = 10$$, so the discriminant is $$4(100 - 100) = 0$$. The line is
   tangent (:ref{#tangent-line}). The quadratic is $$10t^2 - 20t + 10 = 10(t - 1)^2 = 0$$, so
   $$t = 1$$ and the point of contact is $$(3, 0) + (-1, 3) = (2, 3)$$.

Check the points: $$(2, 1)$$ gives $$9 + 1 = 10$$, $$(-2, 5)$$ gives $$1 + 9 = 10$$, and $$(2, 3)$$
gives $$9 + 1 = 10$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-lines-and-circles-equation}

Find where the line $$x - y = -1$$ meets the circle $$(x + 1)^2 + (y - 2)^2 = 10$$. Start by writing
the line as $$P + tA$$.

:::solution

By :ref{#equation-line}, the line has direction $$(1, 1)$$. Putting $$x = 0$$ gives $$y = 1$$, so it
passes through $$P = (0, 1)$$. With $$C = (-1, 2)$$, the step is $$P - C = (1, -1)$$, so
$$|P - C|^2 = 2$$ and $$k = 1 - 1 = 0$$. By :ref{#line-circle}, the quadratic is

```math
2t^2 + (2 - 10) = 2t^2 - 8 = 0
```

so $$t^2 = 4$$, and $$t = 2$$ or $$t = -2$$. The meeting points are $$(2, 3)$$ and $$(-2, -1)$$.
Both satisfy $$x - y = -1$$, and both give $$10$$ in the circle's equation: $$9 + 1$$ and $$1 + 9$$.

The point $$P$$ is inside the circle, since $$2 < 10$$, so :ref{#inside-point-line} promised two
points with parameters of opposite signs.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-lines-and-circles-horizontal}

Use :ref{#line-circle} with $$P = (0, h)$$ and $$A = (1, 0)$$ to find where the horizontal line
$$y = h$$ meets the circle with centre $$(a, b)$$ and radius $$r$$. Check that you get the same
quadratic and the same three cases as :ref{#coordinates/circle-horizontal-line}.

:::solution

The point $$P + tA$$ is $$(t, h)$$, so $$t$$ is the first coordinate $$x$$. Here $$|A|^2 = 1$$,
$$k = 1 \cdot (0 - a) + 0 \cdot (h - b) = -a$$ and $$|P - C|^2 = a^2 + (h - b)^2$$. The quadratic of
:ref{#line-circle} is

```math
t^2 - 2at + \left(a^2 + (h - b)^2 - r^2\right) = 0
```

which is the quadratic of :ref{#coordinates/circle-horizontal-line} with $$t$$ in place of $$x$$.
Its discriminant is

```math
4\left(a^2 - \left(a^2 + (h - b)^2 - r^2\right)\right) = 4\left(r^2 - (h - b)^2\right)
```

the same as before. So the three cases agree: two points when $$|h - b| < r$$, one when
$$|h - b| = r$$, and none when $$|h - b| > r$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-lines-and-circles-tangents}

The lines $$y = 2x + b$$ all have direction $$(1, 2)$$, and the line for $$b$$ passes through
$$(0, b)$$. For which numbers $$b$$ does the line meet the circle $$(x - 1)^2 + (y - 2)^2 = 5$$
twice, once or not at all? Find the points of contact of the tangents.

:::solution

Here $$C = (1, 2)$$, $$P = (0, b)$$ and $$A = (1, 2)$$, so $$|A|^2 = 5$$,
$$k = 1 \cdot (-1) + 2(b - 2) = 2b - 5$$ and $$|P - C|^2 - 5 = 1 + (b - 2)^2 - 5 = b^2 - 4b$$. By
:ref{#line-circle}, the discriminant is $$4$$ times

```math
(2b - 5)^2 - 5\left(b^2 - 4b\right) = 4b^2 - 20b + 25 - 5b^2 + 20b = 25 - b^2
```

So the line crosses twice when $$b^2 < 25$$, that is, when $$-5 < b < 5$$. It is tangent when
$$b = 5$$ or $$b = -5$$, and it misses when $$b > 5$$ or $$b < -5$$.

At a tangent the one solution is $$t = -\frac{2k}{2|A|^2} = -\frac{k}{5}$$
(:ref{#quadratics/root-count}). For $$b = 5$$, $$k = 5$$ and $$t = -1$$, so the point of contact is
$$(0, 5) - (1, 2) = (-1, 3)$$. For $$b = -5$$, $$k = -15$$ and $$t = 3$$, so it is
$$(0, -5) + 3(1, 2) = (3, 1)$$. Both are on the circle: $$4 + 1 = 5$$ and $$4 + 1 = 5$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-lines-and-circles-inside}

Take the lake's circle, with centre $$C = (1, -1)$$ and radius $$5$$, and the point $$P = (3, 2)$$.

1. Show that $$P$$ is inside the circle.
2. Find where the line through $$P$$ with direction $$(1, -1)$$ meets the circle, and check that the
   two parameters have opposite signs.

:::solution

1. $$P - C = (2, 3)$$, so $$|P - C|^2 = 4 + 9 = 13$$, which is less than $$25$$. By
   :ref{#coordinates/disc-inequality}, $$P$$ is inside.
2. $$|A|^2 = 2$$ and $$k = 1 \cdot 2 + (-1) \cdot 3 = -1$$. By :ref{#line-circle}, the quadratic is
   $$2t^2 - 2t + (13 - 25) = 0$$, that is, $$t^2 - t - 6 = (t - 3)(t + 2) = 0$$. So $$t = 3$$,
   giving $$(6, -1)$$, or $$t = -2$$, giving $$(1, 4)$$. The parameters $$3$$ and $$-2$$ have
   opposite signs, as :ref{#inside-point-line} says, and their product $$-6$$ is $$\frac{-12}{2}$$
   (:ref{#quadratics/vieta}).

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-lines-and-circles-on}

The point $$P = (5, 2)$$ is on the lake's circle, with centre $$C = (1, -1)$$ and radius $$5$$.

1. Find the second point where the line through $$P$$ with direction $$(1, -1)$$ meets the circle.
2. Find the number $$m$$ for which the line through $$P$$ with direction $$(1, m)$$ is tangent to
   the circle.

:::solution

Here $$P - C = (4, 3)$$, which has length $$5$$, so $$P$$ is on the circle.

1. $$k = 4 - 3 = 1$$ and $$|A|^2 = 2$$, so by :ref{#on-circle-line} the second point has
   $$t = -\frac{2}{2} = -1$$. It is $$(5, 2) - (1, -1) = (4, 3)$$, and
   $$(4 - 1)^2 + (3 + 1)^2 = 25$$.
2. With $$A = (1, m)$$, $$k = 4 + 3m$$. By :ref{#on-circle-line}, the line is tangent exactly when
   $$k = 0$$, that is, when $$m = -\frac{4}{3}$$. A tidier direction for the same line is
   $$3\left(1, -\frac{4}{3}\right) = (3, -4)$$ (part 1 of :ref{#line-descriptions}).

:::

::::exercise{#ex-lines-and-circles-diameter}

Show that a line through the centre $$C$$ of a circle with radius $$r$$, with direction $$A$$, meets
the circle at the two points

```math
C + \frac{r}{|A|}A \qquad \text{and} \qquad C - \frac{r}{|A|}A
```

and that these points are $$2r$$ apart. Check it with the lake's circle and $$A = (4, -3)$$.

:::solution

Take $$P = C$$ in :ref{#line-circle}. Then $$P - C = O$$, so $$k = 0$$ and $$|P - C|^2 = 0$$, and
the quadratic is $$|A|^2t^2 - r^2 = 0$$. So $$t^2 = \frac{r^2}{|A|^2}$$, and $$t = \frac{r}{|A|}$$
or $$t = -\frac{r}{|A|}$$, which give the two points.

Their difference is $$\frac{2r}{|A|}A$$, so by :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/distance-norm} and
:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/norm-scale} the distance between them is

```math
\frac{2r}{|A|}\,|A| = 2r
```

For the lake, $$C = (1, -1)$$, $$r = 5$$ and $$|A| = 5$$, so the points are
$$(1, -1) + (4, -3) = (5, -4)$$ and $$(1, -1) - (4, -3) = (-3, 2)$$. These are the two ends of the
bridge in the first example, and they are $$\sqrt{64 + 36} = 10$$ apart.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-lines-and-circles-train}

A train leaves a station at the origin, on a map marked in kilometres, and travels with step
$$(0.6, 0.8)$$ each minute. A phone mast at $$(6, 3)$$ gives a signal up to $$5$$ km away.

1. For how long does a passenger have signal, and where is the train when the signal starts and
   stops?
2. A second mast at $$(10, 0)$$ also reaches $$5$$ km. Does the passenger ever get its signal?

:::solution

1. The train is at $$tA$$ after $$t$$ minutes, with $$A = (0.6, 0.8)$$ and $$|A|^2 = 1$$. With
   $$P = O$$ and $$C = (6, 3)$$, $$k = 0.6 \cdot (-6) + 0.8 \cdot (-3) = -6$$ and
   $$|P - C|^2 = 45$$. As in the radar example, the passenger has signal exactly when
   $$t^2 - 12t + (45 - 25) \le 0$$, and $$t^2 - 12t + 20 = (t - 2)(t - 10)$$. That is negative or
   $$0$$ exactly when $$2 \le t \le 10$$ (:ref{#real-numbers/product-sign}). So the signal lasts
   $$8$$ minutes, from $$(1.2, 1.6)$$ to $$(6, 8)$$.
2. With $$C = (10, 0)$$, $$k = -6$$ and $$|P - C|^2 = 100$$, so the quadratic is
   $$t^2 - 12t + 75 = 0$$, with discriminant $$144 - 300 = -156$$. It is negative, so the track
   never reaches the edge of the second mast's range (:ref{#line-circle}). Completing the square,
   $$t^2 - 12t + 75 = (t - 6)^2 + 39$$, which is positive for every $$t$$. So the train is always
   outside the range, and the passenger never gets that signal.

:::
