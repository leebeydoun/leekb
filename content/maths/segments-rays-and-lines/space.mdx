Imagine lying in a field on a clear afternoon, watching two planes high overhead. Their vapour
trails draw two long white lines across the sky, and the lines seem to cross in a wide X. But one
plane was flying a kilometre or two above the other, so the trails never touched: one passed over
the other. From the ground you can't see height, so the two trails look as if they meet.

In the plane, two lines that aren't parallel always meet. In space they can miss each other, and
lines like that are called **skew**. In this guide we'll write segments, rays and lines in space
with the same formulas as in the plane, and then see what the third coordinate changes.

## Segments, rays and lines in space

A straight path in space works like one in the plane. You start at a point and take the same step
over and over, or a fraction of it, and the step now has three numbers instead of two. The formulas
of the earlier guides in this chapter only add and scale points, and
[Points in space](../adding-and-scaling-points/space.mdx) showed that adding and scaling work for
triples too. So the formulas carry over unchanged.

:::definition[Segments, rays and lines in space]{#space-lines}

Let $$P$$ and $$Q$$ be points of $$\mathbb{R}^3$$ (:ref{#coordinates/coordinate-space}), and let
$$A \ne O$$ be a point of $$\mathbb{R}^3$$ used as a direction, where $$O = (0, 0, 0)$$. Then:

- for $$P \ne Q$$, the **segment** $$PQ$$ is the set of points $$P + t(Q - P)$$ with
  $$0 \le t \le 1$$
- the **ray** from $$P$$ in direction $$A$$ is the set of points $$P + tA$$ with $$t \ge 0$$, and
  for $$Q \ne P$$ the ray from $$P$$ through $$Q$$ is the ray from $$P$$ in direction $$Q - P$$
- the **line** through $$P$$ with direction $$A$$ is $$\{P + tA : t \in \mathbb{R}\}$$, the set of
  all points $$P + tA$$ with $$t$$ a real number

The sums and multiples are those of :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/space-operations}.

:::

These are :ref{#segment-formula}, :ref{#ray-formula} and :ref{#line-formula} with triples in place
of pairs. The parameter $$t$$ still says how many steps of $$A$$ to take from $$P$$, and each step
now moves you east, north and up at once.

:::example[A cable car up a hillside]

A cable car runs in a straight line from a valley station at $$P = (0, 0, 20)$$ to a top station at
$$Q = (180, 60, 110)$$, in metres east, north and up. The step from $$P$$ to $$Q$$ is
$$Q - P = (180, 60, 90)$$, so the cable is the segment of points

```math
P + t(Q - P) = (180t,\ 60t,\ 20 + 90t), \qquad 0 \le t \le 1
```

A third of the way along, $$t = \frac{1}{3}$$ gives $$(60, 20, 50)$$: the car has climbed $$30$$ of
the $$90$$ metres. By :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/space-norm}, the cable is

```math
|Q - P| = \sqrt{180^2 + 60^2 + 90^2} = \sqrt{32\,400 + 3600 + 8100} = \sqrt{44\,100} = 210
```

metres long.

:::

The car at $$(60, 20, 50)$$ is $$70$$ metres from the valley station and $$140$$ metres from the top
station, a third and two thirds of the cable. In the plane that is :ref{#fraction-along}, and its
proof works in space too. The same goes for the facts about lines in [Lines](./lines.mdx). Their
proofs never look at the coordinates of a point: they only add, scale and measure length, and those
follow the same rules for triples (:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/space-rules} and
:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/space-norm-rules}).

:::proposition[Facts about lines that hold in space]{#space-line-facts}

Let $$P$$ and $$Q$$ be different points of $$\mathbb{R}^3$$, and $$A \ne O$$ a point of
$$\mathbb{R}^3$$.

1. **A fraction of the way along**: if $$X = P + t(Q - P)$$ with $$0 \le t \le 1$$, then
   $$d(P, X) = t\,d(P, Q)$$ and $$d(X, Q) = (1 - t)\,d(P, Q)$$
2. **Other descriptions of a line**: for every $$c \ne 0$$, the line through $$P$$ with direction
   $$cA$$ is the line through $$P$$ with direction $$A$$. For every point $$M$$ of that line, the
   line through $$M$$ with direction $$A$$ is the same line
3. **The line through two points**: the line through $$P$$ with direction $$Q - P$$ contains $$P$$
   and $$Q$$, and it is the only line that contains both

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_(geometry)#Parametric_equation"}

The proofs of :ref{#fraction-along}, :ref{#line-descriptions} and :ref{#two-point-line} work word
for word, because every rule they use holds for triples. Here they are in short.

**Part 1.** By :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/space-rules}, $$X - P = t(Q - P)$$ and
$$Q - X = (1 - t)(Q - P)$$. Both $$t$$ and $$1 - t$$ are not negative, so by parts 1 and 2 of
:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/space-norm-rules},

```math
d(P, X) = |t(Q - P)| = t\,|Q - P| = t\,d(P, Q),
\qquad
d(X, Q) = |(1 - t)(Q - P)| = (1 - t)\,d(P, Q)
```

**Part 2.** A point $$P + t(cA)$$ of the first line is $$P + (tc)A$$, a point of the second. A point
$$P + tA$$ of the second is $$P + \frac{t}{c}(cA)$$, a point of the first. For the second sentence,
write $$M = P + uA$$. Then $$P + tA = M + (t - u)A$$ and $$M + tA = P + (u + t)A$$, so the line
through $$P$$ and the line through $$M$$ have the same points.

**Part 3.** The values $$t = 0$$ and $$t = 1$$ give $$P$$ and $$Q$$. Now suppose the line through a
point $$N$$ with direction $$B$$ contains both, say $$P = N + uB$$ and $$Q = N + vB$$. Then
$$Q - P = (v - u)B$$, and $$v \ne u$$ since $$P \ne Q$$. By part 2, that line is the line through
$$P$$ with direction $$B$$, and so also the line through $$P$$ with direction $$(v - u)B = Q - P$$.

:::

Part 1 with $$t = \frac{1}{2}$$ gives the midpoint.
[Points in space](../adding-and-scaling-points/space.mdx) found the midpoint $$\frac{1}{2}(P + Q)$$
in :ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/space-midpoint}, taking a rule for segments from the picture. Now
$$\frac{1}{2}(P + Q) = P + \frac{1}{2}(Q - P)$$ lies on the segment $$PQ$$ by definition, and part 1
puts it half the length from each end, with no picture needed. Halfway up the cable car is
$$(90, 30, 65)$$, $$105$$ metres from each station.

## Parallel lines in space

Picture the two rails of a straight railway climbing a hillside. A step along one rail is the same
as a step along the other: same steepness, same heading. So the rails run side by side and never
meet. In coordinates this says the directions of the two lines are multiples of each other, as in
the plane.

:::definition[Parallel in space]{#space-parallel}

Lines in $$\mathbb{R}^3$$ with directions $$A$$ and $$B$$ are **parallel**, written
$$L \parallel K$$, when $$B = cA$$ for some number $$c \ne 0$$. The directions $$A$$ and $$B$$ have
the **same direction** when $$c > 0$$, and **opposite directions** when $$c < 0$$.

:::

This is :ref{#parallel-formula} and :ref{#same-direction} with triples. To test two directions, find
$$c$$ from a coordinate of $$A$$ that is not $$0$$, and then check the other two coordinates.

:::example[Testing for parallel lines]

Let $$L$$ be the line through $$(1, 0, 2)$$ with direction $$A = (2, -1, 3)$$, and $$K$$ the line
through $$(0, 4, 0)$$ with direction $$B = (-4, 2, -6)$$. The first coordinates need $$-4 = 2c$$, so
$$c = -2$$. Then $$-2A = (-4, 2, -6) = B$$, so $$L \parallel K$$, with opposite directions.

They are different lines. The step from $$(1, 0, 2)$$ to $$(0, 4, 0)$$ is $$(-1, 4, -2)$$. If
$$(0, 4, 0)$$ were on $$L$$, this step would be $$tA$$ for some $$t$$. The first coordinates give
$$t = -\frac{1}{2}$$, but then the second coordinate would be $$\frac{1}{2}$$, not $$4$$.

:::

Two parallel lines that share a point are the same line, as in the plane. Part 2 of
:ref{#space-meeting} proves it, so the lines $$L$$ and $$K$$ in the example never meet.

## Skew lines

Stand in a room and look at two of its edges: the one where the floor meets the front wall, and the
one where the ceiling meets the left wall. The first runs across in front of you along the floor,
and the second runs away from you overhead. They don't point the same way, yet they never meet,
since one stays on the floor and the other stays on the ceiling.

:::definition[Skew lines]{#skew-lines}

Two lines in $$\mathbb{R}^3$$ are **skew** when they are not parallel and never meet.

:::

<SkewLines caption="In a room 6 by 4 by 3, the line L runs along the floor edge at the front and the line K along the ceiling edge above the left wall. They never meet, and the upright edge from O to (0, 0, 3) joins them." />

:::example[Two edges of a room]

Put a corner of a room $$6$$ long, $$4$$ deep and $$3$$ high at $$O$$, with the $$x$$-axis along the
front wall, the $$y$$-axis along the left wall and the $$z$$-axis up. Let $$L$$ be the line through
$$O$$ with direction $$(1, 0, 0)$$, the floor edge, and $$K$$ the line through $$(0, 0, 3)$$ with
direction $$(0, 1, 0)$$, the ceiling edge above the left wall.

**They never meet.** Every point of $$L$$ is $$(t, 0, 0)$$, with third coordinate $$0$$. Every point
of $$K$$ is $$(0, s, 3)$$, with third coordinate $$3$$. No point has both, so no point is on both
lines.

**They are not parallel.** If $$(0, 1, 0) = c(1, 0, 0) = (c, 0, 0)$$, the second coordinates would
give $$1 = 0$$.

So $$L$$ and $$K$$ are skew.

:::

The vapour trails work the same way. Looking up from the ground loses the height, which is like
dropping the third coordinate. Then $$L$$ becomes the points $$(t, 0)$$ and $$K$$ the points
$$(0, s)$$, the two axes of the plane, and they cross at the origin. So the flattened lines cross,
while the lines themselves miss by $$3$$, the height of the room.

## When two lines in space meet

To find where two lines meet, look for a point that is on both. A point of the first line is
$$P + tA$$ and a point of the second is $$Q + sB$$, so we need numbers $$t$$ and $$s$$ that make
these equal. In the plane that is two equations, one for each coordinate. In space it is three
equations in the same two unknowns, and three conditions are harder to meet than two.

:::proposition[Meeting in space]{#space-meeting}

Let $$L$$ be the line through $$P = (p_1, p_2, p_3)$$ with direction $$A = (a_1, a_2, a_3)$$, and
$$K$$ the line through $$Q = (q_1, q_2, q_3)$$ with direction $$B = (b_1, b_2, b_3)$$, in
$$\mathbb{R}^3$$.

1. $$L$$ and $$K$$ meet exactly when there are numbers $$t$$ and $$s$$ with $$P + tA = Q + sB$$,
   that is,

   ```math
   p_1 + ta_1 = q_1 + sb_1, \qquad p_2 + ta_2 = q_2 + sb_2, \qquad p_3 + ta_3 = q_3 + sb_3
   ```

   and then they meet at $$P + tA$$

2. If $$L \parallel K$$, then $$L$$ and $$K$$ are the same line or never meet
3. If $$L$$ and $$K$$ are not parallel, they meet in at most one point. So they meet in exactly one
   point, or they are skew

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line%E2%80%93line_intersection#In_three_dimensions"}

**Part 1.** By :ref{#space-lines}, a point is on $$L$$ exactly when it is $$P + tA$$ for some $$t$$,
and on $$K$$ exactly when it is $$Q + sB$$ for some $$s$$. Two triples are equal exactly when their
three coordinates are (:ref{#coordinates/coordinate-space}), which gives the three equations.

**Part 2.** Let $$B = cA$$ with $$c \ne 0$$, and suppose a point $$M$$ is on both lines. By part 2
of :ref{#space-line-facts}, $$L$$ is the line through $$M$$ with direction $$A$$, and $$K$$ is the
line through $$M$$ with direction $$cA$$. By the same part, these are the same line.

**Part 3.** Suppose $$L$$ and $$K$$ meet at two different points $$M$$ and $$N$$. On $$L$$, write
$$M = P + t_1A$$ and $$N = P + t_2A$$. Then $$N - M = (t_2 - t_1)A$$, with $$t_2 \ne t_1$$ since
$$M \ne N$$. On $$K$$ in the same way, $$N - M = (s_2 - s_1)B$$ with $$s_2 \ne s_1$$. So

```math
B = \frac{t_2 - t_1}{s_2 - s_1}\,A
```

a multiple of $$A$$ that is not $$0$$, and $$L \parallel K$$. So lines that are not parallel share
at most one point.

:::

In each example below, two of the equations fix $$t$$ and $$s$$, and the third is a check. It holds
when the lines meet and fails when they miss.

:::example[Two lines that meet]

Let $$L$$ be the line through $$P = (1, 0, 2)$$ with direction $$A = (1, 1, 0)$$, and $$K$$ the line
through $$Q = (3, -1, 5)$$ with direction $$B = (0, 1, -1)$$. The three equations of
:ref{#space-meeting} are

```math
1 + t = 3, \qquad t = -1 + s, \qquad 2 = 5 - s
```

The first gives $$t = 2$$ and the third gives $$s = 3$$. The middle one checks: $$2 = -1 + 3$$. So
the lines meet at $$P + 2A = (3, 2, 2)$$, and as a check, $$Q + 3B = (3, 2, 2)$$ too.

:::

:::example[Lifting one line by one unit]

Keep $$L$$, and replace $$K$$ by the line $$K'$$ through $$(3, -1, 6)$$ with the same direction
$$(0, 1, -1)$$, one unit higher. Now the equations are

```math
1 + t = 3, \qquad t = -1 + s, \qquad 2 = 6 - s
```

The first gives $$t = 2$$ and the third gives $$s = 4$$, but then the middle one says $$2 = 3$$. So
the lines never meet. They are not parallel either: $$(0, 1, -1) = c(1, 1, 0)$$ would need $$c = 0$$
from the first coordinates and $$c = 1$$ from the second. So $$L$$ and $$K'$$ are skew.

:::

Air traffic control uses this idea. One way to keep two planes on crossing routes apart is to fly
them at heights at least $$1000$$ feet apart. Each plane flying level follows a line with a fixed
third coordinate, like $$L$$ and $$K$$ in the room. The routes cross on the map, so the directions
are not parallel, and the heights differ, so the paths never meet: they are skew. Paths that do meet
lead to a collision exactly when both planes reach the meeting point at the same time, as in
[Crossing paths and collisions](./lines.mdx#crossing-paths-and-collisions).

## Right angles in space

Look at a corner of a box. Three edges meet there, and each is square to the other two. In the
plane, [Perpendicular lines](./perpendicular.mdx) found a test for a right angle with numbers alone,
built from Pythagoras and the length of a difference. The same algebra works with a third
coordinate.

:::definition[Dot product in space]{#space-dot}

For points $$A = (a_1, a_2, a_3)$$ and $$B = (b_1, b_2, b_3)$$ of $$\mathbb{R}^3$$, the **dot
product** is the number

```math
A \cdot B = a_1b_1 + a_2b_2 + a_3b_3
```

:::

This is :ref{#dot-product} with a third term. The rules in :ref{#dot-rules} carry over, one
coordinate at a time. In particular $$A \cdot A = a_1^2 + a_2^2 + a_3^2 = |A|^2$$, by
:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/space-norm}.

:::proposition[The length of a difference in space]{#space-difference-length}

For all points $$A$$ and $$B$$ of $$\mathbb{R}^3$$,

```math
|A - B|^2 = |A|^2 + |B|^2 - 2\,A \cdot B
```

:::

:::proof{source="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dot_product#Application_to_the_law_of_cosines"}

The coordinates of $$A - B$$ are $$a_1 - b_1$$, $$a_2 - b_2$$ and $$a_3 - b_3$$, so $$|A - B|^2$$ is
the sum of their squares (:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/space-norm}). By
:ref{#numbers/square-formulas}, each square is $$(a_i - b_i)^2 = a_i^2 - 2a_ib_i + b_i^2$$. Adding
the three,

```math
|A - B|^2 = (a_1^2 + a_2^2 + a_3^2) + (b_1^2 + b_2^2 + b_3^2) - 2(a_1b_1 + a_2b_2 + a_3b_3)
```

which is $$|A|^2 + |B|^2 - 2\,A \cdot B$$. This is the proof of :ref{#difference-length} with one
more term.

:::

Now take $$A$$ and $$B$$ not on one line through $$O$$. Then $$O$$, $$A$$ and $$B$$ are the corners
of a triangle, and the triangle lies flat, in a plane through its three corners. That is taken from
the picture, since flat planes in space have no formulas in these notes yet. In that flat plane,
:ref{#distance-and-angles/pythagoras} and its converse :ref{#coordinates/pythagoras-converse} hold.
So the angle at $$O$$ is a right angle exactly when $$|A - B|^2 = |A|^2 + |B|^2$$, which by
:ref{#space-difference-length} is exactly when $$A \cdot B = 0$$.

So we use $$A \cdot B = 0$$ as the meaning of a right angle between two directions in space. Lines
need one more condition, because in space two lines can have such directions and still miss each
other.

:::definition[Perpendicular in space]{#space-perpendicular}

Two directions $$A$$ and $$B$$ in $$\mathbb{R}^3$$ are **perpendicular** when $$A \cdot B = 0$$. Two
lines in $$\mathbb{R}^3$$ are **perpendicular**, written $$L \perp K$$, when they meet and their
directions are perpendicular.

:::

The edges of the room show why the lines must meet. The floor edge $$L$$ and the ceiling edge $$K$$
have directions with $$(1, 0, 0) \cdot (0, 1, 0) = 0$$, but they never meet. They are skew lines
with perpendicular directions, not perpendicular lines. In the plane the extra condition changes
nothing: perpendicular directions are never multiples of each other (one of the exercises proves
it), so the lines meet by :ref{#line-intersection}, and the definition agrees with
:ref{#perpendicular-formula}.

:::example[The edges of a tilted crate]

A computer game stores a crate tipped at an angle, with one corner at $$O$$ and the three edges from
that corner ending at

```math
A = (2, 2, 1), \qquad B = (-1, 2, -2), \qquad C = (2, -1, -2)
```

The dot products are

```math
A \cdot B = -2 + 4 - 2 = 0, \qquad A \cdot C = 4 - 2 - 2 = 0, \qquad B \cdot C = -2 - 2 + 4 = 0
```

So the three edges are perpendicular in pairs, as the edges at a corner of a box must be. Each has
length $$\sqrt{4 + 4 + 1} = 3$$, so the crate is a cube of side $$3$$. As a check with Pythagoras,
$$|A - B|^2 = |(3, 0, 3)|^2 = 18 = 3^2 + 3^2$$.

:::

One warning about equations. In the plane, $$ax + by = c$$ is a line, but in space a single equation
$$ax + by + cz = d$$ describes a flat plane: the points with $$z = 0$$ make up the whole floor, not
a line. So in space we describe lines with a parameter, as in this guide.

## Exercises

:::::accordion[Exercises on lines in space, with solutions]

::::exercise{#ex-space-floor-crossing}

A straight wire runs from $$P = (1, -2, 4)$$ to $$Q = (7, 1, -2)$$. Where does it pass through the
floor $$z = 0$$, and how far is that point from each end?

:::solution

The step is $$Q - P = (6, 3, -6)$$, so the wire is the segment of points
$$(1 + 6t,\ -2 + 3t,\ 4 - 6t)$$ with $$0 \le t \le 1$$ (:ref{#space-lines}). The third coordinate is
$$0$$ when $$t = \frac{2}{3}$$, which is between $$0$$ and $$1$$, at the point

```math
\left(1 + 4,\ -2 + 2,\ 4 - 4\right) = (5, 0, 0)
```

The wire has length $$|Q - P| = \sqrt{36 + 9 + 36} = \sqrt{81} = 9$$. By part 1 of
:ref{#space-line-facts}, the point is $$\frac{2}{3} \cdot 9 = 6$$ from $$P$$ and
$$\frac{1}{3} \cdot 9 = 3$$ from $$Q$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-space-parallel-test}

Which of these directions give lines parallel to a line with direction $$(2, -1, 3)$$? For those
that do, say whether the direction is the same or opposite.

1. $$(-6, 3, -9)$$
2. $$(4, -2, 5)$$
3. $$\left(1, -\frac{1}{2}, \frac{3}{2}\right)$$

:::solution

By :ref{#space-parallel}, find $$c$$ from the first coordinate and check the other two:

1. $$c = -3$$, and $$-3(2, -1, 3) = (-6, 3, -9)$$. Parallel, opposite directions
2. $$c = 2$$ gives $$(4, -2, 6)$$, but the third coordinate is $$5$$. Not parallel
3. $$c = \frac{1}{2}$$, and $$\frac{1}{2}(2, -1, 3) = \left(1, -\frac{1}{2}, \frac{3}{2}\right)$$.
   Parallel, same direction

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-space-meet-or-skew}

For each pair, decide whether the lines meet, and if they do, find where.

1. The line through $$(2, 1, 0)$$ with direction $$(1, -1, 2)$$, and the line through $$(0, 4, 1)$$
   with direction $$(1, 1, -1)$$
2. The line through $$(1, 2, -1)$$ with direction $$(2, 0, 1)$$, and the line through $$(4, -1, 5)$$
   with direction $$(1, 1, -1)$$

:::solution

By :ref{#space-meeting}, write out the three equations.

1. They are $$2 + t = s$$, $$1 - t = 4 + s$$ and $$2t = 1 - s$$. Adding the first two gives
   $$3 = 4 + 2s$$, so $$s = -\frac{1}{2}$$ and $$t = s - 2 = -\frac{5}{2}$$. Then the third says
   $$-5 = \frac{3}{2}$$, which is false, so the lines never meet. They are not parallel, since
   $$(1, 1, -1) = c(1, -1, 2)$$ would need $$c = 1$$ and $$c = -1$$. So they are skew.
2. They are $$1 + 2t = 4 + s$$, $$2 = -1 + s$$ and $$-1 + t = 5 - s$$. The second gives $$s = 3$$,
   and then the third gives $$t = 3$$. The first checks: $$1 + 6 = 7 = 4 + 3$$. So the lines meet,
   at $$(1, 2, -1) + 3(2, 0, 1) = (7, 2, 2)$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-space-cube-diagonals}

In the cube with corners at $$O$$ and $$(1, 1, 1)$$, let $$L$$ be the line through $$O$$ with
direction $$(1, 1, 1)$$, along the long diagonal, and $$K$$ the line through $$(1, 0, 0)$$ with
direction $$(-1, 1, 0)$$, along a diagonal of the floor. Show that $$L$$ and $$K$$ are skew, and
that their directions are perpendicular.

:::solution

A point on both would have $$(t, t, t) = (1 - s, s, 0)$$. The third coordinates give $$t = 0$$, and
then the first two give $$1 - s = 0$$ and $$s = 0$$, which can't both hold. So the lines never meet.
They are not parallel, since $$(-1, 1, 0) = c(1, 1, 1)$$ would need $$c = -1$$ and $$c = 1$$. So
they are skew (:ref{#skew-lines}).

Their directions have $$(1, 1, 1) \cdot (-1, 1, 0) = -1 + 1 + 0 = 0$$, so they are perpendicular
(:ref{#space-perpendicular}). The lines are not perpendicular lines, though, since they don't meet.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-space-third-edge}

Show that $$A = (1, 2, 2)$$ and $$B = (2, 1, -2)$$ are perpendicular. Then find the point
$$C = (x, y, 1)$$ that is perpendicular to both, and check that $$A$$, $$B$$ and $$C$$ all have the
same length.

:::solution

$$A \cdot B = 2 + 2 - 4 = 0$$ (:ref{#space-dot}). For $$C$$ we need

```math
A \cdot C = x + 2y + 2 = 0, \qquad B \cdot C = 2x + y - 2 = 0
```

Doubling the second gives $$4x + 2y = 4$$, and taking the first away gives $$3x - 2 = 4$$, so
$$x = 2$$. Then $$y = 2 - 2x = -2$$, and $$C = (2, -2, 1)$$. Check: $$A \cdot C = 2 - 4 + 2 = 0$$
and $$B \cdot C = 4 - 2 - 2 = 0$$.

All three have length $$\sqrt{1 + 4 + 4} = \sqrt{4 + 1 + 4} = \sqrt{4 + 4 + 1} = 3$$. So $$A$$,
$$B$$ and $$C$$ are the three edges at a corner of a cube of side $$3$$.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-space-perpendicular-not-parallel}

Let $$A$$ and $$B$$ be directions, both $$\ne O$$, in the plane or in space. Prove that if
$$A \cdot B = 0$$, then $$B$$ is not a multiple of $$A$$.

:::solution

Suppose $$B = cA$$ for some number $$c$$. Since $$B \ne O$$, $$c \ne 0$$. Then, by the rules in
:ref{#dot-rules},

```math
A \cdot B = A \cdot (cA) = c\,(A \cdot A) = c\,|A|^2
```

Here $$c \ne 0$$, and $$|A|^2 > 0$$ because $$A \ne O$$. A product of two numbers that are not $$0$$
is not $$0$$, so $$A \cdot B \ne 0$$. This contradicts $$A \cdot B = 0$$, so $$B$$ is not a multiple
of $$A$$. In particular, lines with perpendicular directions are never parallel.

:::

::::

::::exercise{#ex-space-vapour-trails}

Two planes fly level in straight lines, with distances in kilometres east, north and up. The first
follows the line through $$(0, 0, 9)$$ with direction $$(1, 2, 0)$$, and the second the line through
$$(6, 0, 11)$$ with direction $$(-1, 1, 0)$$. Show that their paths are skew. Then find the point on
the ground above which the paths seem to cross, and how far apart in height they are there.

:::solution

Every point of the first path has height $$9$$, and every point of the second has height $$11$$, so
the paths never meet. The directions are not parallel, since $$(-1, 1, 0) = c(1, 2, 0)$$ would need
$$c = -1$$ and $$c = \frac{1}{2}$$. So the paths are skew.

Dropping the height leaves the points $$(t, 2t)$$ and $$(6 - s, s)$$ on the ground. These are equal
when $$t = 6 - s$$ and $$2t = s$$, so $$3t = 6$$, $$t = 2$$ and $$s = 4$$. The paths seem to cross
above $$(2, 4)$$, where the first plane's path is at $$(2, 4, 9)$$ and the second's at
$$(2, 4, 11)$$, $$2$$ kilometres higher.
