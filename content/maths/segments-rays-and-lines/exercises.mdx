These exercises use the whole chapter, and most need ideas from more than one guide. Try each one
before you open its solution, and name the rule or result behind each step, as the guides do.

Two habits carry most of them. To find where a line meets something, write its points as $$P + tA$$,
put that into the other condition and solve for $$t$$. To test for a right angle, take a dot product
and see whether it is $$0$$.

## Segments and lines

::::exercise{#ex-lines-ch-thirds}

Let $$P = (-2, 1)$$ and $$Q = (7, 7)$$.

1. Find the two points that split the segment $$PQ$$ into three equal pieces.
2. Find an equation $$ax + by = c$$ for the line $$PQ$$, and check that all four points satisfy it.

:::solution

1. The step from $$P$$ to $$Q$$ is $$Q - P = (9, 6)$$. By :ref{#fraction-along}, the points with
   $$t = \frac{1}{3}$$ and $$t = \frac{2}{3}$$ are a third and two thirds of the way along:

   ```math
   P + \tfrac{1}{3}(9, 6) = (1, 3) \qquad P + \tfrac{2}{3}(9, 6) = (4, 5)
   ```

2. The direction $$(9, 6)$$ is $$3$$ times $$(3, 2)$$, so the line has direction $$(3, 2)$$ too
   (:ref{#line-descriptions}). By :ref{#line-equation}, with $$a_1 = 3$$, $$a_2 = 2$$ and the point
   $$P$$, its equation is

   ```math
   2x - 3y = 2 \cdot (-2) - 3 \cdot 1 = -7
   ```

   Check: $$2(-2) - 3(1)$$, $$2(1) - 3(3)$$, $$2(4) - 3(5)$$ and $$2(7) - 3(7)$$ are all $$-7$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-lines-ch-both-ways}

1. Find an equation for the line through $$(1, -2)$$ and $$(4, 4)$$, and the points where it crosses
   the axes.
2. Write the line $$3x - 2y = 6$$ in the form $$P + tA$$.

:::solution

1. The step between the points is $$(3, 6)$$, so $$(1, 2)$$ is a direction. By :ref{#line-equation}
   the equation is $$2x - y = 2 \cdot 1 - (-2) = 4$$, and $$(4, 4)$$ checks: $$8 - 4 = 4$$. Setting
   $$y = 0$$ gives $$x = 2$$, and setting $$x = 0$$ gives $$y = -4$$, so the line crosses the axes
   at $$(2, 0)$$ and $$(0, -4)$$.
2. Setting $$y = 0$$ gives the solution $$(2, 0)$$. By :ref{#equation-line} the line has direction
   $$(-b, a) = (2, 3)$$, so it is the set of points $$(2, 0) + t(2, 3)$$. Check with $$t = 1$$: the
   point $$(4, 3)$$ has $$12 - 6 = 6$$.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-lines-ch-meet}

Find where the line through $$(1, 1)$$ with direction $$(2, 1)$$ meets the line $$3x + y = 18$$.

:::solution

The points of the first line are $$(1 + 2t, 1 + t)$$. Such a point is on the second line exactly
when

```math
3(1 + 2t) + (1 + t) = 18
```

that is, $$4 + 7t = 18$$, so $$t = 2$$. The lines meet at $$(5, 3)$$, and they meet nowhere else,
since there was only one solution. This agrees with :ref{#line-intersection}: the second line has
direction $$(-1, 3)$$ by :ref{#equation-line}, and $$2 \cdot 3 - 1 \cdot (-1) = 7 \ne 0$$, so the
lines are not parallel (:ref{#parallel-determinant}).

:::

::::exercise{#ex-lines-ch-collide}

At time $$t$$ hours, one boat is at $$(0, 0) + t(4, 2)$$ and another is at $$(10, -2) + t(-1, 3)$$,
in kilometres.

1. Find where their paths cross.
2. Do the boats collide? If they do, say when and where.
3. How far apart are they after one hour?

:::solution

1. The paths meet where $$t(4, 2) = (10, -2) + s(-1, 3)$$ for some numbers $$t$$ and $$s$$, that is,
   $$4t = 10 - s$$ and $$2t = -2 + 3s$$. The first gives $$s = 10 - 4t$$, and then
   $$2t = -2 + 30 - 12t$$, so $$t = 2$$ and $$s = 2$$. The paths cross at $$(8, 4)$$.
2. The parameters agree: the first boat reaches $$(8, 4)$$ at $$t = 2$$, and so does the second. So
   they collide after $$2$$ hours at $$(8, 4)$$, unless one of them changes course. Had the two
   parameters been different, the paths would cross but the boats would pass through the crossing at
   different times.
3. After one hour they are at $$(4, 2)$$ and $$(9, 1)$$, which are $$\sqrt{25 + 1} = \sqrt{26}$$ km
   apart, about $$5.1$$ km.

:::

## Circles and right angles

::::exercise{#ex-lines-ch-circle}

Find where the line $$y = x + 1$$ meets the circle $$x^2 + y^2 = 25$$.

:::solution

The line goes through $$(0, 1)$$ with direction $$(1, 1)$$, so its points are $$(t, 1 + t)$$. Such a
point is on the circle exactly when

```math
t^2 + (1 + t)^2 = 25
```

which is $$2t^2 + 2t - 24 = 0$$, or $$t^2 + t - 12 = 0$$. This factorises as $$(t + 4)(t - 3) = 0$$,
so $$t = 3$$ or $$t = -4$$, and the meeting points are $$(3, 4)$$ and $$(-4, -3)$$. Two solutions
means two meeting points, as :ref{#line-circle} says.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-lines-ch-tangent}

Find the equation of the tangent to the circle $$x^2 + y^2 = 25$$ at the point $$(3, 4)$$, and check
that its distance from the centre is the radius.

:::solution

By :ref{#tangent-perpendicular}, the tangent at $$(3, 4)$$ is the line through $$(3, 4)$$
perpendicular to the radius, whose direction is $$(3, 4)$$. By :ref{#normal-direction}, the lines
perpendicular to the direction $$(3, 4)$$ have equations $$3x + 4y = c$$. Putting in $$(3, 4)$$
gives $$c = 9 + 16 = 25$$, so the tangent is $$3x + 4y = 25$$.

By :ref{#distance-to-line}, its distance from $$(0, 0)$$ is
$$\frac{|0 + 0 - 25|}{\sqrt{9 + 16}} = 5$$, which is the radius.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-lines-ch-house}

A straight road follows the line $$3x + 4y = 12$$, in units of $$100$$ m. A house stands at
$$X = (2, 9)$$.

1. How far is the house from the road?
2. Find the point of the road closest to the house.

:::solution

1. By :ref{#distance-to-line}, the distance is

   ```math
   \frac{|3 \cdot 2 + 4 \cdot 9 - 12|}{\sqrt{9 + 16}} = \frac{30}{5} = 6
   ```

   that is, $$600$$ m.

2. The closest point $$F$$ is where the perpendicular from $$X$$ meets the road
   (:ref{#closest-point}). The direction $$(3, 4)$$ is perpendicular to the road
   (:ref{#normal-direction}), and $$|(3, 4)| = 5$$. Since $$3 \cdot 2 + 4 \cdot 9 = 42$$ is more
   than $$12$$, the house is on the side the step $$(3, 4)$$ points to, so walk $$6$$ units the
   other way:

   ```math
   F = (2, 9) - \tfrac{6}{5}(3, 4) = \left(-\tfrac{8}{5}, \tfrac{21}{5}\right)
   ```

   Check: $$3\left(-\frac{8}{5}\right) + 4 \cdot \frac{21}{5} = \frac{-24 + 84}{5} = 12$$, so $$F$$
   is on the road.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-lines-ch-bisector}

Let $$A = (1, 2)$$ and $$B = (5, 4)$$.

1. Show that a point $$X$$ satisfies $$|X - A|^2 - |X - B|^2 = 2\,X \cdot (B - A) - |B|^2 + |A|^2$$.
2. Deduce that the points equally far from $$A$$ and $$B$$ make up the line $$2x + y = 9$$.
3. Check that this line passes through the midpoint of $$AB$$ and is perpendicular to $$AB$$.

:::solution

1. By :ref{#difference-length}, $$|X - A|^2 = |X|^2 + |A|^2 - 2\,X \cdot A$$, and the same with
   $$B$$. Subtracting, the $$|X|^2$$ terms cancel, and by :ref{#dot-rules}

   ```math
   |X - A|^2 - |X - B|^2 = 2\,X \cdot B - 2\,X \cdot A + |A|^2 - |B|^2 = 2\,X \cdot (B - A) + |A|^2 - |B|^2
   ```

2. Distances are never negative, so $$|X - A| = |X - B|$$ exactly when their squares are equal, that
   is, when $$2\,X \cdot (B - A) = |B|^2 - |A|^2$$. Here $$B - A = (4, 2)$$, $$|B|^2 = 41$$ and
   $$|A|^2 = 5$$, so with $$X = (x, y)$$ the condition is $$2(4x + 2y) = 36$$, which is
   $$2x + y = 9$$.
3. The midpoint is $$(3, 3)$$ (:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/midpoint-formula}), and
   $$6 + 3 = 9$$. The line $$2x + y = 9$$ is perpendicular to every line with direction $$(2, 1)$$
   (:ref{#normal-direction}), and $$AB$$ has direction $$(4, 2) = 2(2, 1)$$. So this line is the
   perpendicular bisector of $$AB$$, and the exercise proves
   :ref{#distance-and-angles/perpendicular-bisector-rule} for these two points by algebra.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-lines-ch-altitudes}

In a triangle, the line through a corner perpendicular to the opposite side is called an
**altitude**.

1. Let $$H$$ be a point with $$(H - A) \cdot (B - C) = 0$$ and $$(H - B) \cdot (C - A) = 0$$. Show
   that $$(H - C) \cdot (A - B) = 0$$.
2. Explain why this shows that the three altitudes of a triangle meet at one point.
3. Check with $$A = (0, 0)$$, $$B = (6, 0)$$, $$C = (2, 4)$$ and $$H = (2, 2)$$.

:::solution

1. Multiply out each dot product with :ref{#dot-rules}:

   ```math
   (H - A) \cdot (B - C) = H \cdot B - H \cdot C - A \cdot B + A \cdot C
   ```

   ```math
   (H - B) \cdot (C - A) = H \cdot C - H \cdot A - B \cdot C + B \cdot A
   ```

   Adding the two, the terms $$H \cdot C$$ and $$A \cdot B$$ cancel, leaving
   $$H \cdot B - H \cdot A + A \cdot C - B \cdot C$$, which is $$(H - C) \cdot (B - A)$$ multiplied
   out. Both given dot products are $$0$$, so $$(H - C) \cdot (B - A) = 0$$, and so
   $$(H - C) \cdot (A - B) = 0$$ too.

2. The altitudes from $$A$$ and from $$B$$ are perpendicular to the sides $$BC$$ and $$CA$$, which
   are not parallel, so the altitudes are not parallel either (by parts 2 and 4 of
   :ref{#perpendicular-rules-proved}) and meet at one point $$H$$ (:ref{#line-intersection}). That
   $$H$$ satisfies both conditions of part 1, so $$(H - C) \cdot (A - B) = 0$$: the step from $$C$$
   to $$H$$ is perpendicular to $$AB$$, which puts $$H$$ on the altitude from $$C$$ (if $$H = C$$,
   it is on that altitude anyway). This point is called the **orthocentre**.

3. All three dot products are $$0$$:

   - $$(H - A) \cdot (B - C) = (2, 2) \cdot (4, -4) = 0$$
   - $$(H - B) \cdot (C - A) = (-4, 2) \cdot (2, 4) = 0$$
   - $$(H - C) \cdot (A - B) = (0, -2) \cdot (-6, 0) = 0$$

:::

## Moves and space

::::exercise{#ex-lines-ch-zoom}

Find the image of the line $$x + y = 4$$ under the zoom by $$2$$ about $$(1, 1)$$, and check that
the image is parallel to the line.

:::solution

The zoom is $$Z(P) = (1, 1) + 2(P - (1, 1)) = 2P - (1, 1)$$
(:ref{#adding-and-scaling-points/zoom-formula}). By :ref{#moves-keep-lines} it sends the line onto a
line, so two points decide the image. The points $$(4, 0)$$ and $$(0, 4)$$ of the line go to
$$(7, -1)$$ and $$(-1, 7)$$, and the line through these is $$x + y = 6$$.

The old line has direction $$(1, -1)$$, and so does the new one, since
$$(-1, 7) - (7, -1) = (-8, 8) = -8(1, -1)$$. So the two lines are parallel
(:ref{#parallel-formula}). A zoom moves a line straight away from the centre without turning it.

:::

::::exercise{#ex-lines-ch-skew}

In space, let $$L$$ be the line $$(0, 0, 0) + t(1, 1, 0)$$ and $$K$$ the line
$$(0, 2, 1) + s(1, -1, 0)$$.

1. Show that $$L$$ and $$K$$ are skew.
2. Show that their directions are perpendicular, and explain why $$L$$ and $$K$$ are still not
   perpendicular lines.

:::solution

1. Every point of $$L$$ has third coordinate $$0$$ and every point of $$K$$ has third coordinate
   $$1$$, so they never meet. If $$(1, -1, 0) = c(1, 1, 0)$$, the first coordinates give $$c = 1$$
   and the second give $$c = -1$$, which can't both hold, so they aren't parallel
   (:ref{#space-parallel}). So they are skew (:ref{#skew-lines}).
2. $$(1, 1, 0) \cdot (1, -1, 0) = 1 - 1 + 0 = 0$$ (:ref{#space-dot}). But perpendicular lines must
   meet (:ref{#space-perpendicular}), and these never do. Seen from above, the two lines cross
   square on, one $$1$$ unit above the other.

:::
